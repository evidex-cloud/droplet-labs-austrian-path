# Common Austrian Mistakes & Self-Critique: Permabears, Hyperinflation Calls & Dogmatism

## @hook
A good theory in careless hands produces a string of bad predictions. After 2009, plenty of Austrian sympathizers announced that hyperinflation was two years away, that the crash was next month, that gold could only go up. None of it happened — and not one of those claims was something the theory actually required them to say. This is the course's **self-critique lesson**: the ten mistakes Austrians make most often, laid out without excuses and without self-flagellation, each one interrogated with the same question — **what did the theory say, what did it not say, and on which rung of the ladder did the error climb in?**

## @intuition
Start with a true story that still stings.

In November 2010, a couple of dozen economists, investors and commentators published an open letter to Fed chairman Ben Bernanke in the *Wall Street Journal*, warning that the second round of quantitative easing (QE2) risked “currency debasement and inflation.” The signatories were not all Austrians — the list included monetarists, supply-siders and hedge-fund managers — but the loudest version of the argument came from Austrian circles: “They have printed this much money; hyperinflation is a mathematical certainty.” One young Austrian economist went so far as to make a public bet that year-over-year U.S. CPI inflation would exceed 10% by early 2013.

What happened: from 2010 to 2019, U.S. CPI rose at roughly 1.5%–2% a year, one of the calmest stretches in decades. The economist lost $500 and — this is to his enormous credit — conceded publicly and wrote up where he had gone wrong.

Now the sharp question: **was Austrian theory wrong?** The answer surprises people: the theory was fine; what went wrong were the steps that got skipped on the way from theory to forecast. Mises had already written it down in *The Theory of Money and Credit* (1912): the purchasing power of money is set by the supply of money **and the demand to hold it**, and the point at which new money enters the economy determines which prices move first ([[money-demand|Stage 4.2]], [[cantillon-inflation|Stage 4.3]]). After 2009 the new money entered through one specific door — bank reserves — and the Fed started paying interest on those reserves, so the money sat there. Meanwhile households and firms were deleveraging, and the demand for cash exploded. Put those two legs back on the theory and its prediction is not “CPI takes off” but “asset prices rise first, consumer prices barely move” — which is exactly what happened. **The theory was right. Its users misused it.**

This lesson lays those misuses out systematically. They fall into three families:

- **Mistaking a direction for a date.** The theory tells you the system is fragile and the direction is down; you translate that into “crash next month.” That is the root of the permabear disease.
- **Using the theory as a universal solvent.** Every boom is credit-driven, every new technology is a bubble, every institution is a plot. The theory is used instead of analysis rather than to guide it.
- **Treating the school as a sect.** Quoting Mises as evidence, treating internal debates as settled, ignoring what mainstream economics has genuinely contributed, and saying “theft!” where an argument should go.

The school's critics love to use these errors to mock the whole tradition. Our reply is not denial. It is to **say them first, ourselves, and more precisely than the critics can** — and to state clearly what each mistake taught us. A school that can criticize itself candidly is more credible, and more useful, than one that only plays defense.

One housekeeping note: this lesson mentions gold, Bitcoin and index funds. All of it is history and framework only; **none of it is investment advice** ([[austrian-investing|Stage 10.5]] said the same thing).

**In this lesson we break it into six pieces:**

- **① The permabear: being early is being wrong, and the theory never gives a date**
- **② The hyperinflation call: which rung the 2010 letter fell off**
- **③ Gold dogma and “everything is malinvestment”: monetary theory ≠ price forecasting, cycle theory ≠ universal solvent**
- **④ Ignoring the mainstream and conspiracy framing: why public choice beats “plot” on accuracy and persuasion**
- **⑤ Moralizing, appeals to authority, settled debates, and underrating the state's ability to stall**
- **⑥ The habits that prevent all this: Hayekian humility, pattern predictions, and a ledger of your own calls**

## @mechanics
### ① The permabear: being early is being wrong, and the theory never gives a date

A “permabear” is someone whose conclusion never changes whatever the market does: the crash is always just ahead. The condition is unusually common among Austrians, and it is not hard to see why. **Austrian business cycle theory** (ABCT) really does say that an artificially low interest rate piles up **malinvestment**, and that a boom carries the seeds of its own bust ([[abct-one-picture|Stage 5.1]], [[boom-malinvestment|Stage 5.2]]). After 2008 central banks held rates at zero for the better part of a decade. The fragility the theory pointed to was real.

The error is translating “fragile” into a date. A numerical example makes the cost concrete:

- **Investor A** reads ABCT in early 2010, concludes that “zero rates guarantee an even bigger crash,” and moves $1,000,000 entirely into cash to wait. Cash earns roughly nothing for a decade. In 2020 A still has about $1,000,000 — and after roughly 15%–20% of cumulative price increases, that is about $800,000-and-change in purchasing power.
- **Investor B** knows no theory at all and puts $1,000,000 into a U.S. stock index fund. From the start of 2010 to the end of 2019 the index roughly tripled (a bit more than tripled with dividends). In March 2020 a crash of about 35% really did arrive — the crash A had been waiting for — and at the bottom B still had about $2,000,000. By year-end B was back above $3,000,000.
- **Investor C** is bolder and shorts the index from 2010 on, “because the theory says the crash must come.” C was wiped out by ten years of gains and borrowing costs long before the crash showed up.

That is the old Wall Street line: **being early is being wrong.** The theory gave A and C a correct direction and a genuine fragility. It gave them no date. They supplied the date themselves.

Why can the theory not supply a date? Because ABCT is about **structure**: rates pushed down → long production chains look profitable → resources drawn toward stages far from consumption → the errors surface when real saving proves insufficient to carry the projects. It does not say *when* they surface, because that depends on whether the central bank doubles down, whether the treasury backstops, how the public's demand for money shifts, how entrepreneurs adjust — all choices by acting people, with no constants to plug in. Mises was consistent on this as a matter of method: praxeology yields **qualitative laws** (“doing this leads to that”), never quantitative forecasts (“a 30% decline in month 18”). [[apriori-empirical|Stage 2.2]] made the point that there are no constants in human action; its direct corollary is that **nobody is entitled to use Austrian theory to name a date.**

The honest lesson of the permabear: **direction + fragility ≠ timing + magnitude.** Use the theory as a compass and it serves you well. Use it as a timetable and you are lying to yourself.

### ② The hyperinflation call: which rung the 2010 letter fell off

Back to the open letter. Its reasoning chain ran roughly: the Fed has tripled or quadrupled the monetary base → quantity theory → prices must rise sharply → keep going and it is hyperinflation. Walk that chain one rung at a time.

Rung 1: **the monetary base really did explode.** From autumn 2008 to 2014 the Fed's balance sheet grew from under $1 trillion to about $4.5 trillion; the base roughly quadrupled. No error here.

Rung 2: **where the new money entered.** QE means the Fed buys bonds from banks and credits the banks' reserve accounts at the Fed. And from October 2008 the Fed began paying interest on those reserves (IOR). For a bank, parking money at the central bank now earned a risk-free return, while lending it out meant taking credit risk in an economy that had just been through a financial crisis. So excess reserves went from essentially zero before the crisis to a peak above roughly $2.5 trillion. **The money entered the system but never entered the payments chain of the real economy.** This is the central lesson of Cantillon effects ([[cantillon-inflation|Stage 4.3]]): who gets the money first, and through which door, determines which prices move first. The signatories forgot the one lesson the school is best known for.

Rung 3: **the money multiplier collapsed.** The textbook says base money × multiplier = broad money, with a multiplier of about 8–9. When banks pile new reserves at the Fed instead, the multiplier falls — from about 9 before the crisis to about 3 (below 1 if you measure with M1). M2 grew about 6% a year over the decade, roughly its pre-crisis pace. The broad-money explosion the letter assumed never took place.

Rung 4: **the demand for money surged.** This is the leg Mises put on the table in 1912: purchasing power depends not only on how much money exists but on how much of it people want to hold as cash balances ([[money-demand|Stage 4.2]]). In 2009–2013 households were paying down mortgages, firms were hoarding cash, banks were rebuilding capital — everyone wanted to hold more money and spend less. A rising demand for money offsets a rising supply. That is the other face of money's non-neutrality.

Rung 5: **deleveraging.** The shadow banking system contracted and securitization markets froze; the disappearance of that private credit offset the central bank's expansion in the aggregates.

Line those five rungs up and what does the theory actually predict? **Wherever the new money stops, those prices rise first.** The new money stopped inside the financial system, so the first prices to rise were bond prices, equity prices and house prices — asset prices climbed through the 2010s while CPI averaged about 1.5%–2%. That is not a failure of the theory. It is a rather precise confirmation of it, just not in the form of a CPI spike.

Then how does the 2021–23 inflation fit? It confirms the same logic from the other side. The money of 2020–2021 was not credited to bank reserve accounts; it went through fiscal transfers **straight into household and business accounts** — M2 grew roughly 25% in 2020 alone, the fastest of the postwar era — while supply was constrained and the panic-driven demand for cash receded. This time the new money entered at the front of the payments chain, and CPI inflation touched roughly 9% in mid-2022. [[case-2008-qe|Stage 13.4]] walks the whole case: **the same theory made two different predictions and got both right** — provided you keep the money-demand leg and the injection-point leg attached.

Three honest lessons:
- **Non-neutrality of money is the school's core insight, and the 2010s proved it** — in asset prices rather than in CPI. Defining “inflation” as CPI alone throws away the school's strongest card.
- Austrians failed to use a tool they had owned since 1912 (the demand for money) and borrowed a crude quantity theory to make forecasts instead. **That is the lesson of being assimilated by your opponent's framework.**
- The economist who lost the bet and said so in public did exactly what this lesson is trying to teach: write the forecast down, admit the miss, and then ask which rung it failed on.

### ③ Gold dogma and “everything is malinvestment”: monetary theory ≠ price forecasting, cycle theory ≠ universal solvent

**Gold and Bitcoin dogma.** Austrian monetary theory does hold that a money selected by the market (historically, mostly gold and silver) restrains governments better than fiat money. That is an **institutional argument** — a claim about which monetary arrangements are harder to abuse ([[central-banks-fiat|Stage 4.5]], [[free-banking|Stage 9.4]]). Many people quietly swap it for a very different sentence: “therefore the price of gold must rise.” Nothing in the theory supports that step. The dollar price of gold is the relative price of one asset, driven by the demand for money, real interest rates, the expected returns on competing assets, mine supply and central-bank purchases and sales — **no Austrian theorem says “hard money always appreciates.”**

An example. Gold touched roughly $1,900 an ounce in September 2011, fell to about $1,050 by late 2015, and only recovered to about $1,500 by the end of 2019. **Measured from the 2011 peak, gold went roughly sideways for most of a decade** and finished slightly down; U.S. equities tripled over the same span. Someone who went all-in on gold in 2011 because “fiat must collapse” may have been right in the abstract (fiat did keep losing purchasing power) and still missed an entire cycle in practice. Sound money is an argument about **institutions**, not a portfolio. The same applies to Bitcoin: a fixed supply is a genuinely interesting monetary experiment ([[bitcoin-regression|Stage 17.1]] and [[hard-money|Stage 17.2]] give it a fair hearing, strengths and critiques), but in the chain “fixed supply → must appreciate → replaces the dollar by 2030,” only the first link is a fact; the other two are wishes. **Once more: nothing here is investment advice.**

**“Everything is malinvestment.”** The second dogma is subtler. ABCT is a fine knife, but some people use it to cut everything: every boom is credit-driven, every new technology is a bubble, every richly valued company is malinvestment. The theory becomes a universal solvent — it dissolves everything, so it distinguishes nothing.

[[boom-malinvestment|Stage 5.2]] gave the method for telling booms apart; compressed to three questions:

- **Where does the money come from?** Is the expansion carried by real saving (firms expanding out of profits and equity, household saving rates rising), or by credit expansion (rates far below the natural rate, debt-to-income rising fast, junk-bond spreads collapsing)?
- **Is the market rate below the natural rate?** If the policy rate sits for years below what the economy's saving–investment balance would require, the precondition for malinvestment is present; if not, the boom may be productivity-driven.
- **Which sectors are expanding?** A credit-driven boom has a characteristic shape: long-duration projects far from consumption and the most interest-sensitive sectors (housing, capital goods, the longest-dated investments) swell out of proportion. A productivity-driven boom shows up as falling unit costs, broad-based output growth, and — under stable money — gently falling prices.

Hayek made this distinction in the 1920s: output growth from technical progress should, with stable money, show up as **falling prices**; it was central banks expanding credit to “stabilize the price level” that turned a healthy boom into an unhealthy one. When you assess the AI capital-spending frenzy ([[ai-bubble-abct|Stage 18.5]]), those three questions are the checklist to run — rather than shouting “bubble” the moment you see the phrase “a trillion dollars of capex.” **Only someone who can say “this one may not be malinvestment” has earned the right to say “that one was.”**

### ④ Ignoring the mainstream and conspiracy framing: why public choice beats “plot” on accuracy and persuasion

**Blindness to mainstream contributions.** Some Austrians treat “the mainstream” as a single hostile bloc, and so miss a great deal of work that is compatible with Austrian economics or that shores up its weak spots:

- **Public choice.** Buchanan and Tullock's *The Calculus of Consent* (1962) turned “government officials are acting people pursuing their own ends too” into a working research program. Austrians explain why **interventionism** cannot deliver what it promises ([[intervention-logic|Stage 8.1]]); public choice explains why interventions get adopted anyway — two halves of the same problem ([[economics-of-state|Stage 8.4]]).
- **Coase.** “The Nature of the Firm” (1937) and “The Problem of Social Cost” (1960): transaction costs and the allocation of property rights. Austrians have real criticisms of Coase ([[externalities-coase|Stage 9.2]]), but criticism presupposes having read him.
- **Alchian and Demsetz.** Alchian's “Uncertainty, Evolution, and Economic Theory” (1950) treats the market as a selection process — almost Hayek's discovery procedure in different words. Demsetz's theory of property rights (1967) and his attack on the “nirvana fallacy” (comparing an ideal government with real markets) are natural allies.
- **Industrial organization.** The empirical literature on entry barriers, contestable markets and dynamic competition supports, rather than threatens, the Austrian case for not fearing “big” ([[monopoly-question|Stage 6.4]]).
- **Mechanism design and market design.** Hurwicz, Maskin and Myerson (Nobel 2007) and Roth (Nobel 2012) study which rules get dispersed information reported honestly — in effect, Hayek's **knowledge problem** turned into an engineering question. Austrians need not accept every conclusion, but without reading it you will talk about platforms and algorithms ([[platforms-winner|Stage 15.2]], [[agent-economies|Stage 18.6]]) in the vocabulary of the last century.

An Austrian who reads only Mises and Rothbard attacks a mainstream that is thirty years out of date — a strawman. It is the same ailment [[empirical-method|Stage 13.1]] called “thin empirical work”: **the school does not lack theory; it lacks the patience to grind that theory against real data and real literatures.**

**Conspiracy framing.** “The Fed is a plot to enrich bankers.” “Inflation is a deliberate theft of your savings.” Lines like these sell well in Austrian communities. The problem is not that they are impolite. It is that they are **both inaccurate and unpersuasive**:

- Inaccurate: Fed policy is shaped by hundreds of staff economists, a committee that votes in public, congressional pressure, academic consensus and the memory of the last crisis. Its errors come chiefly from **incentives and knowledge** — officials are expected to “do something” during their term, are judged on CPI and employment, and cannot gather dispersed knowledge ([[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]]) — not from a plan drawn up in a back room. The public-choice explanation (“each person did what was reasonable under the incentives the institution gave them, and the sum was harmful”) predicts central-bank behavior better than the conspiracy explanation does: it predicts a **systematic** tilt toward easing, delay and bailouts, without requiring anyone to be a villain.
- Unpersuasive: a trained listener switches off at the word “conspiracy.” But “the incentive structure produces a systematic bias” is a sentence that people inside the central bank cannot themselves deny. **You want to win the audience, not the applause.**

### ⑤ Moralizing, appeals to authority, settled debates, and underrating the state's ability to stall

This section groups the remaining four mistakes. What they share is **substituting a posture for an analysis.**

**Moralizing instead of analyzing.** “Taxation is theft, therefore harmful.” “Inflation is robbery, therefore it must collapse.” Ethical argument has its place — Rothbard and Hoppe devoted a great deal of work to it — but **it is not economic analysis.** Economics asks “what will this policy cause,” not “is it moral”; blend the two and your economic conclusions look like an ethical stance in costume. Mises himself insisted in *Human Action* on value-freedom (*Wertfreiheit*): an economist may hold any ethics he likes, but leaves them at the door when arguing. The five-step method of [[five-step-analysis|Stage 14.1]] begins with “what is the policy's stated goal” precisely to force analysis before evaluation.

**Quoting Mises as proof.** “As Mises said …” is a common opening move inside the school. But a quotation only tells you Mises's opinion, not whether he was right. The force of a praxeological argument lies in **the chain of reasoning itself** — you should be able to rebuild it without naming anyone. If you can quote but not rebuild, what you hold is not a theory but a creed.

**Treating internal debates as settled.** [[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]] showed that Austrians do not agree on, at least: whether fractional-reserve banking is fraud or a legitimate contract (Rothbard vs Selgin–White); whether Hayek's knowledge argument or Mises's calculation argument is the more fundamental one (Salerno vs Kirzner and others); whether Bitcoin satisfies the regression theorem; how far empirical work should go. Presenting one side as “the Austrian position” leaves you unable to hold your ground even among fellow Austrians, let alone outside. **A living school has arguments; only a sect has none.**

**Underrating the state's capacity to stall.** This is the permabear's twin and deserves its own paragraph. The theory says credit expansion must “eventually” be liquidated; many readers hear “eventually” as “soon.” Look at Japan: after the 1990 bubble burst, the Bank of Japan cut rates to zero in 1999, began quantitative easing in 2001 and escalated it in 2013; government debt rose to roughly 250% of GDP. Austrian sympathizers spent thirty years predicting a collapse of Japanese government bonds — a trade so reliably ruinous that the market nicknamed it “the widowmaker.” The United States and Europe in the 2010s taught the same thing: **a state with a reserve currency, the ability to hold rates at zero and a treasury willing to backstop can postpone liquidation for a very long time.** The costs are zombie firms, low productivity growth and distorted asset distributions ([[debt-zombies|Stage 10.4]], [[case-japan|Stage 13.3]]) — real costs, but a chronic illness, not a cardiac arrest.

Mises's “**crack-up boom**” is often cited as proof that the reckoning must arrive. Read the passage: the crack-up boom happens when the public expects inflation to go on without end and flees money for real goods — it is a **destination reached only if a condition is met**, not a schedule. Thirty years of Japan show that as long as the public still wants to hold the currency (money demand has not collapsed), the condition is not met. Someone who underrates the state's ability to stall has, once again, forgotten the money-demand leg.

### ⑥ The habits that prevent all this: Hayekian humility, pattern predictions, and a ledger of your own calls

The ten mistakes — permabear, hyperinflation reflex, gold dogma, universal solvent, ignoring the mainstream, conspiracy framing, moralizing, appeal to authority, settled debates, underrating the state — share one structure: **they all happen on the rungs between theory and application.** Theory is a priori and certain; application needs auxiliary assumptions (institutional detail, injection point, money demand, political constraints), and auxiliary assumptions can be wrong. The error almost never occurs on the first rung and almost always on the second or the fourth.

The matching habits, each traceable to an earlier lesson in this course:

- **Hayekian humility.** Hayek's 1974 Nobel lecture was titled “The Pretence of Knowledge”: a science of complex phenomena can deliver **pattern predictions** — which kinds of structure will appear, which things cannot happen together — but not specific values. This is not an Austrian weakness; it is the most honest commitment anyone has made to complex systems. Tape it above your screen whenever you write a forecast.
- **Applied claims that are “falsifiable in spirit.”** The theory is a priori; your applied claims are not. Every time you make a call, write two sentences: “If I am right, I expect to see ___.” “If ___ happens, I should admit I was wrong.” Had the 2010 letter added the second sentence (“if CPI is still below 3% in two years, we were mistaken”), it would have become an honorable exercise in learning rather than a punchline.
- **No dates.** You may say “this structure is unsustainable” and “the longer it goes, the worse the unwinding.” You may not say “next March.” Whoever names a date has already left the theory.
- **Steelman first, then reply.** [[writing-argument|Stage 14.2]] on writing: state the opponent's argument until the opponent would nod, then begin your reply. The habit has a side effect — it forces you to read the mainstream literature, which cures the “ignoring the mainstream” disease automatically.
- **Separate theory from application, and say so out loud.** Use two registers in your writing: “necessarily” when speaking of theory, “under these assumptions I expect” when speaking of application. Readers will trust you more for it, not less.
- **Keep a ledger of your own calls.** Write down every public or private judgment: date, claim, reasoning, the evidence you expect, the condition under which you would concede. Reread it a year later. It is the most effective medicine against the permabear disease, because you will discover that the feeling of having “always been right” rarely survives contact with a written record. (This lesson's demo is a miniature of that ledger: twelve Austrian-sounding sentences — you judge each first, then see which rung it fails on.)

One closing line, which [[open-problems|Stage ∞.1]] picks up: **a school's maturity is not measured by never having been wrong, but by whether it can say exactly which rung it fell off and from which rung it climbs back.**

## @analogy
Think of Austrian theory as an **excellent structural engineer**. He walks through a building and can tell you precisely: these beams are under-sized, that wall was poured with weak concrete, the foundation sits on fill. So the building is **fragile** — in an earthquake it will fail before the one next door. Those judgments are reliable; they come from mechanics, not from guessing.

The permabear is the man standing outside every evening shouting “it falls tonight.” He has translated the engineer's “fragile” into “tonight.” The building stands for ten years and he shouts every night; the neighbors go from nervous to amused. When, in year eleven, the building really does come down in a quake, nobody believes him any more — he has been wrong three thousand evenings running.

The hyperinflation forecaster is a student of the same engineer. He learned that “watered-down concrete makes buildings fragile,” and declared “this building has watered-down concrete, so it must fall.” But he never checked whether the weak concrete was in a load-bearing wall or a decorative one (the injection point), nor whether the owner had bolted a row of steel columns alongside (rising money demand). His mechanics were sound; he skipped the site inspection.

The gold dogmatist is the man who says “steel is stronger than wood, so the price of steel must rise” — a conclusion in materials science swapped for a futures call. The “everything is malinvestment” man calls every new building a tofu-dreg project and refuses to distinguish the ones built from savings from the ones built on loan-shark credit.

What does the honest engineer say? “This structure has the following three weaknesses; in a quake of such-and-such magnitude, I expect failure to begin here; if the building comes through such a quake intact, my judgment was wrong and I will come back and re-survey.” He names no date, he states his conditions, and he signs his report. That is the kind of Austrian economist [[open-problems|Stage ∞.1]] keeps asking you to become: **hard mechanics, careful inspection, no dates, and a signed ledger.**

## @misconceptions
- **“The hyperinflation calls failed, so Austrian monetary theory is wrong.”** — The forecasts failed; the theory did not. Mises built money demand and the injection point into the theory in 1912. In the 2010s the new money sat in interest-bearing reserve accounts, the multiplier collapsed and money demand surged, so the theory's real prediction was “asset prices first, CPI flat” — which is what happened. The error was in omitted auxiliary assumptions, not in the theory.
- **“ABCT says the boom must end in a bust, so the right move now is to short.”** — The theory gives direction and fragility, never timing or magnitude. Someone who shorted from 2010 was wiped out by a decade of gains long before the 2020 crash arrived. Being early is being wrong; there are no constants in human action, and anyone naming a date has already left the theory. Nothing here is investment advice.
- **“Hard money is better, therefore the price of gold or Bitcoin must rise.”** — Sound money is an institutional argument about which monetary arrangements are harder to abuse; it is not a price forecast. Gold went roughly sideways from its 2011 peak to 2019 while equities tripled. Monetary theory implies nothing about any asset's future price — swapping an institutional argument for a portfolio is a bait-and-switch.
- **“The Fed's mistakes come down to a bankers' conspiracy.”** — The public-choice explanation is both more accurate and more persuasive: officials, under the incentives they face (pressure to ‘do something’ in office, evaluation by CPI and employment, no access to dispersed knowledge), do what is reasonable for them, and the sum is a systematic tilt toward easing and delay. That account needs no villains and still predicts central-bank behavior; the word ‘conspiracy’ merely makes trained listeners stop listening.
- **“Mises already proved it, so there is nothing left to argue.”** — A quotation shows what Mises thought, not that he was right. The power of praxeology lies in the chain of reasoning, which you should be able to rebuild without a name attached. And Austrians still genuinely disagree about fractional reserves, knowledge versus calculation, Bitcoin and the regression theorem ([[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]]) — presenting one side as ‘the Austrian view’ turns a school into a sect.

   - [x] Rung 2: it omitted the auxiliary assumptions — the injection point (interest-bearing reserves), the collapsed multiplier and rising money demand

   - [ ] Rung 3: pattern predictions are impossible in principle

   - [ ] Because he should have shorted rather than held cash
   - [ ] Because ABCT has been refuted by the mainstream
   - [x] Because the theory gives direction and fragility, not dates; the opportunity cost of a ten-year wait dwarfed the loss he avoided — being early is being wrong
   > The index roughly tripled over the decade and was still more than double even after a roughly 35% drop; the cash-holder earned nothing for ten years and lost purchasing power. **Direction + fragility ≠ timing + magnitude.** There are no constants in human action; whoever names a date has left the theory.

3. Which question does most to separate a credit-driven boom from a productivity-driven one?
   - [ ] Whether the industry's market capitalization is high
   - [ ] Whether the media is talking about a bubble
   - [x] Whether the expansion is financed by real saving or by credit at a rate below the natural rate, and whether the swelling is concentrated in the most interest-sensitive, long-duration sectors
   - [ ] Whether the technology is new
   > The three questions of [[boom-malinvestment|Stage 5.2]] and this lesson: where does the money come from, is the market rate below the natural rate, which sectors are swelling? High valuations, novelty and media chatter are not ABCT criteria — calling every boom malinvestment is using the theory as a universal solvent.

4. Why does this lesson prefer public choice over conspiracy framing to explain the Fed's systematic bias?
   - [ ] Because public choice is an internal Austrian theory
   - [x] Because it requires no villains yet predicts a systematic tilt toward easing, delay and bailouts — more accurate, and more persuasive to a trained audience
   - [ ] Because the Fed never makes mistakes
   - [ ] Because conspiracy claims carry legal risk
   > Buchanan and Tullock's **public choice** treats officials as people acting under given incentives: pressure to ‘do something’ in office, evaluation by CPI and employment, no access to dispersed knowledge — from which a systematic bias follows. That account is testable and predictive; ‘conspiracy’ is neither accurate nor persuasive.

5. What core habit does Hayek's 1974 Nobel lecture, “The Pretence of Knowledge,” leave anyone applying Austrian theory?

   - [ ] Avoid making any predictions at all
   - [ ] Quote Mises verbatim
