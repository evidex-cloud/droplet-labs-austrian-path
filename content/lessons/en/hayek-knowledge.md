# Hayek: The Use of Knowledge in Society

## @hook
Mises said “no prices, no calculation.” Hayek then asked a deeper question: **even if you could calculate, where would the numbers in the ledger come from?** His answer: the knowledge a society runs on never exists as “data” gathered in any one mind or machine. It is dispersed among millions of people, it concerns “the particular circumstances of time and place,” and much of it cannot be put into words even by those who hold it (tacit knowledge). The price system is a “telecommunications system”: it carries the news that a tin mine has collapsed around the world without anyone needing to know that a tin mine collapsed. This is the **knowledge problem** — and it explains why “better information technology” does not bring central planning back to life.

## @intuition
In 1945 Hayek published “The Use of Knowledge in Society” in the *American Economic Review*, and he began by changing the question. The textbook asks: “given all resources, technologies, and preferences, what is the optimal allocation?” Hayek pointed out that this phrasing **quietly assumes the answer to the problem** — it assumes that “all resources, technologies, and preferences” are “given.” Given to whom? To one mind. But no single mind has ever possessed these things.

The problem a society actually faces is: **how to make use of knowledge that nobody possesses in its entirety.**

Look at what that knowledge is like. It is not “the density of steel is 7.85” — scientific knowledge of that kind really can be centralized and written into encyclopedias. Hayek meant something else: the warehouse manager knows which machine is about to fail; the shipowner knows which route has empty cargo space on the outbound leg; the real-estate agent knows which block will have three houses for sale next month; the foreman knows which worker is off his game today. This knowledge has three features: it is **dispersed** (each person holds a small piece), **local** (it is about here and now), and **perishable** (tomorrow it is different). Hayek called it “knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place.” Michael Polanyi later added a fourth feature: much of it is **tacit** — “we know more than we can tell.” A person who can ride a bicycle cannot write down the algorithm.

Now Hayek's most famous example. **Suppose that somewhere in the world tin has become scarcer** — perhaps a mine has collapsed, perhaps a new use has suddenly appeared that needs a great deal of tin. Hayek: “It does not matter for our purpose — and it is significant that it does not matter — which of these two causes has made tin more scarce.”

The price of tin rises — say from 20,000 to 28,000 per ton. What happens next: solder manufacturers see their costs rise and switch some products to lower-tin formulations; canning companies run the numbers and find aluminum cans now beat tinplate, and switch; a small pewter workshop decides it is no longer worth it and cuts output; a mine owner in South America finds that low-grade ore that was uneconomic yesterday now pays, and expands. **Thousands of people adjust, tin flows to where it is needed most urgently, and not one of them knows “why tin got expensive.”** They only need to know two things: the price of tin, and their own alternatives.

That, Hayek said, is the marvel of the price system: it is a **telecommunications system** that compresses the message “tin has become scarcer” into a single number, sends it to everyone who needs to know, sends it *only* to those who need to know, and attaches the right instruction about *how much* to adjust. If the price system had been invented by a person, it would be hailed as one of humanity's greatest inventions. But it was not invented — it grew ([[spontaneous-order|Stage 7.4]]).

This lesson also has to deal with a family quarrel. Are Mises's 1920 argument ([[mises-1920|Stage 7.1]]) and Hayek's 1937/1945 argument one argument or two? Austrians disagree, and we will lay both sides out fairly. But first hold on to the conclusion they share: **“data” is not “knowledge.”** A supercomputer can swallow every piece of data that has already been written down; it cannot swallow the warehouse manager's hunch about that machine, still less knowledge that will only come into existence tomorrow. That point returns in [[data-new-oil|Stage 15.4]] (“Is data the new oil?”) and [[ai-central-planning|Stage 18.1]] (“Can AI central-plan?”).

## @mechanics
### ① 1937: replacing the question economics asks

In November 1936 Hayek delivered the presidential address to the London Economic Club; it appeared the following year in *Economica* as “Economics and Knowledge.” It is usually treated as the turning point in Hayek's thought — away from the technical capital-and-cycle theory of [[abct-one-picture|Stage 5.1]] and toward the theme of knowledge.

The essay starts from the concept of **equilibrium**. Economics at the time defined equilibrium as a state in which everyone's plans are mutually compatible. Hayek asked: what would it take for that definition to hold? Each person's plan would have to rest on **correct expectations** about the same set of facts. But each person sees only a small slice of the facts — so where does “mutual compatibility” come from?

His answer: the truly empirical problem of economics is not “what does equilibrium look like?” but **“how do people acquire knowledge and revise their expectations so that their plans tend toward compatibility?”** In other words, equilibrium theory treats the hardest part — the acquisition and transmission of knowledge — as already accomplished. That is a precise diagnosis of the “math and equilibrium” fight of [[vs-math-models|Stage 2.4]]: the mathematics is not wrong in itself; the word “given” in the model hides the entire problem.

“The Use of Knowledge in Society” (1945) pushed this insight into policy. The economic problem of society, Hayek wrote, “is thus not merely a problem of how to allocate ‘given’ resources — if ‘given’ is taken to mean given to a single mind … It is rather a problem of **how to secure the best use of resources known to any of the members of society, for ends whose relative importance only these individuals know.**”

Note the logical structure. It parallels the division of labor of [[exchange-division|Stage 1.5]]: the division of labor says nobody can produce everything; Hayek says **nobody can know everything** — society requires a “division of knowledge,” and the price system is the mechanism that coordinates it.

### ② Three kinds of knowledge: scientific, time-and-place, tacit

Hayek explicitly distinguished two kinds of knowledge; Polanyi supplied a third.

**Scientific knowledge**: general rules that can be written down. The melting point of steel, the formula of a fertilizer, the optimal rolling temperature. This knowledge can be centralized, held by experts, stored in databases. The planner's confidence is usually built on this kind — “let the best experts decide.”

**Knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place**: which machine is about to fail; which ship has empty space; this village's rainfall this season; this customer's plan to order 20% more next week. Hayek stressed that this knowledge “**by its nature cannot enter into statistics**” — because statistics require classifying things, and this knowledge is precisely about the differences of “this one, this moment.” Its value also lies in **timeliness**: by the time it is aggregated, reported, and entered, it is stale.

**Tacit knowledge**: Michael Polanyi's concept in *Personal Knowledge* (1958) and *The Tacit Dimension* (1966) — “we know more than we can tell.” An old hand glances at a weld and knows something is wrong but cannot say what; an entrepreneur “feels” a market is about to move but cannot write down the reasons. This knowledge **cannot be fully articulated even by its holder**, and therefore cannot be reported to any planner.

The key inference: **decisions should be made by the people who hold the time-and-place knowledge** — that is, decentralization. But decentralization raises a new problem: the person on the spot knows only his own patch. How does he know whether his decision will clash with everyone else's? He needs a way to learn “what is happening out there” with as little information as possible. That is the price.

### ③ The tin story: a telecommunications system nobody needs to understand

Let us work the tin story through with numbers and see what a price actually transmits.

Suppose the tin market supplies 300,000 tons a year at 20,000 per ton. Rank the users by “how much tin matters to me” (the utility staircase of [[diamonds-water|Stage 1.1]], but for firms):

- Electronic solder makers: no tin, no product; willing to pay up to 40,000;
- Tinplate canners: aluminum is a substitute; above 26,000 they switch;
- Tin-alloy bearing makers: alternative alloys exist; above 23,000 they switch;
- Pewter and craft workshops: above 21,000 they cut output.

Now a major mine collapses and supply falls to 240,000 tons. The price starts to rise. At 21,000 the craft workshops drop out; at 23,000 the bearing makers switch alloys; at around 28,000 some canners move to aluminum — the market clears again at the new price. Meanwhile the higher price makes low-grade ore that did not pay at 20,000 profitable, and some mine owners expand; others begin stockpiling, shifting part of the supply into the future (the entrepreneurial judgment of [[entrepreneur-alertness|Stage 6.1]]).

Now ask: **what does each of these thousands of decision-makers need to know?**

- The solder maker: tin is 28,000 and I still have to buy.
- The canner: tin 28,000, aluminum such-and-such — switch.
- The mine owner: tin 28,000 — my deposit now pays.

**Nobody needs to know that the mine collapsed.** Further: if the cause had been a new technology demanding large quantities of tin rather than a collapse, their responses would be identical — because the same number reached them. This, Hayek said, is what is *economical* about the price system: it compresses a fact known to a handful of people into one symbol and **transmits only “how much to adjust,” never “why.”** He called it “a kind of machinery for registering change, or a system of telecommunications.”

Compare the central planner's position. To make the same adjustment he needs: to learn that the mine collapsed (perhaps weeks later); to know every tin-using plant's alternatives and switching costs (time-and-place knowledge, mostly inside plant managers' heads); to know every mine's willingness to produce at each price; then to compute a new allocation and issue it — by which time conditions have changed again. **He is not short of computing power. He is short of inputs.**

This is also the other face of “competition as a discovery procedure” from [[competition-process|Stage 6.2]]: prices do not merely transmit known knowledge; they **force out** unknown knowledge. The canner did not know how high a tin price it could tolerate until tin actually reached that price.

### ④ Mises's “calculation” and Hayek's “knowledge”: one argument or two?

Austrians genuinely disagree here, and the disagreement deserves a fair hearing.

**The “two arguments” camp** — Joseph Salerno (his 1990 postscript to Mises's 1920 essay, and “Mises and Hayek Dehomogenized,” 1993), Jörg Guido Hülsmann, and others — holds that Mises and Hayek were saying different things:

- Mises's problem is **calculation**: even with every piece of knowledge on the table, without prices for producer goods you cannot compare heterogeneous capital goods — because prices come from **property and exchange**, not from “knowing.” Mises himself, in *Human Action*, insisted the calculation problem exists even in a “static” world.
- Hayek's problem is **knowledge**: even with prices, knowledge is dispersed and constantly changing, and the center cannot collect it.
- This camp argues that blending the two weakens Mises: Hayek's version sounds like an “information transmission” problem, which invites the reply that better communications technology could fix it; Mises's version is **structural** — no private property, no prices — and has nothing to do with transmission speed.

**The “one argument” camp** — Israel Kirzner (“The Economic Calculation Debate: Lessons for Austrians,” 1988), Don Lavoie (*Rivalry and Central Planning*, 1985), Peter Boettke, and Leland Yeager (in his 1994 reply to Salerno) — holds that the two are faces of a single argument:

- Producer-goods prices matter precisely because they **carry** dispersed knowledge; if knowledge were not dispersed, the planner could just solve the equations. Mises's “no prices, no calculation” presupposes that “prices contain something obtainable nowhere else” — which is exactly Hayek's knowledge.
- Conversely, Hayek's dispersed knowledge can be put to use only because **someone is responsible for his own decisions within his own property** — which brings us back to Mises's property.
- Lavoie in particular stressed that Mises's 1920 argument already had “dynamic,” “rivalrous,” and “entrepreneurial” dimensions; later textbooks flattened it into “static calculation.”

**How to weigh this?** The two camps agree on far more than they disagree about: both reject “the computer will solve it,” and both hold that property, prices, and knowledge are each indispensable. The disagreement is over **which link is most fundamental**. A practical reading: Mises's argument tells you “why, without prices, there is no ruler”; Hayek's tells you “why the markings on the ruler can only be engraved by a market.” This course uses both, and [[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]] revisits this family of internal Austrian disputes systematically.

Candidly, the dispute also exposes a soft spot: **a century on, the school has not settled whether its most central argument is one argument or two.** That is not shameful — it shows the argument is still alive — but readers should know it exists.

### ⑤ Why better information technology does not dissolve it

Every so often someone says: “Hayek's argument held in 1945; now we have the internet, big data, and AI, and knowledge can be collected in real time.” The objection deserves respect, because it sounds plausible. The Austrian reply has four layers:

**Layer one: tacit knowledge cannot be written down.** Not “nobody bothered,” but **cannot**. The welder knows the weld is bad at a glance but cannot state a rule; if he could, it would be scientific knowledge. However good the technology, it collects only what has already been articulated.

**Layer two: the value of time-and-place knowledge lies in using it, not storing it.** The warehouse manager's knowledge that a machine is about to fail is useful because **he reroutes the order now**. If he must report and await approval, the knowledge's value has already evaporated. Technology can shorten the reporting lag, but as long as decision rights sit away from the spot there is always a lag — and if decision rights sit on the spot, that is decentralized decision-making: a market.

**Layer three: knowledge is not collected; it is produced.** [[competition-process|Stage 6.2]] showed that competition is a discovery procedure: how high a tin price the canner can tolerate is something **the canner itself does not know** until the price actually rises. Such knowledge does not sit anywhere waiting to be gathered — it is **generated** when people face real prices and decide with their own money. Abolish the market and that knowledge stops being produced. This is the same logic as Mises's “prices are byproducts of exchange.”

**Layer four: the incentive to tell the truth.** Getting everyone to report preferences and capacities honestly requires that honesty pay. In a market your bid *is* your report, and lying costs you. In a planned system, overstating capacity needs and understating output capacity is rational (Soviet managers reportedly faced a “ratchet effect” — beat the target this year and next year's target rises, so nobody beat it). Information technology does not alter that incentive structure — one of the themes of [[bigdata-planning|Stage 7.5]].

It helps to say what Hayek's argument is **not**. He did not claim “more information is better, and the market is the system with the most information.” He said the opposite: the beauty of the price system is that **it lets each person know very little**. A system that lays all information in front of everyone is not Hayekian — it is overload. [[algorithms-order|Stage 16.2]] asks an interesting question: do recommendation algorithms compress (like prices) or overload (like propaganda)? [[data-new-oil|Stage 15.4]] applies the “data vs knowledge” line to the slogan that data is the new oil.

Finally, the thread this lesson leaves hanging: if knowledge is dispersed and prices are grown rather than made, **can order itself be grown?** That is the spontaneous order of [[spontaneous-order|Stage 7.4]]. Before that, [[lange-market-socialism|Stage 7.3]] must face the socialist camp's cleverest reply — Lange's proposal to simulate a market.

## @analogy
Picture a large city's traffic with no central dispatcher — just the signals at each intersection and the drivers in their cars.

At eight in the morning an accident closes a main artery. No broadcast, no notice. The first drivers to reach the intersection see the jam and detour; their detour slows the neighboring road; drivers further out see that road turn yellow on their map and pick another; people further still notice nothing except that “this road is a bit slower than usual today” and casually switch. Half an hour later the city's traffic has redistributed itself, and the pressure around the accident is spread across dozens of streets.

**Not one driver knows what the accident was.** All they know is how slow this road is right now (the price) and what alternative routes they have (their own time-and-place knowledge). And the further away a driver is, the weaker the signal she receives and the less she adjusts — which is exactly right, because she is the least affected.

Now imagine a central control room. It must first learn of the accident (minutes later), then learn every car's destination and every driver's acceptable detour (which exist only in drivers' heads), then compute a city-wide optimum, then transmit orders to every car — by which time the accident may be cleared and a new jam has formed elsewhere. **It is not that it computes too slowly; it is that the inputs it needs can never be assembled, and would be stale if they were.**

Isn't a navigation app “central dispatch”? No. It delivers the signal “how slow is this road” to drivers faster — a better price display, with the decision still in each driver's hands. That is exactly Hayek's point: good technology makes the telecommunications system more sensitive; it does not replace it with a command center. [[ai-central-planning|Stage 18.1]] asks what happens if the navigation app drives the car itself — and you will find the problem has merely moved.

## @misconceptions
- **“Hayek's point is that there was too much information to transmit, so the internet made it obsolete.”** — He distinguished three kinds of knowledge: scientific knowledge can be centralized; time-and-place knowledge is stale on arrival; tacit knowledge cannot be articulated even by its holder. The last two are not “hard to transmit” — they **do not exist in transmissible form**. And much knowledge is generated in competition rather than sitting somewhere waiting to be collected.
- **“The price system is good because it gives everyone the most information.”** — The reverse: its beauty is that it lets each person **know very little**. When tin rises, the canner needs only the number and its own alternatives, not the news of the mine collapse. It compresses; it does not broadcast.
- **“Mises and Hayek said the same thing in different words.”** — Austrians genuinely disagree: Salerno and Hülsmann treat “calculation” (property → prices) and “knowledge” (dispersed → transmitted) as two arguments; Kirzner, Lavoie, and Boettke treat them as two faces of one. Both camps reject “the computer will solve it,” but they differ on which link is fundamental.
- **“The knowledge problem just says planners aren't smart enough.”** — Hayek's argument has nothing to do with intelligence. The smartest person alive is still not on the spot, does not hold the here-and-now facts, cannot access tacit knowledge, and receives information after it has changed. It is a problem of **position**, not **ability**.
- **“Central planning failed for lack of computers; Lange was right in 1967 that computers would solve it.”** — Computers solve equations, but the coefficients (preferences, alternatives, switching costs) are tacit, or are generated only in the market process, or are distorted by reporters with an incentive to lie. [[lange-market-socialism|Stages 7.3]] and 7.5 take this head-on.

## @quiz
1. According to Hayek (1945), the economic problem of society is not “allocating given resources” but what?
   - [ ] Getting everyone to possess all information
   - [x] Making use of knowledge that no one possesses in its entirety and that is dispersed among countless people
   - [ ] Making planners smarter
   - [ ] Solving mathematically for the optimal allocation
   > The word “given” hides the hardest part. The real problem is the **division of knowledge**: nobody knows everything, and society needs a mechanism that puts dispersed knowledge to use — the price system.

2. A tin mine collapses and tin rises from 20,000 to 28,000. A canner switches to aluminum. What does the canner need to know?
   - [ ] The cause, location, and recovery time of the collapse
   - [ ] The alternatives of every tin user in the country
   - [x] Only the new tin price and its own alternatives
   - [ ] The planner's new allocation
   > The price system transmits “how much to adjust,” not “why.” Whether the cause was a collapse or a new use, the canner receives the same number and reacts the same way — which is why Hayek said it is significant that the cause does not matter.

3. Which kind of knowledge is least likely to end up in a database?
   - [ ] The melting point of steel
   - [ ] The best fertilizer ratio
   - [x] A veteran welder's ability to spot a bad weld at a glance without being able to say why
   - [ ] Last year's national tin output
   > This is **tacit knowledge** (Polanyi): “we know more than we can tell.” It cannot be articulated, hence cannot be reported; it can only be used in action.

4. What is the disagreement between Salerno and Kirzner over Mises's calculation argument and Hayek's knowledge argument?
   - [x] Salerno holds they are two distinct arguments (calculation from property, knowledge from dispersion); Kirzner and others hold they are two faces of one argument
   - [ ] Salerno thinks Hayek was right and Kirzner thinks Mises was right
   - [ ] Both believe computers can solve the planning problem
   - [ ] They disagree about whether the USSR really existed
   > Both camps reject “a faster computer is the answer” and hold property, prices, and knowledge indispensable; they differ over **which link is most fundamental**. It is a real, unsettled internal Austrian debate ([[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]]).

5. “Better information technology makes Hayek's argument obsolete.” Which of the following is NOT part of the strongest Austrian reply?
   - [ ] Tacit knowledge cannot be written down in the first place
   - [ ] Much knowledge is generated in competition and does not sit anywhere waiting to be collected
   - [ ] Honest reporting requires an incentive to tell the truth, and in a planned system misreporting is rational
   - [x] Computers will never be as fast as the market
   > “Speed” was never the heart of the argument — that is the version opponents would prefer you to attack. The heart is the **form** of knowledge (tacit), its **mode of generation** (in the market process), and the **incentive structure** (property and responsibility).
