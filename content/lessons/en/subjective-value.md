# Subjective Value: Value Lives in Minds, Not in Things

## @hook
Last lesson showed that value attaches to “one more unit.” This lesson takes the word “value” apart: **value is not a property of a thing but a ranking that a particular person gives it at a particular moment.** It cannot be weighed, cannot be added up, cannot be compared between two people, and cannot be read from what you say — only from what you do. Those four “cannots” sound like limitations; they are in fact the Austrian School's four sharpest knives. One cuts away “intrinsic value,” one cuts away “total social welfare,” one cuts away “objective needs,” and one cuts away the economist's license to decide what is good for you.

## @intuition
Start with a question that sounds silly: **how much is an umbrella worth?**

You will say “it depends.” Right. Pouring rain, you are about to leave, you have no umbrella — it outranks everything else you want at that moment, and you would hand over $10 or $20 without blinking. Bright sunshine, three umbrellas already at home — it ranks near the bottom, and even $2 feels like paying for clutter. **The umbrella has not changed. You have.** The same umbrella ranks lower still for your neighbor who is staying in today. So the question “what is the umbrella worth?” is malformed. **Value is not a property of the umbrella; it is a judgment a particular person makes about it at a particular moment.** That is **subjective value** — where “subjective” does not mean “arbitrary” but “located in the subject, the person.”

This sounds like common sense. It has four consequences, each more radical than the last.

**First, value and price are different things.** A price is an exchange ratio observed in a market — “umbrella, $12” — objective, public, recordable. Value is a ranking inside your head — does the umbrella sit above or below $12 in your list? — subjective, known only to you. The price is the trace left behind when countless people's rankings collide ([[price-formation|Stage 1.3]] is about that collision). Mistaking price for value is the most common error in the news: “This painting is worth $100 million.” No: **one person paid $100 million**, and at that moment the painting ranked above every other use of $100 million in that person's mind.

**Second, value is a ranking, not a quantity.** You can say “I would rather have the umbrella than $12.” You cannot say “the umbrella gives me 1.4 times the satisfaction of $12” — there is no utility scale in your head. So value **cannot be added, cannot be averaged, cannot be compared across people.** This one quietly demolishes a lot of things that look scientific: “total social welfare,” “net social benefit of a policy,” “moving money from rich to poor raises total happiness.”

**Third, value shows itself only in action.** You say you care about the environment, but you drive every day. You say money doesn't matter, but you just took the job that pays 30% more. Austrians do not listen to what you say; they watch what you do. Rothbard called this **demonstrated preference**: a person's ranking can be read only from the choices they actually make, and only for the moment of choice.

**Fourth, if value lives in each person's mind, the economist has no standing to rank things for them.** Mises inherited Max Weber's principle of **Wertfreiheit** — value-freedom: economics describes whether means Y will achieve end X; it does not judge whether you ought to want X. That is not indifference; it is a division of labor. The economist draws the map. The traveler picks the destination.

One numerical example ties the four together. You buy a $5 coffee this morning. What else could $5 have bought? A bagel, a subway ride, or five dollars kept. You chose the coffee, and **that action demonstrated** exactly one thing: at that moment, the coffee ranked above the best other thing $5 could get you. Nothing more. It does not say the coffee “is worth” $5. It does not say you got “$7 of utility” from it (that is the textbook's “consumer surplus” — Austrians agree the surplus is real but deny it can be measured in dollars). And it certainly does not say that the person at the next table, spending the same $5, got “the same amount” of satisfaction as you. **One action, one ranking, one moment.**

From here we have to deal with several claims almost everyone believes that cannot survive subjective value: gold has “intrinsic value”; a painting has a “true value”; housing and health care are “objective needs” and so should not be priced by markets; Bitcoin “has no intrinsic value and is therefore worth zero.” You will find they all make the same mistake — treating a judgment as if it were a property of the thing. In [[bitcoin-regression|Stage 17.1]] that mistake reappears dressed up as the regression-theorem debate over Bitcoin.

Finally we have to answer the obvious objection: if everything is subjective, does anything go? No. **Ends are subjective; means are objective.** Whether water quenches thirst does not depend on what you think. Whether scarcity exists does not depend on what you think. Austrian subjectivism is far stricter than it sounds.

**In this lesson we break it into five pieces:**

- **① Value vs price: one lives in a mind, the other in a market**
- **② Ordinal, not cardinal: value is a ranking and cannot be summed**
- **③ Demonstrated preference: value is in the doing, not the saying**
- **④ No interpersonal comparisons, and value-freedom: why “social welfare arithmetic” does not compute**
- **⑤ No “intrinsic value,” no “objective needs” — but subjectivism is not “anything goes”**

## @mechanics
### ① Value vs price: one lives in a mind, the other in a market

Let us separate two words cleanly, because every later step leans on the distinction.

- **Value**: an acting person's judgment about where a thing sits in their ordering of ends. It is a **relation** — this person, this thing, this moment — not an attribute of the thing. Menger, in Chapter III of the *Principles* (1871), is explicit: value is nothing inherent in goods, no property of them, but “the importance that individual goods … attain for us because we are conscious of being dependent on command of them for the satisfaction of our needs.”
- **Price**: the exchange ratio that actually occurs in a market — one umbrella for $12, one hour of labor for $40. It is an objective, historical, recordable fact.

The relationship between the two: **prices are born from values, but a price is not a value.** Exchange happens precisely because the two parties' rankings are **reversed**: in the rain you rank the umbrella above $12; the shopkeeper ranks $12 above the umbrella (he has a stockroom full of them, and the marginal one sits far down his list — this is the marginal utility of [[diamonds-water|Stage 1.1]]). If both of you ranked umbrella and $12 the same way, neither would move, and no exchange would occur. **Behind every voluntary exchange are two opposite rankings.** [[exchange-division|Stage 1.5]] turns this into an a priori proof that exchange benefits both sides.

One confusion to clear away in advance: “the market price reflects society's valuation of the thing.” It does not. A market price reflects the valuations of **the marginal pair** of buyer and seller ([[price-formation|Stage 1.3]]). For the person who paid $100 million for the painting, it ranked above $100 million; for you and me it ranks below. “Society” has no ranking; only particular people do — that is **methodological individualism**, which [[individualism-history|Stage 2.3]] develops.

### ② Ordinal, not cardinal: value is a ranking and cannot be summed

Jevons and Walras treated utility as a measurable quantity — **cardinal** utility, where you can say “10 units of utility” or “1.4 times as much.” Menger, and later Mises and Rothbard, insisted utility is purely **ordinal**: there is a first, a second, a third — but no “how much.”

Why is this not hair-splitting? Because **“how much” requires a unit**, and there is no such unit inside a person. You can say the temperature is 20 degrees because there is a thermometer and a defined degree. If you say “the coffee gives me 8 utils,” what is a util? Nobody can define it, measure it, or convert yours into mine. Behavioral economics and neuroeconomics have gone looking for the scale — questionnaires, dopamine, fMRI — but what they find are **physiological correlates of satisfaction**, not the thing a person uses when they rank. [[vs-marx-behavioral|Stage 11.5]] assesses fairly how far that program has got.

Ordinality has three direct consequences, each of which dismantles a familiar piece of arithmetic:

- **No addition.** “The umbrella gives me 3 utils and the coffee 2, so 5 together” is meaningless. You can only say the umbrella ranks above the coffee.
- **No ratios of marginal utilities.** The mainstream “marginal rate of substitution equals the ratio of marginal utilities” divides two quantities that, on the Austrian view, do not exist. But you do **not** need that step to get a downward-sloping demand curve — [[diamonds-water|Stage 1.1]] already derived it from the logic of action.
- **No comparisons across people.** Your first place and my first place share no common ruler. This is the consequence with the biggest bite, and it gets its own section (④).

In fairness, Austrians disagree among themselves here. **Rothbard** held that even “indifference” (two things equally good) is not an economic concept, because indifference can never be demonstrated in action — you always end up choosing one. **Mises** was gentler. Some later economists (Bryan Caplan's well-known critique, and moderate Austrian replies) think that rejecting indifference curves outright goes too far. [[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]] covers these internal debates.

So what kind of number is a price? A price is an **exchange ratio** — one umbrella for $12. It is a number, but it is a ratio **between two goods**, not a quantity of utility. You can do arithmetic on prices ($12 × 3 umbrellas = $36) because money is homogeneous and countable; you cannot do arithmetic on values. **Economic calculation** ([[mises-1920|Stage 7.1]]) is possible precisely because prices are numbers — and it can only be done with market prices precisely because values are not.

### ③ Demonstrated preference: value is in the doing, not the saying

If value is a ranking that lives in a mind, how could we ever know someone's ranking?

Rothbard's 1956 essay “Toward a Reconstruction of Utility and Welfare Economics” answers with **demonstrated preference**: **a person's preferences are demonstrated only in their actual choices; the economist reads rankings from action — never from surveys, declarations, or “what would you do if …”** This is stricter than Samuelson's 1938 “revealed preference.” Samuelson assumed preferences are stable so that repeated observations can be stitched into an indifference curve; Rothbard concedes only that **this action demonstrated this ranking at this moment** — next time it may be entirely different, and economics has no warrant to assume otherwise.

The “actions only” stance has practical consequences:

- **Stated preferences do not count.** In a survey, 80% say they would pay 20% more for green products; supermarket sales say otherwise. Not hypocrisy — saying and doing carry different costs. Talk is free; buying is not. Economics counts the purchase.
- **Every action is a choice under scarcity, so it demonstrates both what you want and what you give up.** Buying the $5 coffee demonstrates coffee > the best other use of $5; not buying the $7 one demonstrates the best other use of $7 > that coffee. **This is where the opportunity cost of [[opportunity-cost|Stage 1.4]] grows directly out of subjective value.**
- **“He would have been better off doing B” is not a sentence an economist can utter.** If someone freely chose A over B, they demonstrated at that moment that A ranked above B. An observer who says B was really better is using the observer's own ranking.

Demonstrated preference also yields a positive and powerful result, which Rothbard used to rebuild welfare economics: **every voluntary exchange is demonstrated, by both parties, at the moment they act, to be beneficial to each of them** (otherwise they would not trade). So every trade on a free market is “ex ante win–win.” Any coercive intervention, by contrast, has at least one party demonstrating unwillingness (otherwise no coercion would be needed). This is the leanest Austrian framework for criticizing interventionism, and [[intervention-logic|Stage 8.1]] uses it.

One honest limitation: **ex ante is not ex post.** You buy the coffee and it is awful. The action demonstrated that you **expected** the coffee to be better, not that it was. Demonstrated preference tells us the ranking at the moment of action; it does not guarantee outcomes. This is exactly where **uncertainty** and entrepreneurial judgment enter ([[entrepreneur-alertness|Stage 6.1]]).

### ④ No interpersonal comparisons, and value-freedom: why “social welfare arithmetic” does not compute

Now let us take apart an argument almost everyone accepts.

**Steelman first.** Pigou and the welfare economists who followed argued: diminishing marginal utility applies to money too — the millionth dollar means far less to a rich person than the thousandth dollar to a poor one. Therefore moving $100 from rich to poor costs the rich person less utility than it gives the poor person, and **total social utility rises.** The argument is clean, intuitive, morally agreeable — and its first step (diminishing marginal utility) is an Austrian law.

**The Austrian reply** (first systematized by Lionel Robbins in *An Essay on the Nature and Significance of Economic Science*, 1932, and pushed to its conclusion by Rothbard in 1956): diminishing marginal utility is a law **inside one person's ranking** — your millionth dollar ranks below your thousandth. It describes order within **one value scale.** To say “the rich person loses less than the poor person gains” you must place **two different scales** on one common ruler, and by ② no such ruler exists. The “total” in “total utility rises” quietly converts two ordinal rankings into cardinal quantities that can be added. **Step two uses something step one never provided.**

Be careful about what this reply does and does not say:

- It does **not** say redistribution is wrong. It says “redistribution raises total social utility” is **not a scientific proposition** — it is an ethical judgment wearing arithmetic. You may advocate redistribution, but on ethical grounds, while acknowledging that you are making a value judgment.
- It does **not** deny that people have empathy or can roughly guess others' feelings. It denies that economics can turn that guess into a summable number.

The same knife cuts into **cost–benefit analysis**. A government appraises a bridge: “social benefits $800 million, social costs $500 million, net $300 million — build it.” That “social benefit” is countless people's valuations added into one number, via willingness-to-pay surveys or market prices used as proxies. The Austrian critique has two layers. First, surveys are not actions (see ③). Second, even where market prices are used, a market price is the valuation of the **marginal trader**, not of every affected person — the farmer whose land is expropriated does not rank that land at its market price. CBA can be a useful rough comparison tool, but the “net social benefit” it reports **is never the objective magnitude it pretends to be.** [[taxes-regulation|Stage 8.3]], on the hidden costs of taxes and regulation, shows how such sums systematically drop the “unseen” half — which is exactly what Bastiat, in [[opportunity-cost|Stage 1.4]], is about.

All of this leads to **Wertfreiheit** — value-freedom. Proposed by Max Weber and held to firmly by Mises in *Human Action*: economics as a science **can only study the relation between means and ends; it cannot rank ends.** The sentences an economist is entitled to utter have this form: “If your end is X, means Y will produce Z, and Z conflicts with X.” For example: “If your end is affordable housing for the poor, rent control produces a housing shortage ([[price-controls|Stage 8.2]]), which conflicts with your end.” The sentence an economist is not entitled to utter: “Affordable housing for the poor does not matter.” **Economics is the navigation system, not the destination.**

This constrains Austrians too. Mises was a utilitarian liberal, Rothbard a natural-rights theorist, Hayek an evolutionary conservative — they held their political positions **as citizens**, not as deductions from economics, and the three positions are mutually inconsistent ([[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]]). When an Austrian author treats “markets are good” as an economic conclusion, remember: **economics can only say what markets accomplish; “good” is something you add.**

### ⑤ No “intrinsic value,” no “objective needs” — but subjectivism is not “anything goes”

Apply subjective value to several popular claims.

**“Gold has intrinsic value.”** It does not. Gold is durable, divisible, scarce, beautiful — those are gold's **properties** (objective, physical). “Therefore valuable” is **people's judgment about those properties** (subjective). If tomorrow humanity stopped caring about a stable, divisible yellow metal, its properties would be unchanged and its price would go to zero. Mises's **regression theorem** in *The Theory of Money and Credit* (1912) addresses exactly “where does the value of money come from” — and answers: from people's valuation of yesterday's purchasing power, traced back to the day the thing was merely a commodity ([[origin-of-money|Stage 4.1]]). **Both sides of the crypto argument misuse the same logic.** Critics say “Bitcoin has no intrinsic value, so it goes to zero” — by that standard gold and dollars go to zero as well. Supporters say “Bitcoin is scarce, therefore valuable” — scarcity is a property, not a value; scarce garbage is still garbage. The right question is: **are there enough people, pursuing what ends, who rank it above other things?** [[bitcoin-regression|Stage 17.1]] re-examines the regression-theorem debate through that question.

**“The true value of this painting is …”** There is no true value. There is an auction price (the marginal pair's valuation), your valuation, the museum's valuation. An art critic may say “this painting is more important artistically” — that is the critic's ranking under artistic criteria, not a property of the canvas.

**“Housing, health care and education are objective needs and should not be priced by markets.”** The phrase “objective needs” hides a substitution. Needs exist, but **needs must be ranked against one another, and the ranking is subjective**: a climber trades life for a summit, a writer trades health for a manuscript, a grandparent trades medical treatment for a grandchild's tuition. When a planner declares housing an objective need, what he is actually doing is **replacing every person's ranking with his own** — without even knowing what those rankings are (the knowledge problem of [[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]]). This is not to say these goods are unimportant — they usually rank very high — but “high” is where each person puts them, not something read off the goods.

Now the objection: **if everything is subjective, does anything go?** No. Austrian subjectivism is far stricter than that. It is bound by four things:

- **Means are objective.** Water quenches thirst, bread feeds, penicillin kills bacteria — regardless of what you believe. Mises: praxeology is subjective about ends but **objective about means**. If you irrigate with a rain dance, economics does not judge your end, but it will point out that the means fails.
- **Scarcity is objective.** You cannot rank everything first. Ranking means something only because you must give things up.
- **The logic of action is universal.** Whatever you rank, “each unit goes to the most important unsatisfied end” holds for everyone ([[action-axiom|Stage 2.1]]).
- **Prices are objective facts.** Your ranking is subjective, but the prices you face are the outcome of other people's rankings, and you cannot change them by wishing.

So the precise statement of subjective value is: **about ends, only the acting person has a say; about means, scarcity, logic and prices, nobody's say matters.** That combination — complete humility about ends, complete firmness about means — is the methodology of the entire course from here on. In the next lesson ([[price-formation|Stage 1.3]]) we throw a crowd of subjective rankings together and watch a price grow out of them; [[opportunity-cost|Stage 1.4]] shows why cost is subjective too; [[zero-marginal-cost|Stage 15.3]] uses it to explain why software with zero marginal cost can still be priced; and [[attention-economy|Stage 16.1]] uses it to explain how “attention” became a scarce resource with a price.

## @analogy
Think of the value scale in your head as **a menu you can only sort, never score.**

The restaurant's menu has prices — objective, the same for everyone: kung pao chicken, $14. The menu in your head is different: no prices, only positions — what you most want tonight, what comes second. You can drag the kung pao chicken to first place and $14 to second, but there is no field where you could enter “2.5 points ahead.” And this menu **re-sorts every meal**: you had something spicy at lunch, so tonight the kung pao chicken drops to fifth.

The next table has a menu too, sorted completely differently. What can the waiter — the economist — do? He can tell you how long each dish takes, how spicy it is, what it costs. Those are **means**, and they are objective. He cannot tell you “you should want the fish more” — that is an **end**, and only you decide. And he cannot add your menu to the next table's and announce “total satisfaction in the restaurant tonight: 87 points” — the two menus share no scoring system.

How does anyone learn your ranking? **You order.** You say “I love fish” but order the chicken; the kitchen only sees the chicken. Ordering is what demonstrates tonight's first place — and only tonight's.

Finally, no dish on the menu is “born to rank first.” There is no intrinsically best dish, only “tonight, this person put it first.” Gold, paintings, Bitcoin, houses — all dishes on the menu. **The price is the number the kitchen prints after watching how everyone orders; value stays on each person's private menu.**

## @misconceptions
- **“Subjective value means value is arbitrary — anything goes.”** — What is subjective is the ranking of ends, not means, scarcity or logic. Whether water quenches thirst is not up to you; you cannot rank everything first; and you do not set prices alone. The Austrian formula is: total deference to the actor about ends, no deference at all about means.
- **“Price is value; the market price represents society's valuation of a thing.”** — Price is an exchange ratio, the trace left by the collision of the marginal pair's rankings. Value is each person's ranking. “Society” has no ranking; only individuals do. A $100 million painting shows that one person ranked it above $100 million — nothing more.
- **“Money has higher marginal utility for the poor, so redistribution raises total social utility — that follows from the Austrians' own law of diminishing marginal utility.”** — The law describes order within one person's scale. “The rich lose less than the poor gain” requires putting two scales on one ruler, and no such ruler exists. Redistribution can be argued on ethical grounds, but “total utility rises” is not a scientific proposition.
- **“Gold has intrinsic value and Bitcoin has none, so gold is money and Bitcoin is a bubble.”** — Nothing has intrinsic value. Gold's durability, divisibility and scarcity are properties; “therefore valuable” is a human judgment. Under one consistent standard the right question is not “does it have intrinsic value” but “do enough people, for what ends, rank it above other things” — which is the regression-theorem debate of [[bitcoin-regression|Stage 17.1]].
- **“A survey shows 80% of people would pay more for X, so X is highly valued.”** — Saying costs nothing; doing costs money. Demonstrated preference counts only the ranking shown in actual action. A survey measures what people say when the price of saying is zero; sales measure what they do when they must pay. Economics counts the latter.

## @quiz
1. In the rain you pay $12 for an umbrella. Under subjective value theory, what does that action “demonstrate”?
   - [ ] The umbrella's value equals $12
   - [x] At that moment, the umbrella ranked above the best other use of $12 in your value scale
   - [ ] You gained more than $12 of utility, and the difference is your consumer surplus
   - [ ] Everyone should buy an umbrella for $12 when it rains
   > **Demonstrated preference**: an action shows only the ranking at the moment of action — umbrella > the best alternative use of $12. It does not say the umbrella “is worth” $12, gives no measurable surplus, and says nothing about anyone else.

2. In the argument “moving $100 from a rich person to a poor person raises total social utility,” where do Austrians locate the error?
   - [ ] Diminishing marginal utility does not apply to money
   - [ ] Rich people actually need money more than poor people
   - [x] Treating two people's ordinal rankings as cardinal quantities that can be placed on one scale and added
   - [ ] Government transfers have administrative costs
   > Diminishing marginal utility holds only **within one person's** scale. Comparing across people needs a common unit of utility, and utility is ordinal with no unit. “Total utility rises” is an ethical judgment dressed as arithmetic.

3. Mises's principle of Wertfreiheit (value-freedom), inherited from Weber, says economists should do what?
   - [ ] Hold no political views
   - [x] Study only whether means achieve ends, without ranking the ends themselves
   - [ ] Study only phenomena that can be quantified
   - [ ] Support free markets because science proves them best
   > Economics says “if you want X, means Y produces Z.” Economists may hold views as citizens, but those views are not deduced from economics — the fact that Mises, Rothbard and Hayek held different political positions is the proof.

4. What is wrong with the sentence “gold has intrinsic value”?
   - [ ] Gold is not actually scarce
   - [x] Gold's durability, divisibility and scarcity are physical properties; “therefore valuable” is people's judgment about those properties, and judgments live in minds
   - [ ] Gold's value is determined by the labor required to mine it
   - [ ] Only fiat money has value
   > Properties are objective; value is a judgment. If nobody cared about yellow metal tomorrow, the properties would be unchanged and the price zero. The same logic applies to dollars, Bitcoin and paintings — the right question is who ranks it high, and why.

5. Which of the following does Austrian subjectivism accept as an objective fact?
   - [ ] Housing is an objective need for everyone and should rank high
   - [ ] The true value of a famous painting
   - [x] That water quenches thirst and bread feeds — the causal link between means and results
   - [ ] The size of total social welfare
   > What is subjective is the **ranking of ends**; the effectiveness of means, scarcity, the logic of action and market prices are objective. “Objective needs” and “total social welfare” are precisely subjective rankings disguised as objective magnitudes.
