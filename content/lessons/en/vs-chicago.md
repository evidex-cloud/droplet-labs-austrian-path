# vs Chicago & Monetarism: Where Friedman and Hayek Part Ways

## @hook
Friedman and Hayek were friends, allies, co-founders of the Mont Pelerin Society; both trusted markets, both fought Keynes, both demolished the Phillips curve. Yet Friedman said the Austrian business-cycle theory had “done the world a great deal of harm,” and Hayek said Milton's monetarism and Keynesianism had more in common with each other than he had with either. This lesson explains why they could say those things: **the disagreement is not about whether markets work, but about whether money is neutral, whether the 1920s were a boom, whether the Fed should exist, and how economics should be done.** The same Great Depression looks like two different stories through the two lenses — and each story explains something the other cannot.

## @intuition
First, Friedman as a Chicago economist would want him stated.

In 1963 Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz published *A Monetary History of the United States, 1867–1960*. In nearly 900 pages the book did something almost nobody had done before: it reconstructed the American money stock month by month for the better part of a century and set it beside output and prices. Its most famous finding concerns 1929–33, which the authors called **the Great Contraction**: over those four years the U.S. money stock fell by **about one-third**. Banks failed in waves (late 1930, 1931, early 1933), deposits evaporated, and the Federal Reserve — created in 1913 precisely to act as lender of last resort — stood aside, even *raising* rates after Britain left gold in 1931. Friedman's verdict: **the Depression was not a failure of capitalism; it was a failure of the Fed.** An ordinary recession became the Great Depression because the central bank let the money stock collapse.

An entire policy philosophy grew from that diagnosis. If discretionary central banking is that unreliable, take discretion away: **let the money stock grow at a fixed k% per year** (*A Program for Monetary Stability*, 1960). In his 1968 presidential address to the American Economic Association he introduced the **natural rate of unemployment**: the Phillips-curve trade-off between inflation and jobs fools workers once, in the short run; in the long run unemployment returns to a level set by real factors and inflation leaves only inflation. *A Theory of the Consumption Function* (1957) advanced the **permanent-income hypothesis**, knocking the floor out from under the Keynesian multiplier (people consume out of expected lifetime income, not this quarter's paycheck). “The Methodology of Positive Economics” (1953) held that a theory is judged by its predictions, not by the realism of its assumptions. And the **plucking model** (1964, restated in 1993): the economy is a taut string running along a ceiling; a recession is a downward pluck, after which the string snaps back. The depth of a recession predicts the strength of the recovery, but **the height of a boom does not predict the depth of the following bust** — which he presented as evidence against the Austrian cycle theory.

If you have done [[origin-of-money|Stages 4]] and 5, you will notice that Austrians and Friedman **agree about a great deal**: inflation is a monetary phenomenon; the Phillips curve is an illusion; price theory is the core of economics; markets allocate better than governments; the Keynesian multiplier is wrong. Both men were present at the founding of the Mont Pelerin Society in 1947; Friedman recommended *The Road to Serfdom* all his life.

So where is the disagreement? In four places, none of them small.

**First: is money neutral?** Friedman said yes in the long run — more money eventually means all prices rise in proportion and the real economy is unchanged. Austrians say **never**: new money always enters somewhere, early receivers gain, late receivers lose, and relative prices and the structure of production are permanently altered ([[money-demand|Stages 4.2]] and 4.3). “Proportional rise” exists only in the helicopter-drop thought experiment.

**Second: was there a boom in the 1920s?** Friedman looked at prices: stable in the 1920s, therefore no inflation, therefore the prosperity was real and the slump was a later Fed blunder. Rothbard (*America's Great Depression*, also 1963) looked at the money supply: it grew by roughly 60% from 1921 to 1929, and prices failed to rise only because productivity gains should have made them *fall*; stable prices masked a credit expansion, and the malinvestment was hiding inside the “stability.” **Two books, the same year, opposite diagnoses of the same history.**

**Third: should the Fed exist?** Friedman's answer: it exists, so bridle it with a rule. Hayek's 1976 answer, *Denationalisation of Money*: take the issue of money out of the state's hands and let private currencies compete ([[free-banking|Stage 9.4]]).

**Fourth: how should economics be done?** Friedman's 1953 positivism and Mises's praxeology ([[apriori-empirical|Stage 2.2]]) are nearly two different conceptions of science, and they lead the two camps to ask entirely different questions of the same data.

The lesson ends with a modern descendant — Scott Sumner's “market monetarism” and nominal-GDP targeting — which is interesting partly because one of the precursors it cites is the Hayek of the 1930s.

**In this lesson we break it into six pieces:**

- **① Friedman at full strength: the Monetary History, the k% rule, the plucking model**
- **② Allies: price theory, anti-Keynesianism, Mont Pelerin**
- **③ Disagreement one: neutrality and “always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon”**
- **④ Disagreement two: the 1920s — Rothbard vs Friedman**
- **⑤ Disagreement three: methodology and the existence of the Fed**
- **⑥ The modern variant: market monetarism, NGDP targeting, and the Austrian critique**

## @mechanics
### ① Friedman at full strength: the Monetary History, the k% rule, the plucking model

Compress Friedman's macroeconomics into one causal chain:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{change in the money stock } M \\
&\quad \rightarrow\; \text{(short run) fluctuations in output and employment} \\
&\quad \rightarrow\; \text{(long run) only a change in prices}
\end{aligned}
$$

Every link is held up by one of his books or papers:

- ***A Monetary History* (1963, with Schwartz).** M and nominal income are tightly correlated over the long run; the causation runs mainly from M to income, because changes in money usually *lead* changes in income and many of them have sources independent of the economy (gold flows, central-bank decisions, bank panics). In 1929–33 M fell by about one-third, the most violent contraction in American history; had the Fed bought government bonds on a large scale during the first banking panic in late 1930, it could have been avoided. After Benjamin Strong of the New York Fed died in 1928 the System was leaderless — an institutional accident.
- **The k% rule (1960).** Since discretionary action with “long and variable lags” (Friedman put them at roughly 6 to 18 months) tends to amplify fluctuations, the best rule is steady growth of M at about 3–5% a year.
- **The natural rate of unemployment (1968).** Monetary expansion can lower unemployment only by fooling workers into accepting wages that are really falling; once expectations adjust, unemployment returns to its natural rate and only the higher inflation remains. This predicted the stagflation of the 1970s and was the peak of Friedman's reputation.
- **The permanent-income hypothesis (1957).** People consume according to expected lifetime income; temporary changes are mostly smoothed by saving or borrowing — so the Keynesian marginal propensity to consume, and the multiplier, are far smaller in the data than in the theory.
- **Positive methodology (1953).** The sole test of a theory is its predictive power; “unrealistic” assumptions are not a defect but the price of abstraction.
- **The plucking model (1964; restated in *Economic Inquiry*, 1993).** The depth of a contraction correlates with the size of the following expansion, but the size of an expansion does *not* correlate with the depth of the following contraction. The economy is a string running along a ceiling, plucked downward and snapping back. **He explicitly offered this as evidence against the Austrian claim that booms cause busts.**

To be fair: this is one of the most successful research programs of the twentieth century. It predicted stagflation, it made “inflation is a monetary phenomenon” common sense, and it forced every central bank to watch the monetary aggregates (for a few years at least). **The Austrian critique is a critique of a formidable opponent.**

### ② Allies: price theory, anti-Keynesianism, Mont Pelerin

Before the disagreements, draw the shared ground clearly, because it is larger than most people think:

- **Price theory is the core.** The Chicago school (Knight, Simons, Stigler, Friedman, Becker) and the Austrians both treat prices as the nerve center of resource allocation and both resisted turning economics into pure mathematics (Chicago's “Marshallian” and the Austrians' “Mengerian” price theory were both minorities within the 1950s mainstream).
- **Anti-Keynesianism.** The permanent-income hypothesis and the Austrian “saving is demand for future goods” ([[vs-keynes|Stage 11.1]]) dismantle the multiplier from different directions; the natural-rate hypothesis and the Austrian critique of the Phillips curve (Hayek had argued in the 1950s that inflation can sustain employment only by accelerating) almost coincide.
- **Political philosophy.** Hayek convened the Mont Pelerin Society in 1947 with Friedman as a founding member; *Capitalism and Freedom* (1962) and *The Constitution of Liberty* (1960) are two books of one movement.
- **Distrust of discretion.** Neither trusted the judgment of central bankers — they merely gave different answers to “so what then?”

So when Hayek said that Milton's monetarism had more in common with Keynesianism than with him, he meant not politics but **macroeconomic method**: Friedman and Keynes both think in aggregates (M, total income, the price level) and both believe the economy can be stabilized by steering one of them; Hayek held that aggregates conceal what actually does the work — relative prices and the structure of production. That one sentence is the whole theme of this lesson.

### ③ Disagreement one: neutrality and “always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon”

Friedman's most quoted line: “Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon.” Austrians agree completely — and would change one word. **Inflation *is* the expansion of the money supply itself**; rising prices are only its most common symptom (Mises insisted on this older definition). That is not pedantry. If inflation means “rising prices,” then the monetary expansion of the 1920s “was not inflation,” because prices did not rise. If it means “monetary expansion,” the 1920s were a large inflation offset by the deflationary pull of productivity growth. **Half of the two schools' disagreement about the 1920s is a disagreement about definitions.**

The deeper disagreement is **neutrality**. In Friedman's framework money is neutral in the long run: double M and eventually every price doubles, with relative prices and real output unchanged — the helicopter drop, in which money falls uniformly from the sky. The Austrian reply ([[money-demand|Stages 4.2]] and 4.3): **there is no helicopter.** New money always arrives through an entry point — the central bank buying bonds, a bank lending to a developer, the Treasury paying a contractor — and those who receive it first buy at old prices while those who receive it last buy at new ones. That is the Cantillon effect, and its consequences do not “wash out in the long run”:

- In numbers: the central bank creates 10 billion and injects it by buying bonds → bond prices rise and yields fall first → developers borrow cheaply first → house prices, building materials and construction wages rise first → only at the end does the pensioner notice bread costs more. By the time “the price level” is up 5%, a batch of buildings that should never have been built already exists. **Those buildings are the permanent footprint of M**, whatever proportion prices finally settle at.
- Hence Austrians say **there is no such thing as long-run neutrality**, because “the long run” is the accumulation of a sequence of short-run structural changes, and those changes are irreversible (the heterogeneous capital of [[heterogeneous-capital|Stage 3.4]]).

Friedman was not ignorant of transmission mechanisms; he regarded them as second-order effects, with the aggregate as the first-order effect. Austrians reverse the order. That reversal directly decides the two schools' readings of the 1920s in the next section.

### ④ Disagreement two: the 1920s — Rothbard vs Friedman

**The Friedman–Schwartz story:** the 1920s were a healthy growth decade with stable prices and no inflation; the 1929 recession was ordinary; the catastrophe came when the Fed allowed the money stock to collapse in 1930–33. **The fault lies in the central bank's inaction.**

**Rothbard's story:** between 1921 and 1929 the Fed and the banking system pushed the money supply up by roughly 60% (his measure is broad — it includes savings deposits and life-insurance reserves — and is contested); prices did not rise because 1920s productivity growth should have delivered mild deflation; the suppressed interest rate steered resources into real estate, the stock market and capital-goods industries (the malinvestment of [[boom-malinvestment|Stage 5.2]]); the crash of 1929 exposed them. Then **the Hoover administration mounted history's first “anti-depression” intervention** — propping wages, expanding public works, the tariff, farm-price supports — and stretched a liquidation that should have taken a year or two into a decade. **The fault lies in the central bank's action (1920s) and the government's action (1930s).**

The difference between the stories reduces to one question: **is the boom itself the disease?** Friedman said no, on two grounds:
- Stable prices → no inflation → no disequilibrium. (Austrian reply: see the previous section — a matter of definition.)
- The plucking model → the size of an expansion does not predict the depth of a contraction → booms do not cause busts.

To the plucking model, Austrians (Garrison among others) answer: **malinvestment hides in the composition of the aggregate, not in its height.** During a credit expansion the buildings that should not have been built and the mines that should not have been opened all count toward GDP; the boom looks just like normal growth. The string was not plucked *upward*; it was pulled *sideways*. So you cannot see the pathology of a boom in the level of total output; you must look at the **structure of production** — how far capital-goods industries expanded relative to consumer-goods industries. The plucking data do not contradict ABCT; they measure with the wrong ruler. [[abct-critiques|Stage 5.5]] opened this argument; [[case-great-depression|Stage 13.2]] lays both schools' data side by side and lets you judge.

Austrians must also concede, candidly, that Friedman explained something they explain poorly: **the “secondary deflation” of 1930–33.** Hayek himself later admitted he had underestimated in the 1930s how badly the monetary contraction caused by bank failures damaged healthy firms; a necessary liquidation turned into a spiral that consumed everything. Austrians are still split on this ([[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]]): Rothbardians hold that deflation is part of the liquidation and should not be resisted; the Hayek–Selgin–White line holds that the central bank should at least prevent the money stock from collapsing (a “secondary depression” is not a cure). **Friedman was right about this, and some Austrians have long said so.**

### ⑤ Disagreement three: methodology and the existence of the Fed

**Methodology.** Friedman's 1953 position: a theory's assumptions may be wholly unrealistic (“firms behave *as if* they maximize profit”) as long as it predicts. Mises's position ([[apriori-empirical|Stage 2.2]]): economic theorems are deduced from the action axiom and their truth is not decided by prediction — because there are no constants in social phenomena, the same policy produces different results under different expectations, and “does it predict?” is not itself a stable test. This is not merely philosophy: **it decides what question each school asks of the same data.** Friedman asks “what is the correlation between M and income, and with what lag?”; the Austrian asks “through which entry point did this money come in, and which relative prices did it alter first?” The former can be written as an equation; the latter only as a narrative — a genuine Austrian handicap in academic competition, faced squarely in [[empirical-method|Stage 13.1]].

**The Fed.** Friedman's evolving position deserves honest recording:
- 1960: keep the Fed, bind it with the k% rule.
- 1980s: the link between monetary aggregates and income destabilized under financial innovation; the k% rule failed in practice (tried and abandoned in both Britain and the U.S.), and Friedman acknowledged it.
- Late 1990s: on Japan, his 1997 *Wall Street Journal* piece urged the Bank of Japan to buy government bonds in quantity to expand the money supply — an early version of quantitative easing.
- Late in life: in several interviews he said that, given the choice, he would abolish the Fed and replace it with a computer running a rule.

Hayek took the other road. *Denationalisation of Money* (1976) argued that the question is not which rule the central bank follows but **who has the right to issue money**. As long as issue is a state monopoly, any rule will loosen under political pressure (the public choice of [[economics-of-state|Stage 8.4]]). The answer: privately issued, competing currencies, with the market weeding out the ones that depreciate ([[free-banking|Stage 9.4]]). **Friedman wanted to put a bridle on the tiger; Hayek wanted to release it into the wild to compete with the other animals.** Interestingly, Friedman grew more sympathetic to Hayek's scheme with age — his 1986 article with Schwartz, “Has Government Any Role in Money?”, was already wavering.

One fair Austrian criticism concerns Friedman's **record of judgment**: in 2003 he praised the Greenspan Fed in the *Wall Street Journal* for stabilizing the economy “like a thermostat” — just as the housing bubble was inflating ([[abct-history|Stage 5.4]]). That is not a personal attack; it is a test of the theory: **if you watch only aggregates (inflation, nominal income) and not where credit is flowing, you will pronounce everything normal at the very moment the bubble is largest.**

### ⑥ The modern variant: market monetarism, NGDP targeting, and the Austrian critique

After monetarism receded in the 1980s, its most vigorous descendant has been the **“market monetarism”** of Scott Sumner and others: the central bank should target neither inflation nor a monetary aggregate but **the level path of nominal GDP** — steady growth in nominal spending of about 4–5% a year, topping up shortfalls and reining in overshoots. Sumner's reading of 2008 is that the real cause of the crisis was not housing but the Fed allowing nominal spending to collapse in the second half of 2008 (“too tight”); had NGDP been held, the recession would have been far milder.

Austrians should take this seriously, for three reasons:

- **It cites Hayek.** The Hayek of the 1930s did argue that the aim of monetary policy should be to stabilize MV (money times velocity — nominal total spending) rather than the price level, which is exactly the core of NGDP targeting.
- **Some Austrians accept half of it.** The free-banking wing (Selgin, White, Horwitz) argues that a competitive banking system automatically tends to stabilize nominal spending: banks issue more when money demand rises and less when it falls. Their disagreement with market monetarism is only over *who* does it — the market or the central bank.
- **Its criticism of the 2008 “secondary deflation” partly overlaps the Austrian one** (see section ④).

The Austrian critique — especially from the Rothbard–Garrison line — concentrates on one point: **a stable nominal aggregate is not the same as a healthy structure of production.** In numbers: suppose NGDP grows a steady 5% a year, 3% real and 2% prices. Now productivity jumps (say AI lifts real growth to 5%); to keep nominal growth at 5% the bank must hold price growth at zero — harmless, apparently. But if hitting the target in a weak year requires the bank to **inject credit**, that credit still enters somewhere, still lowers the rate, still manufactures malinvestment. **The 1920s were a decade of roughly stable nominal spending and a badly distorted production structure.** The stability of the nominal aggregate is a curtain; the relative prices behind it are the play.

The more fundamental issue is the **entry point**: market monetarists hold that what assets the central bank buys does not matter, only the total of nominal spending. Austrians hold that the entry point is everything — injecting through mortgage securities and injecting through short-term Treasuries produce two different economies. [[case-2008-qe|Stage 13.4]], on quantitative easing, works that difference through the 2008–2021 data; [[stablecoins-cbdc|Stage 17.3]] shows that once stablecoins and private digital money begin to compete, the question “who stabilizes nominal spending?” has, for the first time, a non-central-bank answer.

One sentence to close: **Friedman and the Austrians both trust markets, both oppose Keynes, both hold that inflation is monetary; but Friedman looks down from the aggregates and Austrians look up from the structure of production. The aggregate can tell you that money fell by a third in 1930–33; only the structure can tell you where the 60% of new money went in 1921–29, and why it was bound to fall.**

## @analogy
Two doctors examine the same patient: a middle-aged man who collapsed in the autumn of 1929.

**Dr. Friedman** reads the blood-pressure chart. He sees that in the four years after the collapse the patient's pressure fell by a third, and that the nurse on duty (the Fed) never started a drip. Diagnosis: **hypovolemic shock; the nurse failed.** Treatment: from now on a fixed 3% drip every day, whoever is on shift. His diagnosis is accurate, quantifiable, and it saved many later patients — Bernanke followed this prescription in 2008.

**Dr. Rothbard** opens the patient's file for the previous eight years. He finds that from 1921 to 1929 the patient was taking a stimulant called “cheap credit” every day, the cumulative dose rising by about 60%; the “temperature” on the check-up reports (prices) stayed normal, so nobody worried — but a normal temperature was hiding a heart running far above capacity. Diagnosis: **the patient collapsed not because of the blood loss afterward but because of eight years of stimulants before; and after 1930 he was force-fed painkillers — “hold wages up,” “public works” — that stopped the body from repairing itself.** Treatment: stop the stimulant, let the liquidation finish — though he concedes the bleeding after the collapse should have been stanched (Austrians still argue about this among themselves).

Neither doctor is lying. The blood-pressure chart is real; so is the file. The difference is that **one looks only at what happened after the collapse; the other insists on looking at what happened before** — and only the latter can answer “how do we stop him collapsing again?” Market monetarism is Dr. Friedman's student: it has replaced “blood pressure” with “nominal GDP” and made the drip rule cleverer, but it still does not open the file.

## @misconceptions
- **“Friedman and Hayek were on the same team; the Austrian School is a branch of Chicago.”** — Political allies, methodological opponents. Hayek said explicitly that Friedman's monetarism was closer to Keynesianism: both think in aggregates and both believe the economy can be stabilized by steering one. The Austrian unit of analysis is relative prices and the structure of production, which is fundamentally different from the Chicago macro framework.
- **“Friedman blamed the Fed for the Depression, so he opposed the Fed just as Austrians do.”** — Friedman blamed the Fed's *inaction* (it failed to start the drip); Austrians blame the Fed's *action* (the credit expansion of the 1920s). The former concludes that the Fed needs a better rule; the latter questions its existence. Hayek's Denationalisation of Money and Friedman's k% rule are two different roads.
- **“Stable prices prove there was no inflation.”** — This is the definitional root of the disagreement. Austrians define inflation as expansion of the money supply; when productivity growth should have made prices fall, “stable” prices are themselves evidence of credit expansion. The 1920s and the early 2000s both fit: mild CPI, badly distorted asset prices and production structure.
- **“The plucking model proves booms don't cause busts.”** — The plucking model measures the height of total output, but malinvestment hides in its composition: buildings that should not exist count toward GDP during the boom. The string was not plucked up; it was pulled sideways. To test ABCT you look at capital-goods industries relative to consumer-goods industries, not at the GDP peak.
- **“Austrians deny the monetary contraction of 1930–33 did harm.”** — Hayek himself later admitted underestimating the damage of secondary deflation, and free-banking Austrians explicitly favor preventing a collapse of the money stock. The internal split (Rothbardians vs the Hayek–Selgin line) is real and [[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]] develops it. Honest Austrians acknowledge Friedman's contribution on this point.

   - [ ] About 10%, because of gold outflows
   - [x] About one-third, because the Fed failed to stop deposits evaporating in successive bank panics
   - [ ] About 60%, because of the credit expansion of the 1920s
   - [ ] It did not fall; only velocity fell
   > The “Great Contraction”: three waves of bank panics cut the money stock by about a third while the Fed, the lender of last resort, stood aside — **the fault of inaction**. This is the monetarist core explanation of the Depression.

2. What is the crux of the disagreement between Rothbard's America's Great Depression and Friedman–Schwartz about the 1920s?
   - [ ] Rothbard held there was no growth in the 1920s
   - [ ] Both held the boom was genuine and differed only on 1930s policy
   - [ ] Rothbard held the Fed did not expand money in the 1920s
   - [x] Friedman held stable prices meant no imbalance; Rothbard held the money supply grew about 60% while prices should have fallen, so stable prices masked a credit expansion
   > Both books appeared in 1963 and read the same history: Friedman through prices, Rothbard through the money supply. Half the disagreement is definitional (inflation as rising prices vs monetary expansion), half is whether the boom itself is the disease.

3. Why do Austrians deny the “long-run neutrality” of money?
   - [x] Because new money always enters at some point, altering relative prices and the structure of production first, and those changes (e.g. buildings that should not exist) are irreversible rather than washing out proportionally
   - [ ] Because the money supply never increases
   - [ ] Because prices never rise in the long run
   - [ ] Because the central bank always withdraws the money it prints
   > The Cantillon effect: there is no helicopter; early receivers buy at old prices and late receivers at new ones; the resulting malinvestment is a permanent footprint. “The long run” is an accumulation of irreversible short runs.

4. When Hayek said Milton's monetarism had more in common with Keynesianism than with him, what did he mean?
   - [ ] That the two men shared a political position
   - [ ] That both opposed markets
   - [x] That both think in aggregates and believe steering one can stabilize the economy, whereas Hayek held aggregates conceal relative prices and the structure of production
   - [ ] That both favored the gold standard
   > A methodological point, not a political one: Friedman and Keynes practice “aggregate macro,” Hayek “structural macro.” That sentence summarizes the whole lesson.

5. What is the core Austrian criticism of nominal-GDP targeting (market monetarism)?
   - [ ] Nominal GDP cannot be measured
   - [x] Stable nominal spending is not a healthy production structure; credit injected to hit the target still enters somewhere, lowers the rate and creates malinvestment — the 1920s had roughly stable nominal spending and a badly distorted structure
   - [ ] NGDP targeting causes hyperinflation
   - [ ] Austrians reject any monetary rule whatsoever
   > Austrians grant that NGDP targeting cites Hayek's 1930s “stabilize MV” norm, and free bankers accept half of it (achieved by the market rather than the central bank). The core criticism: the aggregate is a curtain; the relative prices and entry point behind it decide whether malinvestment occurs.
