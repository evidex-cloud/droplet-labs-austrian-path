# Garrison's Model: Capital-Based Macro in Three Diagrams

## @hook
For decades the Austrian theory of the business cycle had an awkward problem: a good story and no diagram that would fit in a macro textbook. The mainstream had the Keynesian cross, IS-LM and the Solow model; Austrians had prose and one lonely Hayekian triangle. In 2001 Roger Garrison's *Time and Money* wired three diagrams that already existed — **the production-possibilities frontier, the loanable-funds market and the Hayekian triangle** — into a single linked machine: turn the saving dial and all three move together into sustainable growth; turn the credit dial and all three twist into boom and bust. **This lesson teaches you to run that machine**, and is honest about what it can and cannot do.

## @intuition
Start from two things you already know. [[saving-stages|Stage 3.3]] showed that when a society saves a little more, the interest rate falls a little, entrepreneurs dare to start longer projects, and the structure of production gets “stretched” — that is healthy. [[abct-one-picture|Stage 5.1]] showed that when the rate falls not because saving rose but because the central bank and the banking system created new money, entrepreneurs stretch the structure just the same, but consumers never agreed to wait longer — that is unhealthy, and it ends in liquidation.

The question is: what do those two stories look like on a diagram? A mainstream economist's first reaction is usually: “This ‘stretching,’ this ‘malinvestment,’ this ‘structure of production’ — can you draw it, so I can see it in a coordinate system I already know?” For decades Austrians did not answer that request well. Hayek's triangle from *Prices and Production* (1931) existed, but it stood alone, connected to nothing the mainstream used.

Roger Garrison, in *Time and Money: The Macroeconomics of Capital Structure* (2001), did something that looks simple and is in fact clever. He invented no new diagram. He took three that were already on the shelf:

- **The production-possibilities frontier (PPF)**: investment on the horizontal axis, consumption on the vertical, a bowed-out curve showing every combination the economy can produce with its resources. Every introductory textbook has it.
- **The loanable-funds market**: quantity of saving/investment on the horizontal axis, the interest rate on the vertical; an upward-sloping supply of saving, a downward-sloping demand for investment, and the rate at the crossing. Every introductory textbook has this too.
- **The Hayekian triangle**: production time (stages) on the horizontal axis, the value of consumer goods on the vertical, the hypotenuse showing value accumulating stage by stage toward the consumer. This one is the Austrians' own.

Then he **joined them with lines**. The investment quantity on the PPF is the investment quantity in the loanable-funds diagram; the interest rate set in the loanable-funds diagram determines how long the triangle's base is; the consumption quantity on the PPF is the height of the triangle. The three pictures stop being three separate facts and become three gauges on one machine: **move one, and the other two must move**.

Run it with numbers. Suppose an economy produces 100 a year. People save 20 and consume 80, the rate is 5%, and the chain of production has 4 stages. Now people become more patient and saving rises to 25. In the loanable-funds diagram the saving line shifts right and the rate falls to 4%; on the PPF the point slides along the frontier from (investment 20, consumption 80) to (25, 75); the triangle gets longer (say 5 stages) and shorter (consumption 75). A few years later the longer chain delivers more output and the whole PPF pushes outward. That is **saving-driven, sustainable growth**.

Now the other run. Saving is unchanged at 20, but the banking system creates 10 of new credit out of nothing. In the loanable-funds diagram the funds available to investors become “real saving 20 + new money 10” and the rate is pushed down to about 3.75%. Here something appears that you can only see clearly on this diagram: **the rate fell, so savers save less** (say 15), **but investors invest more** (say 25). Investment wants 25, consumption wants 85, the sum is 110 — and the economy can only produce 100. **The point on the PPF has moved outside the frontier**, a position that is physically impossible to hold for long. The triangle is pulled at both ends: the upstream (investment) end is being lengthened while the downstream (consumption) end is being raised, and the middle is hollowed out. The tug of war can be sustained for a while only by using up tomorrow's resources today; then comes the scramble for inputs, rising costs, a rebounding rate, unfinished projects — and the point falls from outside the frontier to inside it, into the region of unemployment and idle capacity.

The machine is worth more than its elegance. It lets someone trained only in mainstream macro see, for the first time in their own coordinates, what Austrians are talking about: **the triangle that mainstream models lack is simply what the aggregate “investment” looks like once you give it a time dimension**. Later in the lesson we set it beside the Keynesian cross and the Solow model — stating their strongest versions first, then showing why neither can see a cycle. [[aggregates-limits|Stage 10.2]] follows this thread into why aggregates like GDP flatten the triangle into a single number; [[vs-keynes|Stage 11.1]] and [[vs-neoclassical|Stage 11.3]] are the head-on conversations with Keynes and DSGE; [[ai-bubble-abct|Stage 18.5]] uses this same machine to read today's AI capital-spending frenzy.

One sentence on the machine's limits, to be spelled out at the end: **it is a diagram, not a measuring instrument**. It tells you direction and structure, not numbers and dates. That fact directly shapes the humility [[austrian-investing|Stage 10.5]] asks for when theory meets investing.

**In this lesson we break it into five pieces:**

- **① What each diagram says: PPF, loanable funds, Hayekian triangle**
- **② How they move together: saving-driven sustainable growth**
- **③ Credit expansion: driving a wedge between saving and investment**
- **④ Why this lets ABCT talk to the mainstream: the Keynesian cross and the Solow model**
- **⑤ Limits: it is still a diagram, not a measurement**

## @mechanics
### ① What each diagram says: PPF, loanable funds, Hayekian triangle

None of Garrison's three diagrams is new on its own. What is new is that their **meanings are reassigned**.

**The production-possibilities frontier (PPF).** Investment I on the horizontal axis, consumption C on the vertical. Textbooks usually use the PPF for “guns versus butter”; Garrison relabels the axes as “consumption now” versus “investment for later.” Every point on the frontier is full employment; inside it, resources are idle; outside it is unattainable. One detail matters: zero investment does not preserve the status quo, because capital depreciates. So there is a “maintenance point” on the frontier where investment just covers depreciation; to its right net investment is positive and the economy grows; to its left the society is living off its capital — capital consumption.

**The loanable-funds market.** Quantity of saving/investment on the horizontal axis, the interest rate r on the vertical. The supply of saving slopes upward (a higher rate makes deferring consumption more attractive); the demand for investment slopes downward (a lower rate makes more projects pencil out). Their crossing sets the rate — which, absent monetary disturbance, is the natural rate of [[natural-rate|Stage 3.5]]. Garrison deliberately labels the axis “interest rate” rather than “price of funds,” because he wants it read as the **intertemporal price**: it coordinates how long savers are willing to wait with how long investors need.

**The Hayekian triangle.** Production time on the horizontal axis (from the most upstream mining and research to the most downstream retail), the value of consumer goods on the vertical. The hypotenuse shows value accumulating as goods approach the consumer. The triangle's **base length** depends on the interest rate — the lower the rate, the longer the chain that can be financed; its **height** is the volume of consumption. [[roundabout-production|Stage 3.2]] covered it in detail.

Only three connecting lines are needed:

- I on the PPF equals I in the loanable-funds diagram (investment must be financed by something).
- r from the loanable-funds diagram sets the base of the triangle (the rate decides how long the chain is).
- C on the PPF equals the height of the triangle (consumption is where the triangle ends).

### ② How they move together: saving-driven sustainable growth

With the diagrams linked, run the “good story” first, using the numbers from [[saving-stages|Stage 3.3]].

**Starting point**: output 100, consumption 80, investment 20, rate 5%, a 4-stage chain. On the three diagrams: the PPF point sits at (20, 80); the S and I lines cross at r = 5% and quantity 20; the triangle has a base of 4 stages and a height of 80.

**The change**: time preference falls — perhaps a generation saving ahead of retirement, perhaps a culture that comes to weigh the future more heavily. Desired saving rises from 20 to 25.

The three diagrams respond in turn:

- **Loanable funds**: the supply of saving shifts right. More funds are available at any rate, so the rate slides down the investment-demand line, say from 5% to 4%. New crossing: quantity 25, rate 4%.
- **PPF**: investment goes from 20 to 25 and consumption from 80 to 75. The point slides down and to the right — note, **along the frontier**, never beyond it, because the extra 5 units of investment are exactly the 5 units of consumption that consumers themselves gave up.
- **Hayekian triangle**: at 4%, upstream stages that did not pencil out before (say a five-year research project) now do, and the base stretches from 4 stages to 5; meanwhile the height falls from 80 to 75 — demand for consumer goods dips for a while, retailers at the downstream end see thinner business, while equipment makers and research labs at the upstream end see more.

Here is a detail Austrians care about especially: **resources do not flow uniformly from consumption to investment; they flow from the far downstream to the far upstream**. Garrison describes the triangle as “pivoting” around a point in the middle: retail shrinks, research expands. The signals entrepreneurs see all agree — borrowing is cheaper, retail margins are thinner, upstream order books are fuller. Because the three signals point the same way, their adjustments are correct and no liquidation follows.

**A few years later**: the longer chain is complete and output rises from 100 to, say, 110. The whole PPF pushes outward; on the new frontier consumption can return above 80 while investment stays higher too. That is **growth driven by saving** — slow, but it never lies.

### ③ Credit expansion: driving a wedge between saving and investment

Now the “bad story,” which is the full version of the picture in [[abct-one-picture|Stage 5.1]].

**The change**: desired saving is unchanged at 20. But the central bank cuts rates, or the banking system loosens, and 10 of new credit is created from nothing and poured into the loanable-funds market.

**Loanable funds**: the supply of funds facing investors shifts right — but this time the shift is not saving, it is new money (labelled ΔM on the diagram). The rate is pushed down to, say, 3.75%. Then the single most important phenomenon in the whole model appears:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{real saving } S(3.75\%) \approx 15 \;<\; \text{investment } I(3.75\%) \approx 25 \\
&\text{gap of } 10 = \text{new money, corresponding to nobody's willingness to wait}
\end{aligned}
$$

The rate now tells savers and investors opposite things: to savers, “stop saving, the return is poor”; to investors, “invest more, funds are cheap.” **Saving and investment have been pulled apart** — Garrison's “wedge.” In a healthy market the rate makes S and I equal; a depressed rate makes them diverge.

**PPF**: investment wants 25 and consumption wants 85 (because saving fell). The point goes to (25, 85), outside the frontier. How is that physically possible? For a while it is patched together three ways: resources that should have covered depreciation are diverted to new projects, inventories are run down, and people work overtime — Garrison says the economy is “temporarily beyond the frontier,” at the expense of the future. This is also what the Cantillon effect of [[cantillon-inflation|Stage 4.3]] looks like at the macro level: where the new money arrives first (the investment side) expands first; where it arrives last (the consumption side) only discovers the dilution once prices have risen.

**Hayekian triangle**: the most vivid frame. The upstream end is lengthened by the low rate; the downstream end is lifted by rising wages and wealth effects. **Both ends are pulled outward at once**, and the middle stages — ordinary manufacturing and logistics that are neither new projects nor retail — lose resources to both. In Garrison's words the triangle is no longer “pivoting” but being “pulled apart.”

**The collapse in three steps**:
- First, the scramble for resources. New upstream projects and swelling downstream consumption both want workers and materials; input prices and wages rise; the new projects run over budget.
- Second, the rate rebounds. Saving never really increased; the one-off effect of the new money is spent; when investors go to refinance they find the rate back at 5% or higher (inflation expectations push nominal rates up too).
- Third, abandonment and liquidation. A five-year project discovers in year three that it no longer pencils out; it halts, dumps equipment, lays off staff. On the PPF the point falls from outside the frontier to inside it — unemployment and idle capacity. This is the liquidation phase of [[bust-liquidation|Stage 5.3]].

A concrete case: the United States in 2001–2006. The federal funds rate was held at 1%, the 30-year mortgage rate near 5.5%. Housing is a classic long-lived capital good (far from final consumption, very long duration), and the upstream end of the triangle was stretched by construction; at the same time rising home prices let households consume out of home-equity loans, so the downstream end swelled too. When the rate reached 5.25% in 2006, developments were abandoned, construction workers were laid off, and consumption contracted — both ends gave way together. [[abct-history|Stage 5.4]] tells that history in full; Garrison's three diagrams compress it into a single motion.

### ④ Why this lets ABCT talk to the mainstream: the Keynesian cross and the Solow model

Garrison's subtitle is “the macroeconomics of capital structure,” and he places the Austrian account deliberately alongside the mainstream's. To see the value of that, first state the two mainstream models at their strongest.

**The Keynesian cross.** Its core insight: total spending determines total output, and spending can fall short of full employment and stay there. A 45-degree line represents “output = income”; the total-spending line C + I + G has a slope below one; where the two cross is equilibrium output. If entrepreneurs' “animal spirits” collapse and I drops, the crossing moves left, output falls and unemployment appears — and **there is no automatic mechanism back to full employment**, because wages and prices are sticky and additional saving only lowers spending (the paradox of thrift). That was the lived experience of the 1930s: masses of idle resources and a price mechanism that seemed to have stopped working. Keynes's prescription follows: let G fill the gap left by I. This is not foolish; it accurately describes the state of an economy sitting at a point inside the PPF.

Garrison's reply is not that Keynes was wrong but that **the Keynesian cross draws only the region inside the PPF, and it treats “investment” as an aggregate with no time dimension**. It explains why a depressed economy stays at a low point; it cannot explain how the economy got there, because it has no triangle and so cannot see the structural mismatch inside investment. In Garrison's framework the Keynesian cross amounts to switching off the loanable-funds market (the rate no longer coordinates) and flattening the triangle into one number (I) — exactly the special case of the deepest point of a slump, not the normal state.

**The Solow model (1956).** Its core insight: long-run growth is determined by the saving rate, population growth and technological progress; more saving means a larger capital stock and higher output per head, up to a steady state where new investment just balances depreciation. This is the mainstream's own “saving drives growth,” fully compatible with the story of section ② — saving rises, the point slides along the PPF, the frontier moves out. The mathematics is clean and calibratable, and decades of growth accounting rest on it.

Garrison's reply: **Solow's capital is a homogeneous lump, “K,”** with quantity but no structure. So the Solow model cannot represent “more investment, but in the wrong places” — in its world more investment is always good. Yet the heterogeneous capital of [[heterogeneous-capital|Stage 3.4]] is the foundation of the Austrian cycle theory: a half-built five-year project cannot be turned back into two-year machinery. Solow describes the triangle getting bigger; Garrison's diagrams also describe it getting bent.

**How they relate**: in Garrison's own framing, his framework “treats Keynes as a special case (inside the PPF) and Solow as the long-run trend (the frontier moving out),” while the Austrian contribution is the layer in between — **the credit-driven path from a point on the frontier to a point inside it**. That is why it can converse with the mainstream: it does not ask anyone to abandon their diagrams, only to add a time dimension to them.

Candor is owed here too: that conversation largely did not happen. By the 2000s mainstream macro had turned to DSGE ([[vs-neoclassical|Stage 11.3]]), and Garrison's diagrams found almost no echo in mainstream journals. Their main effect has been pedagogical, inside the Austrian school, plus some indirect resonance after 2008 among non-Austrian economists working on “credit cycles.”

### ⑤ Limits: it is still a diagram, not a measurement

Listing what this machine cannot do is a precondition for using it well.

**It has no scale.** How far “beyond the frontier,” how many stages “longer” — all qualitative. You cannot read off “10% credit expansion produces a 3% output gap two years later.” Austrian methodology itself ([[apriori-empirical|Stage 2.2]]) forbids that kind of prediction: it deduces direction and structure, not magnitudes. [[aggregates-limits|Stage 10.2]] will show that even if you tried to measure, aggregates like GDP flatten the triangle, so what you measure is not the structure.

**It assumes a “natural rate” as its benchmark.** The whole wedge analysis rests on “what the rate would have been without new money.” But that benchmark is unobservable ([[natural-rate|Stage 3.5]]). Critics are right that you cannot, after the fact, attribute every slump to “the rate was too low” unless you can independently say how high it should have been. The Austrian answer is to rest the argument on observable changes in money supply and bank credit rather than on the rate itself — but this remains a genuine weakness.

**It is two-dimensional.** In a real economy new money does not only enter “investment”; it enters housing, equities, government spending; and there is not one triangle but many, with different industries of different lengths. Garrison adds several variants in the book (a deficit-financed government version, for instance), but every added dimension makes the picture harder to read.

**It does not model expectations.** If every entrepreneur had read Garrison and knew a rate cut was a false signal, would they still be fooled? That is the rational-expectations critique discussed in [[abct-critiques|Stage 5.5]]. Garrison's answer: even if you know the rate is false, you do not know how long it will stay false; and an entrepreneur who sits out the boom is pushed out of the market by rivals who join it. A strong answer — but not one that can be drawn.

**It gives no timing.** The three diagrams say “this structure is fragile and this direction will reverse,” not “next quarter.” This returns again and again in [[austrian-investing|Stage 10.5]] on investing frameworks: Austrian macro hands you a map, not a clock. [[common-mistakes|Stage 14.4]] records what it cost Austrian investors who forgot that.

With the limits stated, what remains is still substantial: this is the **only macro diagram that draws the interest rate, the consumption–investment trade-off and the time structure of production on one page**. Use it on the AI capital spending of [[ai-bubble-abct|Stage 18.5]] — upstream data centers, chips and power plants being stretched out under one rate environment and tested under another — and you will find that the picture of three diagrams moving together says more than any single aggregate.

## @analogy
Think of the economy as a **restaurant**. The dining room is consumption (dishes that reach the table today); the kitchen's prep is investment (meat curing for a week, stock simmering for two days, a kitchen garden that will not be harvested until next year). The restaurant has a fixed number of staff and a fixed pantry — that is the PPF.

**The saving version**: the customers say, “We're in no hurry — take your time, we'd rather come back next week for something better.” The owner moves two waiters into the kitchen to cure, simmer and plant. Today's menu is shorter and simpler (consumption falls), and the kitchen's chain of preparation gets longer (the triangle stretches). Next week the food is better, more customers come, the restaurant grows. All three gauges agree: customers willing to wait (saving), a longer kitchen process (the triangle), a thinner menu today (the point sliding along the PPF).

**The credit version**: the customers never said they would wait. But someone hands the owner a pile of “free” money, so he hires temps to cure and simmer and expand the garden — while cutting nobody from the dining room; in fact, flush with cash, he raises the waiters' pay and orders extra dishes for friends. Kitchen and dining room both swell (the triangle pulled at both ends), but staff and pantry are what they were (the point beyond the PPF). It holds for a few days: ingredient prices rise, the temps demand raises, the money runs out and cannot be renewed (the rate rebounds). Half-cured meat, half-simmered stock, a half-planted garden — all written off (liquidation). The regulars notice the prices went up and come less often. The restaurant is now emptier than when it started (the point inside the PPF).

**Garrison's contribution** was to fit this restaurant with three gauges: the split of staff between dining room and kitchen (PPF), the rate at which the owner borrows (loanable funds), and the length of each kitchen process (the triangle). Keynes looked only at the gauge that reads during the empty days; Solow only at the gauge that reads the restaurant's long-run expansion. Austrians ask you to watch all three — and to admit that these gauges have needles but no numbers.

## @misconceptions
- **“Garrison's three diagrams are a new Austrian model.”** — All three were already on the shelf: the PPF and the loanable-funds market are in every introductory text, and the triangle comes from Hayek's Prices and Production (1931). Garrison's contribution was to join them with three connecting lines into a single linked machine and to reassign the axes (consumption vs investment; the rate as an intertemporal price).
- **“More saving and credit expansion both lower the rate, so the effects are the same.”** — That is exactly the illusion the model breaks. With more saving, the PPF point slides along the frontier and saving still equals investment. With credit expansion, the point leaves the frontier and saving and investment are pulled apart by a wedge equal to the new money. Same number on the rate, different source, entirely different structure.
- **“The boom is too much investment, so the bust is ‘over-investment.’”** — The triangle shows both ends pulled at once: upstream lengthened, downstream lifted, the middle hollowed out. The problem is not the size of investment but a time structure that does not match consumers' willingness to wait — malinvestment, not over-investment. The Solow model looks only at the total and so cannot see the difference.
- **“If the Keynesian cross explains low output in a slump, it contradicts the Austrians.”** — Garrison treats the Keynesian cross as the special case of the region inside the PPF: it accurately describes an economy stuck at a low point but, lacking a triangle, cannot explain how it fell there. Not a contradiction; the Austrian picture simply draws one more segment — the path from the frontier to the interior.
- **“With these three diagrams, Austrians can predict when the recession comes.”** — The diagrams have no scale and no clock. They describe fragile structure and a direction that will reverse, not quarters and years. Using them as a forecasting tool is precisely the permabear mistake criticized in [[common-mistakes|Stage 14.4]].

## @quiz
1. In Garrison's model, saving rises by 5 units (from 20 to 25). How does the point on the PPF move?

   - [ ] It does not move, because saving only affects the interest rate
   > More saving means consumers voluntarily give up 5 units of consumption in exchange for investment, so the point moves **along the frontier**; investment plus consumption still equals output. Leaving the frontier happens only under credit expansion.

2. After credit expansion, what is the “wedge” in the loanable-funds diagram?
   - [ ] The difference in slope between the saving and investment lines
   - [x] The gap between reduced real saving and increased investment at the depressed rate — a gap exactly equal to the new money
   - [ ] The spread between the central bank's rate and commercial bank rates
   - [ ] The difference between nominal and real interest rates
   > The low rate tells savers to save less and investors to invest more, pulling the two apart; the gap is filled by new money that corresponds to nobody's willingness to wait — that is the wedge.

3. What happens to the Hayekian triangle under credit expansion?
   - [ ] It becomes longer and lower, just as under more saving
   - [ ] It becomes shorter and taller
   - [x] The upstream end is lengthened and the downstream end lifted — both ends pulled at once, the middle hollowed out
   - [ ] Nothing; the triangle reflects only technology
   > The low rate stretches the upstream end; wealth effects and reduced saving lift downstream consumption; the middle stages lose resources to both — Garrison calls this the triangle being “pulled apart” rather than “pivoting.”

4. How does Garrison position the Keynesian cross and the Solow model?
   - [ ] Both are wrong and should be discarded
   - [x] Keynes is the special case inside the PPF, Solow is the long-run outward trend of the frontier, and the Austrians add the credit-driven path from the frontier to the interior
   - [ ] Keynes covers the long run, Solow the short run, Austrians the medium run
   - [ ] All three are the same model in different notation
   > Garrison does not ask the mainstream to abandon its diagrams; he adds a time dimension in the same coordinates: Keynes describes the trough, Solow describes long-run growth, and Austrians describe the fall in between.

5. Which of the following is a genuine limitation of Garrison's model?
   - [ ] It cannot represent an increase in saving
   - [ ] It contains no interest rate
   - [x] It has no scale, relies on an unobservable natural-rate benchmark, and gives no timing
   - [ ] It is incompatible with the Hayekian triangle
   > The three diagrams give direction and structure, not magnitudes and dates; the wedge analysis needs a natural rate that cannot be directly observed. Acknowledging these limits is the precondition for using the model well.
