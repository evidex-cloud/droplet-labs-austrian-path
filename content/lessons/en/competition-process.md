# Competition Is a Process, Not a State: Hayek's Discovery Procedure

## @hook
The textbook's “perfect competition” looks like this: countless identical firms selling identical goods, none able to move the price, all knowing everything. What you see in the real world under the name “competition” is something else entirely: price cuts, advertising, new recipes, poaching staff, opening a new branch. In 1946 Hayek pointed out the absurdity — **the textbook's “perfect competition” describes a world in which no competitive activity takes place at all.** In 1968 he went further: competition is valuable because it is a **discovery procedure** — a way of finding out things that, without competition, nobody could know: who has the lowest costs, what consumers actually want, which approaches work. This lesson explains that reversal and what it implies for antitrust and the internet.

## @intuition
Ask a man who sells dumplings what “competition” means and he will tell you: the shop next door cut its price by fifty cents, so I need to do something — cut mine, make the filling better, open half an hour earlier, or put a sign out front saying “family recipe.” That is competition as a **verb**: a crowd of people jostling, each trying to do something the others have not.

Now ask a student fresh out of a microeconomics course. She will list the four conditions of “perfect competition”: infinitely many firms, a homogeneous product, perfect information, free entry and exit. In that world every firm is a “price-taker”; nobody sets prices, nobody needs to advertise (everyone already knows everything), nobody needs to improve the product (products must be identical). That is competition as a **noun**: a state that has already been reached.

Put the two definitions side by side and something strange emerges: **every single thing the dumpling seller does counts, in the student's model, as evidence of “imperfect competition.”** Cutting the price? Then you are not a price-taker. Changing the filling? Then the product is not homogeneous. Hanging a sign? Then information is not perfect. The model classifies every real-world act of competing as a “departure from perfect competition.”

Hayek called this out in his 1946 lecture “The Meaning of Competition”: **if the state of affairs assumed by perfect competition ever existed, all the activities described by the verb “to compete” would not merely have nothing to do — they would be impossible.** Perfect competition is not competition at its most intense; it is a photograph taken after competition has ended and everything there was to discover has been discovered.

So what is real competition doing? Hayek's 1968 answer: **discovering.** There is an enormous amount that nobody knows in advance — whose costs are really lowest, whether consumers will pay for a new feature, whether this material can replace that one, whether a city can support a third coffee shop. None of it can be found by survey, because the knowledge is scattered across millions of minds, and much of it comes into existence only when someone **tries** (the knowledge problem of [[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]]). Competition is a procedure that **lets everyone try at once and lets consumers' money keep score.** **The output of competition is not an “equilibrium price” but facts we did not know before and now do.**

The reversal sounds like wordplay; its consequences are large. If competition is a state, the government's job is to **maintain that state** — break up firms that grow too big, control prices that rise too high, restrict advertising that grows too loud. If competition is a discovery procedure, the government's job is to **keep out of the procedure's way** — no licenses, no protection for incumbents, no deciding on consumers' behalf what a “good product” is. [[monopoly-question|Stage 6.4]] uses this distinction for the monopoly question; [[platforms-winner|Stage 15.2]] uses it again for platform “winner-take-all.”

There is one more consequence, and it lives in your phone. The internet has pushed the cost of “trying once” toward zero — opening an online shop, launching an app, posting a video. The discovery procedure has therefore been accelerated many times over, and creative destruction has shrunk from decades to years ([[creative-destruction|Stage 15.5]]). Understand competition as discovery, and you understand why today's markets look messier than ever and are, at the same time, more effective than ever.

**In this lesson we break it into five pieces:**

- **① The steelman of perfect competition: what it assumes and what it is for**
- **② Hayek 1946: perfect competition describes the absence of competition**
- **③ Hayek 1968: competition as a discovery procedure — what it discovers and why nothing else can**
- **④ Entry barriers: natural versus state-made**
- **⑤ Consequences: how to think about antitrust, and how the internet speeds things up**

## @mechanics
### ① The steelman of perfect competition: what it assumes and what it is for

Start by stating the strongest version of the other side. The perfect-competition model — systematized by Frank Knight in 1921, standard in every textbook since — rests on four assumptions:

- **Very many firms**, each so small relative to the market that its output has no effect on price: firms are **price-takers**.
- **A homogeneous product**: every firm sells exactly the same thing, and consumers look only at price.
- **Perfect information**: everyone knows every price, every technology, every preference.
- **Free entry and exit**: no cost to enter or leave.

From these one can derive an elegant set of results: price equals marginal cost (P = MC); each firm's long-run profit is zero (it earns only “normal profit,” i.e., its opportunity cost); and the allocation of resources is Pareto-optimal — no reshuffling could make anyone better off without making someone worse off.

The model **has legitimate uses**, and Austrians should not pretend otherwise:

- As a **benchmark**: it tells you where prices and quantities would be in the limiting case in which all existing information has been fully used. Measuring the distance between reality and that benchmark is sometimes useful.
- As a **teaching device**: it displays the relations among supply, demand and cost in the simplest possible way; students learn it before the complicated cases.
- As the **anchor of welfare economics**: “P = MC is optimal” is the starting point of every later analysis of “market failure” — monopoly, externalities, public goods are all defined as departures from this benchmark.

Austrians do not deny that the model is logically consistent. The Austrian criticism is that **it answers not “how does competition work?” but “what does the world look like after competition is finished?”** — and mistaking the second question for the first leads to exactly backwards judgments about real competitive behavior.

### ② Hayek 1946: perfect competition describes the absence of competition

In May 1946 Hayek delivered a lecture at Princeton titled “The Meaning of Competition,” published in 1948 in *Individualism and Economic Order*. His argument compresses into three steps.

**Step one: the model assumes what it was supposed to explain.** Of the four assumptions, the fatal one is “perfect information.” Whose costs are lowest? What do consumers want? Which technology is best? The model says: “these are all known.” But in the real world **these are precisely the things competition exists to find out.** Assuming them known is assuming that competition is already over.

**Step two: so there is no room in the model for any competitive act.** Think of what the verb “to compete” covers: **cutting prices** to win customers, **advertising** so people know you exist, **improving the product** to stand out from rivals, **opening a new branch** to enter a new market, **hiring away** a better team. In the perfect-competition model none of these can happen: a price-taker cannot cut price (price is given), a homogeneous product needs no advertising or differentiation (everyone's is the same), and perfect information means nobody needs to be told anything. Hayek's point, in substance: **the state of affairs described by perfect competition, were it ever to exist, would strip every activity the verb “to compete” refers to of its purpose, and make them all impossible.**

**Step three: the model therefore condemns all real competition as “imperfect.”** This is the worst consequence. When a firm beats its rivals by cutting prices, innovating, or advertising, the model sees “market power, product differentiation, information asymmetry” — three “defects.” It is as if someone who had never seen a race, only the photo at the finish line, declared: “a true race is one in which everyone stands on the finish line simultaneously; any race in which someone is out in front is an imperfect race.”

A numerical example makes the inversion vivid. Three bakeries have costs of $6, $7 and $8 per loaf; the market price is $8. The first baker discovers a new kneading method that cuts her cost to $5, and drops her price to $6.50 to win customers. **The model's reading**: this bakery “is not a price-taker” (it set its own price), and the market “is imperfect” (the other two are forced to respond). **The realistic reading**: this is competition at its healthiest — consumers save $1.50 a loaf, the other two must learn the new method or exit, and one person's discovery has rewritten the cost structure of the entire trade. **The model has classified competition's finest moment as competition's defect.**

### ③ Hayek 1968: competition as a discovery procedure — what it discovers and why nothing else can

In 1968, in a lecture at the University of Kiel, Hayek gave the positive definition: “Competition as a Discovery Procedure.” The core claim fits in one sentence: **we use competition because it discovers facts that, without competition, nobody could know.**

Discover what? At least four kinds of thing:

- **Who can produce at lowest cost.** There is no ready-made cost table; each firm's costs are the product of its own organization, technology, location and tacit knowledge — much of it unknown even to the firm until it tries.
- **What consumers actually want.** Surveys cannot tell you — what people say they want and what they pay for often differ. Only when a product is put on the shelf with a price on it and consumers vote with their money ([[profit-loss|Stage 6.3]]) is the preference “revealed.”
- **Which resources can substitute for which.** Can aluminum replace copper? Can outsourcing replace in-house production? The answers shift with prices, technology and time; they can only be tried.
- **How many firms a market can support.** Can a third coffee shop survive? Open it and see.

Why can nothing else do the job? Because this knowledge has three properties, two of which Hayek already covered in [[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]]: it is **dispersed** (scattered across countless minds), it is often **tacit** (the people who hold it cannot articulate it; they can only do it), and — the crucial one — much of it is **generated only in the act of trying.** The third point is decisive: before a firm attempts to lower its costs, the fact “whether its costs can be lowered” **does not yet exist.** So even a central planner who could gather every piece of existing information in the world could not gather the information that **only competition brings into being.** That is why Hayek calls competition a **procedure**, like a scientific experiment or a sporting contest: we hold the contest precisely **because we do not know who will win**; if we knew in advance, there would be no point.

Note how the loop connects to [[entrepreneur-alertness|Stage 6.1]]: **① the guess** is the entrepreneur's alertness and judgment, and **③ profit/loss** is the subject of the next lesson ([[profit-loss|Stage 6.3]]). Competition is not some “market structure” external to entrepreneurs; it is **the sum of countless entrepreneurs judging simultaneously and correcting one another.** Kirzner calls this the “competitive entrepreneurial process”: as long as entry is free, any profit one entrepreneur earns is an invitation to every other — “there is something here you have not seen.”

### ④ Entry barriers: natural versus state-made

If the heart of competition is “being free to try,” then the heart of what obstructs competition is “not being allowed to try” — **entry barriers.** Here Austrians draw a distinction that mainstream textbooks often blur.

**Natural barriers (cost barriers).** Entering some industries is simply expensive. A chip fab costs tens of billions of dollars; an airline needs aircraft and crews; a social network needs users before it has anything to offer (the network effects of [[network-effects|Stage 15.1]]). These barriers reflect the **real scarcity of resources** — they tell potential entrants “do not come unless you are confident you can do better than the incumbent,” which is exactly the message a discovery procedure should transmit. And they are never absolute: whenever incumbents' profits are high enough, capital finds a way (TSMC, Ryanair and TikTok all grew up in industries thought to be “impossible to enter”). George Stigler of the Chicago school (1968) offered a stricter definition: **an entry barrier is a cost the entrant must bear that the incumbent did not.** On that definition, “building a factory is expensive” is not a barrier (the incumbent built one too); it is just a high threshold.

**State barriers (legal barriers).** Entering some industries is **illegal**, or requires a license issued in limited numbers. Taxi medallions (in New York a medallion traded for roughly a million dollars around 2013 — that price is the market value of “forbidden competition”), occupational licenses, import quotas, tariffs, franchises, patents, “certificate of need” laws (in many U.S. states, opening a new hospital first requires proving to the government that the area “needs” one). What these have in common: **whether or not you could do better, you are not allowed to try.** The discovery procedure is cut off at the root — not because resources are scarce, but because incumbents have persuaded legislators. [[taxes-regulation|Stage 8.3]] covers the hidden costs of such regulation in detail, and [[economics-of-state|Stage 8.4]] explains why incumbents always have an incentive to lobby.

Why does the distinction matter? Because **the same observation — an industry with only two or three large firms — means opposite things in the two cases.** Two or three firms behind natural barriers are the winners of the discovery procedure, facing new challengers at all times; the moment they slacken they are replaced. Two or three firms behind state barriers are protected; they need discover nothing, and consumers have nowhere else to go. The Austrian test is not “how many firms?” but “**is entry free?**”

### ⑤ Consequences: how to think about antitrust, and how the internet speeds things up

**For antitrust.** If your yardstick is perfect competition, then every departure — large firms, high profits, brands, advertising, price differences — is evidence of “market power” and a candidate for intervention. If you understand competition as a discovery procedure, all of these must be re-read: **high profits are the signal the procedure is transmitting** (“there is something here worth imitating”), **brands and advertising are carriers of information** ([[profit-loss|Stage 6.3]] develops this), and **price differences are discoveries not yet completed.** Austrians do not claim that “big firms are always innocent” — [[monopoly-question|Stage 6.4]] spells out what a genuine monopoly problem is — but they insist the antitrust test should be: **was this firm's position won by serving consumers, or by having the law keep others out?** The first is the result of competition; only the second is its enemy. The question becomes acute in [[platforms-winner|Stage 15.2]] on platform “winner-take-all”: a search engine with 90% share — is it there because it is re-tested by every single search, every day, or because nobody else may enter?

**For the internet.** The speed of a discovery procedure depends on the cost of “trying once.” In the industrial era a try was expensive: build a plant, set up distribution, run a national ad campaign. The internet has pushed that cost toward zero — one person, one afternoon, can launch an online shop, an app, or a video, and receive consumer feedback **within hours.** The result is a discovery loop that turns many times faster: last year's hit is replaced this year, and a single category has thousands of variants under test at once. This is the mechanism behind “creative destruction at internet speed” in [[creative-destruction|Stage 15.5]], and a key lens for [[algorithms-order|Stage 16.2]] on recommendation algorithms — the algorithm itself is a discovery machine, testing millions of items to find “what people actually want to see.”

**What Austrians must candidly concede.** The defense of competition as discovery has two real weak points. First, discovery is costly: countless failed entrepreneurs burn resources — that is the procedure's price, and Austrians should face it rather than wave it away. Second, when incumbents use network effects or accumulated data to raise the cost of “trying once” for newcomers, the line between natural and state barriers blurs — a contemporary problem that [[platforms-winner|Stages 15.2]] and 15.4 must take seriously, not dismiss with “the market will sort it out.”

The lesson in one sentence: **competition is not a photograph of everyone standing on the finish line; it is a race whose winner nobody knows — and we need it precisely because nobody knows.**

## @analogy
Think of the market as a **cooking contest** with no judges' table — the judges are tens of thousands of ticket-holders who wander freely and spend as they please.

**The textbook's “perfect competition”** describes the contest like this: every cook makes the identical dish from the identical recipe, every spectator already knows exactly how every dish tastes, every stall has the same queue, every price is the same. You would ask: then what is being contested? The answer: **nothing.** That is not a contest; it is the scene after the contest is over and everyone has learned the same dish.

**The real contest** looks like this: nobody knows whether the crowd wants spicy or sweet today. One cook tries putting cheese in the dumplings, another halves her price, a third hangs a sign reading “three generations of tradition.” The crowd votes with money: a queue forms at the cheese-dumpling stall; the price-cutter is run off her feet but finds at closing time that she lost money; nobody bothers with “tradition.” The next day everyone is trying cheese, prices readjust, and “tradition” becomes “new flavor.” **At the end of every round, the whole hall knows one more thing than it did yesterday** — what the crowd actually wants, and who can make it well and cheaply. That is the discovery procedure.

Now imagine two ways of wrecking it. One is **an expensive entry ticket**: a stall costs a fortune to rent, so many cooks who wanted to try cannot get in. That is a natural barrier — annoying, but as long as cheese dumplings are profitable enough, someone will scrape the money together and come in. The other is **a rule from the organizers that only three established houses may compete.** That is a state barrier: however unhappy the crowd, tomorrow they face the same three houses, and cheese dumplings are never invented.

Looking ahead: [[profit-loss|Stage 6.3]] shows how the crowd's money becomes a score for every cook; [[monopoly-question|Stage 6.4]] asks whether a stall with an unusually long queue should be broken up; [[creative-destruction|Stage 15.5]] shows what the contest becomes when it moves online and anyone can set up a stall at zero cost.

## @misconceptions
- **“Perfect competition is the ideal that reality merely fails to reach.”** — Perfect competition describes the state **after** competition has ended: all information known, all costs discovered, no room for any action. It is not the peak of competition but its absence. Real competitive acts — price cuts, innovation, advertising — are precisely what cannot happen inside the model.
- **“Advertising, brands and product differentiation are evidence of market imperfection.”** — Seen as a discovery procedure, they are competition itself: advertising carries the message “I exist and I am different” to people who did not know; differentiation is a guess about what consumers actually want. Calling them defects is treating the finish-line photo as the rules of the race.
- **“An industry with only two or three big firms must be short of competition.”** — The number of firms is not the test; **whether entry is free** is. Two or three firms behind natural barriers face challengers constantly and are replaced the moment they slacken; only behind state barriers do consumers have nowhere else to go. The same structure can mean opposite things.
- **“Austrians think the market always fixes everything and there are no real entry barriers.”** — Austrians accept that natural barriers are real (a chip fab really does cost tens of billions) and that network effects and data accumulation can raise a newcomer's cost of trying to nearly the level of a state barrier ([[platforms-winner|Stages 15.2]], 15.4). The Austrian claim is to distinguish the two kinds of barrier, not to deny barriers exist.
- **“If competition is a discovery procedure, government could replace it with surveys and big data and just ‘discover’ the optimum directly.”** — A large part of what competition discovers is **generated only in the act of trying**: before a firm attempts to cut costs, the fact of whether it can does not yet exist. Surveys can only collect information that already exists, not information that has not yet been created ([[bigdata-planning|Stages 7.5]], 18.1).

## @quiz
1. What is the central claim of Hayek's 1946 lecture “The Meaning of Competition”?
   - [ ] The mathematics of the perfect-competition model contains an error
   - [x] If the state of affairs assumed by perfect competition existed, every activity the word “competition” refers to would be impossible
   - [ ] Monopoly is more efficient than perfect competition
   - [ ] Governments should force firms to achieve perfect competition
   > The model assumes perfect information, homogeneous goods and price-taking firms — so price cuts, advertising, differentiation and entry, the real acts of competing, have **no place** in it. Perfect competition describes the static state after competition is finished, not competition itself.

2. A bakery discovers a new method that cuts its cost from $6 to $5 and lowers its price from $8 to $6.50. How does the perfect-competition model read this event?
   - [ ] As competition at its healthiest
   - [x] As evidence the firm is not a price-taker and the market is “imperfect”
   - [ ] As predatory pricing that should be banned
   - [ ] As irrelevant, because the model only considers the long run
   > In the model firms cannot set prices, so “cutting price to win customers” is classified as a departure from perfect competition. In the discovery view it is the high point of competition: one discovery rewrote the industry's cost structure and consumers saved $1.50.

3. On Hayek's 1968 argument, why can what competition discovers not be replaced by surveys or planning?
   - [ ] Because surveys are too expensive
   - [ ] Because firms would lie to the surveyors
   - [x] Because much of the knowledge is generated in the act of trying and does not exist before the attempt
   - [ ] Because governments lack sufficient computing power
   > Dispersion and tacitness are only part of the problem; the decisive property is **generation**: before a firm tries to cut costs, the fact of whether it can does not yet exist. No data-gathering, however powerful, can collect information not yet created ([[bigdata-planning|Stages 7.5]] and 18.1 return to this).

4. Which of the following is a “state barrier” rather than a “natural barrier” in the Austrian sense?
   - [ ] Building a chip fab costs tens of billions of dollars
   - [ ] A new social network lacks users and suffers from network effects
   - [x] Opening a new hospital requires first proving to the government that the area “needs” one
   - [ ] An airline must buy aircraft and train crews
   > The other three reflect genuine resource scarcity — high thresholds that can be crossed. Certificate-of-need laws mean **you may not try, no matter how much better you could do** — the discovery procedure is cut off by law. The same logic put a New York taxi medallion at roughly a million dollars.

5. Starting from “competition is a discovery procedure,” what is the right test of whether a firm with 90% market share is harmful?
   - [ ] Whether its share exceeds 50%
   - [ ] Whether its profit margin exceeds the industry average
   - [x] Whether its position was won by serving consumers and entry remains free, or was secured by law keeping others out
   - [ ] Whether it advertises
   > In the discovery view, share and margins are signals, not evidence of guilt. The real question is **whether entry is free**: a position maintained by winning every consumer test can be replaced at any time; a position protected by law is the one that leaves consumers no exit ([[monopoly-question|Stages 6.4]], 15.2).
