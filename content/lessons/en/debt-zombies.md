# Debt, Zombie Firms & Secular Stagnation

## @hook
[[bust-liquidation|Stage 5.3]] said the bust is the cure: liquidation sends resources back to where consumers actually want them. But what if the cure is postponed indefinitely? Hold rates near zero for a decade and let firms that should have failed live on by borrowing to pay interest — the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) gave them a name: **zombie firms**, companies that cannot earn their interest and yet do not die. This lesson covers how they arise, how they drag down the productivity of an entire economy, why Japan is the thirty-year textbook case, and the mainstream idea of “secular stagnation” — stated first in Larry Summers's strongest form, then answered the Austrian way: **stagnation is not fate; it is made by policy, and unliquidated malinvestment is its corpse.**

## @intuition
Picture a paper mill that borrowed 100 million in 2005 to build a new production line, at 6% — 6 million a year in interest. The line earns only 4 million a year. By the logic of [[bust-liquidation|Stage 5.3]] the mill should fail: the machines sold at a discount to someone who can use them better, the workers moved to an industry with a future.

But in 2009 the central bank cuts rates to 1%. The mill's bank “refinances” it: a new loan at 1.5%, only 1.5 million a year in interest. The 4 million of profit now covers the interest with 2.5 million to spare. The mill survives — not because it got better, but because **the interest rate commuted its death sentence to life imprisonment.**

Five years later the machines are old, the product has been undercut by cheaper rivals, profit is down to 1 million — not even enough for the 1.5 million of interest. What does the bank do? Writing the loan off would shrink its capital and bring the regulator to the door. So the bank “extends”: it lends the mill another 2 million so the mill can pay the interest. On the mill's books, the interest was “paid”; on the bank's books, the loan is “performing.” **Nobody defaulted, and nobody is creating value.** That is a zombie firm. The BIS definition: a company whose **operating profit has been insufficient to cover its interest for three consecutive years** (interest coverage ratio below 1).

BIS economists Ryan Banerjee and Boris Hofmann counted listed firms in 14 advanced economies in a 2018 study: **the share of zombies rose from about 2% in the late 1980s to about 12% around 2016** (broad definition), and it ratcheted up after every recession without ever fully falling back. They found that lower rates go with more zombies, and that in industries with more zombies, healthy firms invest and hire less. This is not Austrian research; it is the central banks' own central bank.

Why do zombies drag everyone down? Go back to the heterogeneous capital of [[heterogeneous-capital|Stage 3.4]] and the profit-and-loss mechanism of [[profit-loss|Stage 6.3]]. The market's engine of evolution is that loss-making firms release resources — machines, buildings, workers, credit lines — and profitable firms absorb them. Zombies jam the engine: they sit on capital, labor and bank credit while producing below average — **so average productivity is pulled down**; they cut prices to survive, squeezing margins across their industry so that healthy firms hesitate to invest; they occupy bank balance sheets so that banks have no room to lend to new firms. The result is an economy that looks as if it “has no recession” but has stopped growing — everyone alive, nobody growing.

Japan is the prototype. After the bubble burst in 1990, the Bank of Japan cut rates to zero and banks rolled over bad loans for a decade; the phrase “zombie firm” was coined to describe 1990s Japan (the famous 2008 paper by Caballero, Hoshi and Kashyap). Thirty years on, Japan's nominal GDP sits roughly where it was in the 1990s and government debt is about 250% of GDP — [[case-japan|Stage 13.3]] tells that story in full.

The mainstream has a name for this state of low growth, low rates and low inflation: **secular stagnation**, revived by Lawrence Summers in 2013. His explanation is strong: an aging population, a savings glut and weak investment demand have pushed the “natural rate” itself below zero — so low rates are not imposed by central banks but demanded by the economy; the central bank merely follows. We will take that argument seriously and then give the Austrian reply: **if the natural rate really were below zero, why did zero rates breed 12% zombies?** Stagnation needs no demography to explain it; it needs only a look at who was never liquidated.

The most awkward part comes last: the Austrian prescription — let rates return to market levels, let zombies fail, let liquidation happen — **is almost politically impossible.** Every zombie has employees, a creditor bank and a constituency; the gains from liquidation are dispersed, future and unseen. That follows directly from the public-choice logic of [[economics-of-state|Stage 8.4]], and [[common-mistakes|Stage 14.4]] discusses it candidly: Austrians know the prescription and cannot get it filled.

- **① Zombie firms: the BIS definition and the numbers**
- **② How ultra-low rates keep malinvestment on life support**
- **③ Debt overhang and the productivity drag: Banerjee and Hofmann's mechanisms**
- **④ Japan: the thirty-year prototype**
- **⑤ Secular stagnation: Summers first, then the Austrian reply**
- **⑥ Public-debt arithmetic, the temptation of inflation, and a prescription nobody will fill**

## @mechanics
### ① Zombie firms: the BIS definition and the numbers

In the September 2018 BIS Quarterly Review, Banerjee and Hofmann offered two definitions:

- **Broad**: a listed firm at least 10 years old whose **interest coverage ratio has been below 1 for three consecutive years** — its operating profit (before interest and tax) does not cover its interest.
- **Narrow**: the same, plus a low market valuation (Tobin's q below the industry median), meaning the market does not expect a turnaround either.

In numbers: a firm earns 800,000 before interest and tax, owes 1,000,000 in interest — coverage 0.8 — and stays there three years running: a zombie. The key is **not losses** (many young firms lose money with bright prospects) but **failing to earn even the interest, for three years**, which means the firm is neither growing nor exiting.

Their main findings (“about,” since numbers vary by definition and year):

- Among listed firms in 14 advanced economies, the broad zombie share rose from **about 2%** in the late 1980s to **about 12%** around 2016; the narrow share from about 1% to about 6%.
- After every recession (early 1990s, 2001, 2008) the share jumped and only partly fell back — **a staircase rising over time.**
- The zombie share is negatively correlated with interest rates: **lower rates, more zombies.** Their estimate attributes a substantial part of the rise to falling rates.
- The probability that a zombie **stays** a zombie rose from about 60% in the 1980s to about 85% recently — they are ever less likely to die and ever less likely to recover.

Another wave followed 2020: pandemic-era zero rates and government-guaranteed loans pushed the US zombie share, by some estimates, to about 15–20% (with wide variation by definition). The precise numbers are disputed; the **direction** is the same in every study.

### ② How ultra-low rates keep malinvestment on life support

Return to the logic of [[boom-malinvestment|Stage 5.2]] and [[bust-liquidation|Stage 5.3]]. In the boom, a depressed rate lured resources into long projects consumers were not willing to wait for (malinvestment); in the bust, those projects are exposed, lose money and are liquidated, and resources are reallocated. **The trigger for liquidation is interest**: a malinvestment is identified because its return fails to cover the cost of its funding.

Now watch what the interest rate does to that trigger. A project costs 100 and returns 4 a year:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{rate }6\%\text{:} &\quad \text{return } 4 < \text{cost of funds } 6 \;\rightarrow\; \text{loss} \;\rightarrow\; \text{liquidation} \\
\text{rate }3\%\text{:} &\quad \text{return } 4 > \text{cost of funds } 3 \;\rightarrow\; \text{“profit”} \;\rightarrow\; \text{survives} \\
\text{rate }1\%\text{:} &\quad \text{return } 4 \gg \text{cost of funds } 1 \;\rightarrow\; \text{“very profitable”} \;\rightarrow\; \text{expands}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Same project, same 4% return — only the rate changed, and the project went from doomed to “very profitable.”** That is the mechanism by which ultra-low rates keep malinvestment alive: they change no project's real return, only its accounting result. And society's real cost of funds — savers' time preference — did not actually fall to 1%; that 1% was manufactured by the central bank ([[natural-rate|Stage 3.5]], [[central-banks-fiat|Stage 4.5]]).

Three layers of consequence follow:

**First, what should be liquidated is not.** A 4% project lives on at 1% rates, occupying capital that could have gone to an 8% project.

**Second, new malinvestment keeps piling up.** A 1% rate rescues not only old projects; it also lets new 2% projects break ground — the boom mechanism of [[boom-malinvestment|Stage 5.2]] keeps running through the bust.

**Third, the exit channel is blocked.** In a healthy market failing firms close, assets sell at a discount, and new firms acquire plants and equipment cheaply — the entry point of creative destruction. When zombies do not exit, assets are not repriced, and entry costs more.

State the other side at full strength first: central banks cut rates in 2009 and 2020 to prevent **healthy** firms from failing in a temporary liquidity panic — a legitimate aim. The Austrian reply: short-term liquidity support and a decade of zero rates are different things. The former rescues good firms wrongly caught in a panic; the latter feeds bad firms that should have died; and the central bank has no way to tell them apart — the knowledge problem of [[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]] — so it rescues both.

### ③ Debt overhang and the productivity drag: Banerjee and Hofmann's mechanisms

The most valuable part of the BIS work is not the numbers but the **mechanisms** — how zombies drag down healthy firms. They identify three channels, each explicable by the profit-and-loss logic of [[profit-loss|Stage 6.3]].

**Channel one: locked-up resources.** Zombies hold capital, labor and land. The BIS finds zombies markedly less productive than non-zombies, with slower investment and employment growth. When zombies hold 10% of an industry's capital, that 10% produces far less than it could — **the most direct productivity drag.**

**Channel two: congestion.** To survive, zombies cut prices and compress margins. Healthy firms in the same industry face lower prices and thinner profits, so **their willingness to invest falls too.** The BIS estimates that a one-percentage-point rise in an industry's zombie share lowers healthy firms' investment rate by about one point and their employment growth by about 0.3 points (their estimates; orders of magnitude only).

**Channel three: credit misallocation.** To avoid recognizing bad loans, banks give new credit first to zombies (“evergreening”), and healthy new firms cannot borrow. This was especially visible in 1990s Japan: the zombie share of bank loan books rose while the share going to new firms fell.

Add a layer of **debt overhang**: a firm carrying too much debt will not invest even in good projects, because most of the new return goes to creditors rather than shareholders — a classic corporate-finance result (Myers 1977). Zero rates keep zombies alive but let their debt keep compounding; the overhang grows heavier and new investment ever less likely.

Put the three channels together and you get a phenomenon Austrians long predicted and the mainstream now measures: **the broad slowdown in productivity growth across advanced economies after 2008.** The mainstream offers many explanations (exhausted technology, mismeasurement, demography); the Austrian one is the simplest: **liquidation never happened, and the corpses of malinvestment are still inside the structure of production.**

### ④ Japan: the thirty-year prototype

Japan is the prototype for all of these mechanisms. [[case-japan|Stage 13.3]] is the full case; here is the skeleton.

- **1985–89 boom**: after the Plaza Accord the yen appreciated and the Bank of Japan cut the discount rate from 5% to 2.5% to offset it; credit poured into property and stocks. The Nikkei reached about 38,900 at the end of 1989; the grounds of the Imperial Palace in Tokyo were said to be worth more than all of California.
- **1990–92 bust**: the central bank raised rates; stocks fell about 60% and land prices later about 70%. Banks' collateral evaporated and bad loans piled up.
- **The 1990s: the zombie decade.** Banks did not recognize losses; they rolled over loans to failing firms — Caballero, Hoshi and Kashyap's 2008 paper, “Zombie Lending and Depressed Restructuring in Japan,” estimates that in the mid-1990s about 30% of listed Japanese firms were receiving some form of subsidized lending. Healthy firms' investment and hiring were measurably depressed.
- **Zero rates from 1999, quantitative easing from 2001** — Japan was the first country in the world to do either.
- **“Abenomics” from 2013**: more aggressive QE, negative rates (2016), yield-curve control. The Bank of Japan ended up holding roughly half of all Japanese government bonds.
- **Result**: thirty years of roughly flat nominal GDP; government debt about 250% of GDP; chronically weak productivity growth; only when inflation returned in 2022–23 did the Bank of Japan end negative rates, in 2024.

The lesson of Japan is not that the Japanese did something uniquely wrong, but that **every step was the least painful political choice available at the time** — not recognizing bad loans, not raising rates, not letting firms fail — and the sum of those choices was thirty years. That is exactly Mises's logic of intervention from [[intervention-logic|Stage 8.1]]: each intervention creates the problem that calls for the next, larger one.

### ⑤ Secular stagnation: Summers first, then the Austrian reply

In November 2013, at an IMF conference, Lawrence Summers revived Alvin Hansen's 1938 concept of **secular stagnation**. State it at full strength.

**Summers's argument**: over recent decades several structural forces have pushed down the “natural rate” (which he calls \(r^{*}\), the rate at which saving equals investment at full employment):
- **Demography**: advanced economies are aging; labor-force growth is slowing; investment demand (equipping new workers) falls.
- **A savings glut**: income has shifted toward high-saving rich households; emerging economies (China) have poured savings into advanced ones; retirement saving has risen.
- **Weaker investment demand**: new-economy firms (software, platforms) are capital-light and do not need factories; equipment has become relatively cheap.
- **Result**: the saving supply curve shifts right, the investment demand curve shifts left, and the equilibrium \(r^{*}\) falls below zero. But nominal rates have a floor at zero, so real rates cannot fall that far — saving exceeds investment, demand is deficient, and the economy runs persistently below potential.

**Summers's prescription**: if \(r^{*}\) is below zero, monetary policy is powerless; fiscal expansion is needed — government borrowing to invest in infrastructure and absorb the excess saving.

This is no straw man. It explains the three lows of the 2010s (growth, inflation, rates), and one of its corollaries — that the central bank is not pushing rates down but following \(r^{*}\) — directly rebuts the core Austrian charge.

**The Austrian reply** has four layers.

**First, \(r^{*}\) is unobservable, and its estimates are circular.** The mainstream estimates \(r^{*}\) with models (Laubach–Williams, for instance) whose inputs include real interest rates and the output gap — both of which are shaped by central-bank policy. Hold rates down for a decade and the model will “estimate” a low \(r^{*}\). As [[natural-rate|Stage 3.5]] put it, that is not measurement but fitting.

**Second, if \(r^{*}\) really were below zero, zombies should not exist.** \(r^{*}\) is the rate at which saving equals investment; if it truly lay below zero, every project surviving at zero rates would be one consumers were willing to wait for. But the BIS counted: at zero rates, 12% of listed firms could not even earn their interest. **A rate that genuinely reflected time preference would not breed 12% zombies.** The very existence of zombies is evidence that the market rate lies below the natural rate.

**Third, stagnation is made by policy, not demography.** Austrians see three man-made causes: (a) **unliquidated malinvestment** — everything above; (b) **regime uncertainty** (Robert Higgs's term) — the unpredictability of regulation, taxation and monetary policy after 2008 led entrepreneurs to defer long-term investment; (c) **financial repression** — zero rates are a hidden tax on savers, which reduces real saving rather than reflecting a glut of it, as Summers claims. Aging is real, but it does not explain why productivity growth slowed simultaneously in Japan, Europe and the United States, nor why, once rates returned to 4% in 2022, nobody spoke of “\(r^{*}\) below zero” any more.

**Fourth, Summers's prescription worsens the cause.** Fiscal expansion financed by central-bank bond purchases is more credit expansion, more Cantillon effects ([[cantillon-inflation|Stage 4.3]]), more resources steered to politically chosen projects. It treats the aggregate symptom, “deficient demand” ([[aggregates-limits|Stage 10.2]] showed that aggregates cannot see structure), while the disease is in the structure.

Candor is due here too: the Austrian empirical case in this debate is thin. “Unliquidated malinvestment drags productivity” is a statement of mechanism; the work of quantifying it has been done mostly by non-Austrian institutions like the BIS. [[empirical-method|Stage 13.1]] takes up how Austrians can do better empirical work.

### ⑥ Public-debt arithmetic, the temptation of inflation, and a prescription nobody will fill

Zombies are not only firms. Government is the largest candidate — its interest coverage depends on tax revenue and interest rates, and it has one option firms lack: **printing.**

Public-debt arithmetic fits on one line:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{annual change in debt/GDP} \approx (r - g) \times \dfrac{\text{debt}}{\GDP} - \dfrac{\text{primary surplus}}{\GDP} \\[6pt]
&r\text{: average interest rate on the debt;}\quad g\text{: nominal GDP growth}
\end{aligned}
$$

In numbers: debt is 100% of GDP, the rate 1%, nominal growth 4%, the primary deficit 3%. Annual change = (1% − 4%) × 100% + 3% = 0 — the ratio is stable. **Zero rates make 100% debt look sustainable.** Now let the rate return to 4%: change = (4% − 4%) × 100% + 3% = +3 points a year; if growth slows to 2%: +5 points a year. In ten years, debt is 150% of GDP.

That is why every highly indebted government after 2022 faces the same temptation: **rates cannot truly return to market levels**, because if they do the debt arithmetic explodes. Only three options exist: raise taxes (politically hard), cut spending (harder), or have the central bank keep rates down and tolerate inflation (easiest, because inflation is a hidden tax — [[cantillon-inflation|Stage 4.3]]). Historically the great majority of highly indebted governments chose the third — “financial repression,” low rates plus inflation, shrinking the real value of the debt. Through Austrian eyes this is zombie life support at the largest scale: the government refinancing itself with inflation.

**The Austrian prescription** is simple to state: let rates return to market levels; let zombies fail and their assets be sold at a discount to those who can use them; have governments cut spending rather than inflate; accept a year or two of painful liquidation in exchange for real growth afterward. [[bust-liquidation|Stage 5.3]] gave the example of the US depression of 1920–21: the government did almost nothing, and the downturn was over in eighteen months.

**Why it cannot be filled**: every zombie has particular employees, a particular creditor bank, a particular member of parliament; the gains from liquidation go to dispersed, not-yet-existing new firms and future consumers. The public-choice logic of [[economics-of-state|Stage 8.4]] says concentrated losses always beat dispersed gains. Austrians know this, which is why Austrian policy advice has almost never been fully implemented in a democracy — not a failure of the theory, but a real limit of it. [[common-mistakes|Stage 14.4]] discusses more frankly how a school that knows the prescription but cannot get it filled should conduct itself. [[hard-money|Stage 17.2]] considers another road — if money itself cannot be printed (hard money), where would the funding for zombie life support come from?

## @analogy
Think of an economy as a **forest**, firms as trees, and the interest rate as **the minimum height for reaching sunlight** — trees that do not grow tall enough die, freeing space and nutrients for new saplings.

In a healthy forest, some trees die every year and new ones grow. An old tree falling is ugly — but the patch of sky it clears is exactly what the saplings need.

Now the forest manager decides on “zero deaths”: he fits every tree that cannot reach the sun with an artificial lamp (zero-rate refinancing). No tree dies. The manager announces: the forest is remarkably stable — not one tree has fallen in years.

But a few years later the forest stops growing. The lamp-lit old trees hold the ground and the water and bear no fruit; saplings have no room; even healthy trees are shaded by the old canopy and slow down. The manager measures average yield and finds it falling year after year — so he installs more lamps (more QE), because if he switched them off now, too many trees would fall and the forest would “collapse.”

Japan is that forest thirty years on. Summers says: “The forest doesn't grow because the soil is old and the rain is scarce (demography, savings glut).” Austrians say: “Look at the lamps — if the soil really couldn't feed that many trees, those trees would have died long ago; the fact that they are alive proves the lamps are holding them up.”

The Austrian prescription is to switch off the lamps. The manager knows he should, and cannot: under every old tree stands someone who depends on its shade.

## @misconceptions
- **“A zombie firm is just a loss-making firm.”** — The BIS definition is interest coverage below 1 for three consecutive years, in a firm at least 10 years old. Many young firms lose money with bright prospects and high valuations; they are not zombies. The zombie's mark is neither growing nor exiting — unable to earn even its interest, kept alive by refinancing. The narrow definition also requires a low market valuation.
- **“Low rates are what the economy wants (\(r^{*}\) fell); the central bank merely follows, so zombies are not its doing.”** — That is Summers's argument and deserves to be taken seriously. But \(r^{*}\) is unobservable and its estimates use data already shaped by policy; more decisively, if the rate truly reflected time preference, zero rates would not leave 12% of listed firms unable to earn their interest. The existence of zombies is itself evidence that the market rate lies below the natural rate.
- **“No recession is good news; at least zombies preserve jobs.”** — The jobs preserved are those inside zombies; the jobs lost are those that healthy and new firms would have created. The BIS finds that in industries where the zombie share rises, healthy firms' investment and hiring fall. The seen jobs are kept, the unseen jobs vanish — Bastiat's broken window.
- **“Austrians want every firm to fail — cruel ‘liquidationism.’”** — Austrians distinguish two things: short-term liquidity support (rescuing good firms wrongly hit by a panic) and a decade of zero rates (feeding bad firms that should have died). They oppose the latter. The pain of liquidation is real but one-off; the cost of zombification is chronic low productivity — Japan has paid it for thirty years.
- **“If the Austrian prescription cannot be filled, the theory is useless.”** — Political infeasibility is not theoretical error; it means the theory's use lies in explanation and warning rather than adoption. Knowing why the forest has stopped growing at least keeps you from believing the “old soil” story, and shows you why highly indebted governments will choose inflation — which matters for understanding [[central-banks-fiat|Stage 4.5]] and [[hard-money|Stage 17.2]].
