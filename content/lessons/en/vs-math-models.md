# Austrians vs the Mainstream: What the Fight over Math, Equilibrium & Models Is Really About

## @hook
“Austrians are against mathematics” is the school's most popular label and its least accurate. What Mises and Hayek objected to was not equations but **what equations quietly assume away in order to be writable**: cardinal utility, continuous and differentiable preferences, equilibrium treated as a description of reality, a single “representative agent” standing in for millions of different people, a future distributed like the past (ergodicity), and time reduced to a subscript t. Each assumption is convenient; each erases a real phenomenon — entrepreneurial profit, discovery, cycles, genuine waiting. This lesson first makes the strongest case for mathematics (clarity, consistency, discipline, communication), then takes the Austrian objections one by one, and then says honestly that Austrians themselves use diagrams, verbal logic and, in a few cases, formal models. **The real disagreement is not “math or no math” but whether the things the standard model assumes away are exactly the things economics should be studying.**

## @intuition
Start with a standard introductory model. Demand is Q = 100 − 2P, supply is Q = 20 + 1.5P. Solve: \(P^{*}\) ≈ 22.9, \(Q^{*}\) ≈ 54.3. Draw two lines; the crossing is the equilibrium. Clear, precise, the same for whoever computes it.

Now ask a few questions an Austrian would ask.

**Who knows those two lines?** Nobody in the model “knows” them — they are just there. In reality the demand curve is the maximum willingness to pay in a hundred separate heads, and the supply curve is the minimum asking price in fifty others; **no one can see either line in full** (the knowledge problem of [[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]]). How does the price 22.9 get “found”? The model says: solve the equations. Reality says: someone tries 30 and sells nothing, drops to 25, the shop next door sells out at 20, raises to 24… **Prices are groped toward; groping takes time; and while it happens some people gain and some lose.** The model has compressed a whole film into a single photograph.

**What if everyone were identical?** Many macro models, to be solvable, assume the economy contains one “representative agent” who is every consumer and every producer at once. Convenient: one person's optimal choice is the whole economy's equilibrium. But in the “marginal pairs” of [[price-formation|Stage 1.3]], price comes precisely from **people being different** — the gap between the last pair that trades and the first that does not. If everyone is the same, there is no exchange (who trades with himself?), no marginal pairs, no entrepreneur (entrepreneurial profit comes from seeing what others have not seen — and when everyone is identical there is nothing “others haven't seen”).

**Where did time go?** The model has a t, but t is only a subscript: t = 1, t = 2, t = 3 … each period a complete equilibrium snapshot. Real time is not like that. A decision made at t = 1 may already be proven wrong at t = 2, with the capital sunk into it unrecoverable (the heterogeneous capital of [[heterogeneous-capital|Stage 3.4]]); at t = 2 you know things you did not know at t = 1; someone at t = 2 invents a product that did not exist at t = 1. **Real time brings novelty; model time merely repeats.**

Every Austrian criticism of mathematical models is a variation on those three questions: **Who knows? How many kinds of people? Does time bring anything new?** The standard model, to be writable and solvable, answers: everyone knows, one kind, no. Austrians hold that the three things assumed away — **dispersed knowledge, heterogeneous people, real time** — are exactly what economics should be studying. Entrepreneurship, the market process, the business cycle: all of them live inside those three things. Assume them away and what remains is not “a simplified economy” but “an economy with no economic problem.”

But do not pick a side yet. Mathematics has done a lot of good in economics: it forces you to write your assumptions down (prose can be vague; equations cannot); it lets you check the reasoning for gaps (step by step, an error is visible at once); it lets different schools argue on the same sheet of paper. The first part of this lesson makes that case seriously — **some Austrians themselves think “rejecting mathematics” overstates the position**, and their reasons deserve a hearing.

**In this lesson we break it into five pieces:**

- **① The strongest case for mathematics: clarity, consistency, discipline, communication**
- **② Six Austrian objections — each pointing at a phenomenon assumed away**
- **③ Mises's *Human Action* Chapter XVI and Hayek 1974: what exactly is being objected to**
- **④ What Austrians actually use: verbal logic, Garrison's diagrams, and limited formalization**
- **⑤ Where the real disagreement lies: not math, but the assumed-away time, heterogeneity, uncertainty and entrepreneur**

## @mechanics
### ① The strongest case for mathematics: clarity, consistency, discipline, communication

To refute a position, state its best version first. The defense of mathematical economics rests on four claims, every one of them true.

**Clarity.** Prose can hide. “Demand will fall somewhat” — by how much? Under what conditions? Depending on what? Write Q = f(P, Y, P_other) and you are forced to say which variables demand depends on and which it does not. Samuelson's *Foundations of Economic Analysis* (1947) rewrote the whole of economics in this spirit — not to show off, but to force everyone to put their assumptions on the table.

**Consistency.** A verbal argument two thousand words long may harbor a contradiction its author never notices. A system of equations either has a solution or it does not; it is either consistent or it is not. One value of general equilibrium theory (proposed by Walras in 1874, rigorously proved by Arrow and Debreu in 1954) is that it showed a price vector at which all markets clear simultaneously **exists** — something prose cannot prove, and without which “markets can coordinate” is a belief rather than a result.

**Discipline.** A model forces you to answer “and then what?” Prose can say “pushing the rate down misleads investment” and stop. The model presses on: misleads by how much? Through which channel? In which sectors? Even if you finally decide the model's answer is wrong, being pressed through the questions is valuable in itself.

**Communication.** Equations are a lingua franca. A Chinese economist and a Brazilian economist can argue over the same model without first spending an afternoon on “what do you mean by that sentence?” Austrians have long been marginal in the journals partly because they decline to use this common language — a real cost the school bears, which [[career-paths|Stage 14.5]] will discuss.

Put the four together and the mainstream defense reads: **mathematics is not content but grammar.** It does not choose your assumptions; it forces you to state them. If you object to an assumption, replace it — but do not on that account object to stating assumptions clearly.

That defense is strong. The Austrian reply is not to deny it but to point out that **some of the most important assumptions, once you try to write them clearly, cannot be written in at all.**

### ② Six Austrian objections — each pointing at a phenomenon assumed away

The Austrian objection is not “math is too hard” or “math is ugly.” It is six specific assumptions — assumptions the standard model must make in order to be written and solved — each of which erases a real phenomenon.

**(1) Cardinal utility.** To write a utility function U(x, y) and differentiate it, utility must be a number. But as [[diamonds-water|Stage 1.1]] explained, Menger–Mises utility is **ordinal**: you can say “survival ranks above cooking,” not “1.7 times as important.” Modern textbooks reply that “we only use ordinal utility; indifference curves need no cardinality.” But Rothbard's 1956 essay “Toward a Reconstruction of Utility and Welfare Economics” pointed out that **indifference is not a category of action at all**: action always reveals preference, never indifference (if you chose A, A ranked higher; if you did not choose, you were not choosing). And once risk enters (von Neumann–Morgenstern expected utility), cardinality walks back in through the side door. Phenomenon erased: **valuation is ranking, not measurement.**

**(2) Continuity and differentiability.** To take derivatives, functions must be smooth. But goods come in units: buying the 3rd refrigerator is not the same as buying the 2.7th; marginal utility is the **jump between the nth and the (n+1)th unit**, not the slope of a curve. Phenomenon erased: **discrete units and discrete choices.** This looks like the most technical objection, but it colors every step that follows.

**(3) Equilibrium as description.** Equilibrium — everyone's plans mutually compatible, nobody wanting to change — is a hugely useful thought tool; Mises's own “evenly rotating economy” is an equilibrium construct built to show reality by contrast ([[profit-loss|Stage 6.3]]). The trouble starts when **equilibrium is taken as a description of the actual economy**: DSGE models assume the economy is always in equilibrium (hit by a random shock, it jumps straight to the new one). But if the economy is always in equilibrium there is no “process of tending toward equilibrium” — and [[competition-process|Stage 6.2]]'s “competition is a process, not a state” says exactly that the real economic question is not what equilibrium looks like but **how people grope when they are not in it.** Phenomenon erased: **the market process itself.**

**(4) The representative agent.** To solve a macro model, assume every household is alike and every firm is alike, so that one person's choice is the whole economy. But exchange, marginal pairs, the division of labor and entrepreneurial profit all arise from people being **different**: different valuations, different knowledge, different expectations. Tellingly, the sharpest version of this criticism came from inside the mainstream: Alan Kirman's 1992 paper “Whom or What Does the Representative Individual Represent?” showed that the aggregate behavior of heterogeneous individuals in general **does not resemble** the behavior of any individual — the representative agent is not a simplification but an error. Phenomenon erased: **heterogeneity**, and every problem of exchange and coordination that flows from it.

**(5) Ergodicity.** For an econometric model to forecast the future from the past, it must assume the future's probability distribution matches the past's — “ergodicity,” in the statistician's term. But [[apriori-empirical|Stage 2.2]] already showed that people learn and expectations shift; that is how the Phillips curve vanished in the 1970s. The post-Keynesian Paul Davidson made this criticism most systematically, and Austrians agree entirely. Phenomenon erased: **uncertainty in the Knight–Mises sense** — the future is not a die whose odds can be computed but something not yet created ([[entrepreneur-alertness|Stage 6.1]]).

**(6) Time as a parameter.** In the model, t is a subscript; each period is a complete snapshot; the world at t = 2 contains nothing new relative to t = 1 except parameter values. O'Driscoll and Rizzo's *The Economics of Time and Ignorance* (1985) call this “Newtonian time” and contrast it with “real time,” in which action is **irreversible** (sunk capital cannot be recovered, [[heterogeneous-capital|Stage 3.4]]), **learning happens** (at t = 2 you know what you did not at t = 1), and **novelty appears** (inventions, new products, new preferences). Phenomenon erased: **irreversibility, learning and novelty** — which is to say, the place where cycles and creative destruction live.

### ③ Mises's *Human Action* Chapter XVI and Hayek 1974: what exactly is being objected to

Let the two men speak for themselves, so the label does not do the talking.

**Mises, *Human Action*, Chapter XVI §5, “Logical Catallactics versus Mathematical Catallactics.”** Mises sorts mathematical economists into three groups. The first are **statisticians** who gather price and quantity data and run regressions — economic history, says Mises, not economics ([[apriori-empirical|Stage 2.2]]). The second use equations to **describe states of equilibrium** (Walrasian simultaneous systems). Mises's objection is not that the equations are wrong but that **they tell you nothing you did not already know**: they describe what things would look like if everyone's plans were mutually compatible, but **say nothing about how such a state is reached or what happens between one equilibrium and the next** — and that in-between process is precisely what economics has to explain. In his blunt phrase (paraphrasing), the mathematical description of various equilibrium states is “mere play.” The third group tries to describe the **process itself** with differential equations — the worst of the three, in Mises's view, because it pretends acting people adjust like moving bodies with fixed “equations of motion,” when what acting people have is judgment, learning and change.

Note what Mises did **not** say: that logic and mathematics are incompatible; that the equilibrium concept is useless (his own evenly rotating economy is an equilibrium construct); that Austrians must refuse all formalization. What he said is: **the question equilibrium equations answer is not the economic question.**

**Hayek, 1974 Nobel lecture.** Hayek was gentler. He explicitly said he was **not** opposed to mathematics: the Walras–Pareto system of equations, even if it can never be solved, has value — it displays the **pattern** of interdependence. What he opposed was the leap from “we have written the equations” to “we can fill in the numbers and forecast”: filling in the numbers requires knowing the preferences and knowledge of millions, which are dispersed across countless minds and cannot be collected by anyone ([[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]]). His famous line: economists who insist on studying only what can be measured end up **“pretending to possess knowledge they do not have.”** So Hayek's position is: **mathematics may be used to display patterns; it may not be used to impersonate measurement.**

Together, the Austrian objection to mathematics fits in one sentence: **not an objection to form, but to the content that form steals in order to be solvable.**

### ④ What Austrians actually use: verbal logic, Garrison's diagrams, and limited formalization

If Austrians do not use the standard models, what do they use?

**Verbal deduction.** The nine hundred pages of *Human Action* contain no equations, yet the book is a tight deductive system: from axiom to categories to laws, step by step. The derivation-chain demo of [[action-axiom|Stage 2.1]] is a miniature of this method. Prose has the virtue of accommodating “judgment,” “expectation,” “discovery” — things that cannot be written as functions; its cost is that readers cannot easily check each step, which is why Austrian writing discipline ([[writing-argument|Stage 14.2]]) matters so much.

**Diagrams.** Austrians have never shunned diagrams. Hayek's triangles in *Prices and Production* (1931) ([[roundabout-production|Stage 3.2]]); Garrison's three-diagram model in *Time and Money* (2001), which joins the production-possibilities frontier, the loanable-funds market and the Hayekian triangle ([[garrison-macro|Stage 10.1]]) — these diagrams draw “structure” and “process,” which no aggregate equation can. They are not a “not-quite-mathematical” compromise; they are **the most formalization one can achieve while keeping heterogeneity and time.**

**Limited formalization.** Honestly: Austrians disagree among themselves here. Rothbard and Lachmann essentially refused; Mises accepted that equilibrium states can be expressed in equations but thought it pointless; Hayek accepted formal display of patterns. Some contemporary Austrian scholars go further: game theory for institutional evolution, agent-based models to simulate market processes among heterogeneous individuals, computational experiments on price discovery — arguing that **as long as the formalization retains heterogeneity, local knowledge and real time, it is not the mathematics Austrians object to.** Indeed the spirit of agent-based modeling is thoroughly Austrian: each agent knows only local information, acts by its own rules, and order emerges from interaction ([[spontaneous-order|Stage 7.4]]). The demo of this lesson is a minimal agent-based model.

**A cost to admit.** Refusing the lingua franca has real consequences: Austrian papers struggle to enter mainstream journals, Austrian students struggle to enter mainstream departments, and many correct insights were treated by the mainstream as nonexistent because they “weren't in a model,” until someone rediscovered them decades later with a model (information economics, search theory, heterogeneous-agent macro all, to varying degrees, repeat what Austrians had already said). [[career-paths|Stage 14.5]] discusses this professional reality.

### ⑤ Where the real disagreement lies: not math, but the assumed-away time, heterogeneity, uncertainty and entrepreneur

Gather the lesson together. The disagreement between Austrians and the mainstream is **not** “prose vs equations.” The real disagreement is a substantive question:

**Are the four things the standard model assumes away to be solvable — real time, heterogeneity, true uncertainty, the entrepreneur — exactly the things economics should be studying?**

The mainstream answer (in its strongest form): they matter, but start with a simple model and add them back gradually. And the mainstream really is adding them: search-friction models restored “finding a price takes time”; heterogeneous-agent New Keynesian models (HANK) restored differences among households; information economics restored “not everyone knows everything”; the Santa Fe complexity economics of Brian Arthur replaced equilibrium with evolution outright. Austrians should welcome these advances, and some Austrian scholars are part of them.

The Austrian answer: the problem is that **these four things are not “details to be added later” but the preconditions for an economic problem to exist at all.** Without heterogeneity there is no exchange; without dispersed knowledge, prices carry no information; without real time there is no capital structure; without uncertainty there is no entrepreneur. Start from a model that lacks all four and add them back one at a time, and what you get is not “a more realistic model” but **a model forced to describe a non-equilibrium process in the language of equilibrium** — like describing a film in the language of photographs: you can stack many of them, but “motion” itself is always between the frames.

A concrete test: **entrepreneurial profit.** In a model where everyone knows everything, everyone is alike and time is a subscript, entrepreneurial profit is strictly zero — not “small,” but **logically impossible**, because profit comes from “seeing what others have not seen,” and in the model there is nothing others have not seen. That is why the mainstream's “profit” is always a risk premium, a monopoly rent or a factor return — never entrepreneurial profit in the Kirzner–Mises sense ([[entrepreneur-alertness|Stages 6.1]], 6.3). The demo of this lesson lets you do this by hand: switch off “heterogeneity,” “imperfect information,” “real time” and “novelty” one at a time, and watch entrepreneurial profit, price groping, cycles and discovery disappear from the simulation one by one.

This disagreement runs through the rest of the course. [[garrison-macro|Stage 10.1]]'s Garrison model shows how precise Austrians can be while keeping time in the picture; [[vs-neoclassical|Stage 11.3]] confronts DSGE directly — it is the concentrated form of this lesson's six objections, and also mainstream macro's most powerful tool; and [[ai-central-planning|Stage 18.1]] asks whether, if AI can process oceans of heterogeneous data, dispersed knowledge can finally be written into a model and central planning made solvable. The answer is this lesson's answer: **what can be written into a model is knowledge that has already been articulated; the economic problem lives precisely in the part that has not.**

## @analogy
A subway map is a great map precisely because it **throws away** most of the information: real distances, the shape of streets, what is above ground. It keeps one thing — how the stations connect — so you can see at a glance how to change lines. Nobody complains that the subway map is “unrealistic.”

The problem arises in exactly one situation: **when someone uses distances on the subway map to estimate walking time.** Two stations adjacent on the map may be three kilometers apart on the ground; a place that needs two changes on the map may be a five-minute walk. The map is not wrong; the user has mistaken “what was thrown away for clarity” for “what does not exist.”

The standard economic model is a subway map. To be drawable, it discards real time, differences among people, knowledge nobody holds in full, products that do not exist yet. As a tool for seeing structure it is good — Austrians too use an equilibrium construct (the evenly rotating economy) to see where entrepreneurial profit comes from. The disagreement begins when someone **uses the subway map to estimate walking time**: forecasting next year's GDP from an equilibrium model, designing monetary policy around a representative agent, pricing the future off the past's distribution. The Austrian says: what you are estimating is exactly what the map threw away.

What, then, is the Austrian map? More like a hand-drawn terrain map: less tidy, useless for precise distances, but showing the slopes (time preference), the rivers (the capital structure), the fog banks (uncertainty) and the roads not yet built (where entrepreneurs are exploring). Each map has its uses. **The argument should not be “which is more map-like,” but “for the journey you are about to make, which information cannot be thrown away.”**

## @misconceptions
- **“Austrians oppose mathematics because Austrian economists can't do it.”** — Mises and Hayek were rigorously trained; Hayek said plainly that the Walrasian system has value for displaying patterns. What Austrians object to is the content equations assume away to be solvable (heterogeneity, real time, dispersed knowledge, uncertainty), not the form. Some contemporary Austrians use game theory and agent-based models.
- **“Equilibrium is a harmless simplification; just add frictions.”** — Austrians use equilibrium as a thought tool too (the evenly rotating economy). The problem is treating it as a description of reality: if the economy is always in equilibrium there is no process of tending toward it, and that process — groping, trial, discovery — is exactly where prices do their informational work. A friction term restores “friction,” not “process.”
- **“The representative agent is just a convenient average.”** — Kirman (1992) showed from inside the mainstream that the aggregate behavior of heterogeneous individuals generally resembles no individual's behavior. More fundamentally, exchange, marginal pairs and entrepreneurial profit all arise from people differing; make everyone identical and these phenomena become logically impossible, not merely approximated away.
- **“Austrians reject all formalization, so they can never produce testable theory.”** — Austrians use verbal deduction (*Human Action*), diagrams (Hayek's triangle, Garrison's three diagrams), and some use limited formalization. And “testable” is not the only standard: the consistency record of pattern predictions like ceilings-cause-shortages and credit-expansion-causes-malinvestment is excellent. The real cost Austrians must own is that refusing the lingua franca marginalizes them in journals and departments.
- **“The mainstream has already added heterogeneity, information and time back; the Austrian critique is obsolete.”** — The mainstream is genuinely advancing (search models, HANK, information economics, complexity economics) and Austrians should welcome it. But starting from a model without the four and adding them back one by one yields a non-equilibrium process described in equilibrium language; entrepreneurial profit in those models can still only be a risk premium or a rent, never the return to seeing what others have not seen.

   - [ ] Equations are a lingua franca across schools and countries (communication)
   > Clarity, consistency, discipline and communication are real virtues of mathematics that Austrians grant. “Measuring how much utility” is precisely the cardinal-utility assumption Austrians reject — utility is a ranking, not a measurement.

   > Mises called the mathematical description of equilibrium states “mere play,” yet he used an equilibrium construct himself (the evenly rotating economy).
