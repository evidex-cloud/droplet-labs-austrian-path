# Mises 1920: No Prices, No Economic Calculation

## @hook
In 1920 Ludwig von Mises raised a problem that has nothing to do with laziness, greed, or incentives: **without private ownership of the means of production there is no exchange of the means of production; without exchange there are no prices for them; and without prices there is no common unit in which to ask whether a railway should be built with steel or with aluminum.** A planner who is perfectly selfless, tireless, and equipped with unlimited computing power still faces thousands of heterogeneous capital goods and can only see that every plan is “technically feasible” — never which one is wasteful. This is the **economic calculation problem**: the opening move of the socialist calculation debate, and the key to understanding why a price is not merely a label.

## @intuition
Let us first set aside a common misunderstanding. Many people assume the Austrian case against central planning is “nobody works hard for the state.” That is a real problem, but it is **not** Mises's 1920 argument. Mises's argument is harsher: **even if everyone were a saint, a planned economy could not do the arithmetic.**

Picture yourself as the ideal planner — brilliant, selfless, in possession of the technical data on every factory and every ton of ore in the country. You must decide: the railway from city A to city B — steel rails or aluminum rails?

The engineers hand you two lists. Steel: 10,000 tons of steel, 500,000 labor-hours, 2,000 tons of coal. Aluminum: 4,000 tons of aluminum, 300,000 labor-hours, 40,000 MWh of electricity. Both are technically sound; trains will run on either. Now: **which one is “cheaper”?**

You will find yourself stuck. “10,000 tons of steel” and “4,000 tons of aluminum” cannot be compared directly. 500,000 labor-hours is more than 300,000, but does the labor saved cover the extra electricity? You need a **common unit** — some way to convert steel, aluminum, hours, coal, and megawatt-hours into the same thing, add each column up, and compare two totals.

In a market, that common unit is the **money price**. Steel at 600 per ton, labor at 20 per hour, coal at 100 per ton; aluminum at 2,400 per ton, electricity at 60 per MWh. So: steel plan = 6.0m + 10.0m + 0.2m = **16.2 million**; aluminum plan = 9.6m + 6.0m + 2.4m = **18.0 million**. Steel it is. One minute of arithmetic.

Where did those prices come from? Back to [[diamonds-water|Stage 1.1]]: a price is not an engineering datum. It is **the result of buying and selling among thousands of owners** — steelmakers, smelters, mine owners, power stations, workers — each with their own uses and their own valuations, bidding against each other. The price of steel is what every other would-be user of steel must be paid to let you have it — the social version of its **opportunity cost**. **A price can serve as a ruler precisely because it compresses everyone else's valuation of that ton of steel.**

Now remove private property. Every steel mill, smelter, mine, and power station belongs to “society.” Nobody **sells** steel to anybody, because the owner of the steel and the owner of the aluminum are the same person — you. No sales, no prices. No prices, and you are back in front of the engineers' two lists: **tons, hours, megawatt-hours**, each speaking its own language. You can judge “feasible.” You cannot judge “worthwhile.”

That is Mises's core argument, in four steps:

- Rational economic behavior requires comparing the **costs and benefits** of alternative plans;
- Comparison requires a **common unit** — money prices;
- Prices for the means of production can only come from **market exchange** of the means of production;
- And exchange requires **private ownership** — which is precisely what socialism abolishes.

Hence the last line of the 1920 essay: “**Socialism is the abolition of rational economy.**” Notice he did not say “somewhat less efficient.” He said **impossible** — not done badly, but not doable at all, because the question “which plan is less wasteful?” has lost its meaning.

The argument has an uncomfortable generalization: it is not only about the Soviet Union. **Any organization without access to external prices** goes blind in the same spot — government agencies, nonprofits, a firm so large it swallows its entire supply chain, and (as [[bigdata-planning|Stages 7.5]] and 18.1 will show) any computer, however powerful. That is why this is the first bead on the Stage 7 string: the next four lessons are all elaborations, rebuttals, and replies to this one.

**In this lesson we break it into five pieces:**

- **① The 1920 essay: the problem is arithmetic, not effort**
- **② The four-step argument: from action to property, no step optional**
- **③ Steel or aluminum: actually doing the sum that cannot be done**
- **④ Why the substitutes fail: labor-hours, calculation in kind, and “world prices”**
- **⑤ Generalizing: bureaus, nonprofits, “one big firm,” and computers**

## @mechanics
### ① The 1920 essay: the problem is arithmetic, not effort

In 1920 Mises published “Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth” (Die Wirtschaftsrechnung im sozialistischen Gemeinwesen) in the Archiv für Sozialwissenschaft und Sozialpolitik; two years later he expanded it into the book *Socialism: An Economic and Sociological Analysis* (1922). The timing matters. The Bolsheviks had held power for three years, and much of the European intelligentsia treated central planning as a mere organizational question — hadn't the wartime economies been planned?

Mises's entry point was not organization, supervision, or willingness to work. He **conceded** all of that to his opponents up front: assume the planners are selfless and diligent, possess all technical knowledge, and the people labor willingly. Then ask: **how would the planner know whether what he is doing is wasteful?**

To answer that, you must first admit something: production is not a multiple-choice question with one right answer. Building a railway, a bakery, or a power plant can each be done in dozens of technically feasible ways — different materials, machines, sequences, and durations. [[heterogeneous-capital|Stage 3.4]] established that capital goods are **heterogeneous**: a rolling mill cannot be used as a tractor, and a railway sleeper and a generator share no physical unit of measurement. Technical knowledge tells you “all of these combinations will run.” Technical knowledge **never tells you which combination wastes the least** — because “waste” is an economic concept: it means “you could have used these same resources to satisfy a more urgent want.”

In a market, that job is done by **monetary calculation**: convert every input to a price, sum, compare with the expected selling price of the output, and read off a profit or a loss ([[profit-loss|Stage 6.3]]). Profit says you moved resources from lower-valued to higher-valued uses; loss says the reverse. This is not an accountant's trick. It is **the only method a civilization has for compressing the valuations of hundreds of millions of people into a single number.**

Mises's conclusion: socialism is not a “less efficient method of calculation.” It is **no calculation at all**. “Where there is no market there is no price system, and where there is no price system there can be no economic calculation.”

**Link one: rational action requires comparison.** Start from the action axiom of [[action-axiom|Stage 2.1]]: to act is to employ scarce means in pursuit of ends, and “scarce” means that what is used here cannot be used there. To choose is to compare. For one person and one good, comparison is easy — the water-bucket staircase of [[diamonds-water|Stage 1.1]] is just a ranking in the mind. But production combines **dozens of heterogeneous inputs** into **dozens of heterogeneous outputs**. No mind can rank that unaided.

**Link two: comparison requires a common unit.** You cannot rank “10,000 tons of steel + 500,000 hours” against “4,000 tons of aluminum + 300,000 hours + 40,000 MWh” unless you **project both onto the same axis**. That axis is the money price. And not just any number will do: the number must **reflect the competing claims of every other use for those inputs**; otherwise it is a label, and adding labels means nothing.

**Link three: prices for the means of production come only from exchange of the means of production.** Consumer goods are the easy case — even under socialism, if people can swap their ration coupons, relative prices for consumer goods will emerge. Mises granted this. But how do **higher-order goods** — Menger's second- and third-order goods from [[diamonds-water|Stage 1.1]] — get their prices? In a market, through **imputation** upward from consumer-goods prices. Imputation, though, is not long division on paper; it is carried out by entrepreneurs **bidding against each other** for those producer goods ([[entrepreneur-alertness|Stage 6.1]]): whoever bids highest believes he can turn them into something more valuable. No bidding, no imputation, no producer-goods prices.

**Link four: exchange requires private ownership.** Buying and selling presuppose that buyer and seller are different people, each entitled to dispose of their own share. Socialism is *defined* by **collective ownership of the means of production** — every factory and mine has one owner. One owner does not haggle with himself. He can keep internal **books** (“I'll enter this steel at 600 a ton”), but where does 600 come from? Either it is arbitrary, or it is copied from outside (see ④).

The chain breaks at its source. That is why Mises insisted this is not a matter of degree — not “socialism calculates slowly or badly,” but **there is nothing to calculate.**

### ③ Steel or aluminum: actually doing the sum that cannot be done

Let us finish the example from the intuition section, and then do two things the market does every day and a planner cannot.

**With prices:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Steel plan} &= 10{,}000\ \text{t} \times 600 + 500{,}000\ \text{h} \times 20 \\
&\quad + 2{,}000\ \text{t coal} \times 100 = 16.2\ \text{million} \\[6pt]
\text{Aluminum plan} &= 4{,}000\ \text{t} \times 2{,}400 + 300{,}000\ \text{h} \times 20 \\
&\quad + 40{,}000\ \text{MWh} \times 60 = 18.0\ \text{million}
\end{aligned}
$$

Choose steel; save 1.8 million.

**First: scarcity shifts, prices re-rank.** Suppose a new hydro dam comes online in the west and electricity falls from 60 to 30. The aluminum plan is now 9.6 + 6.0 + 1.2 = **16.8 million** — steel still wins, but the gap shrinks to 0.6 million. Suppose further that a new smelting process drops aluminum to 1,800: aluminum plan = 7.2 + 6.0 + 2.4 = **15.6 million**, and now **aluminum wins**. Throughout that re-ranking you never needed to know where the dam is or what the new process does — **the prices compressed that knowledge into two numbers for you** (which is exactly where Hayek picks up in [[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]]).

**Second: put cost next to revenue.** Is the railway worth building at all? The market's answer: will the discounted future freight revenue cover 16.2 million (discounted at the interest rate of [[natural-rate|Stage 3.5]])? If the line is worth only 12 million, **neither plan should be built** — that steel, labor, and coal belong in more urgent uses.

Now take the prices away. You are left with: 10,000 t steel vs 4,000 t aluminum; 500,000 vs 300,000 hours; 2,000 t coal vs 40,000 MWh. What can you say?

- “The aluminum plan saves 200,000 hours.” — But it uses 4,000 t more aluminum and 40,000 MWh more electricity while using 10,000 t less steel and 2,000 t less coal. Net saving or net cost? **Unknown.**
- “The steel plan is more energy-efficient.” — Measured how? Coal's heat content or electricity's kilowatt-hours? They are different things to begin with, and the smelter's electricity is already inside the 40,000 MWh.
- “Should the line be built at all?” — Its “benefit” is transport capacity in ton-kilometers; its “cost” is steel, hours, and coal. **Ton-kilometers minus tons equals what?** Nothing meaningful.

The only move left is to ask an expert to “use his judgment.” But where did his judgment come from? If he worked in a market economy, his intuition is the **afterimage of market prices**; if he did not, his judgment is a guess without feedback — if he guesses wrong, no loss will tell him so ([[profit-loss|Stage 6.3]]).

Mises's wording was precise: the planner can perform **technical calculation** (can this pile of materials be turned into that bridge?) but not **economic calculation** (is the bridge worth this pile of materials?). The former is the engineer's job; the latter is the entrepreneur's — and the latter is what decides whether a society is rich or poor.

### ④ Why the substitutes fail: labor-hours, calculation in kind, and “world prices”

Mises anticipated several ways around prices and rejected each. They still resurface in the debate today.

**Labor-hour calculation.** The Marxian tradition proposed “socially necessary labor time” as the common unit: the cost of anything is the total hours needed to produce it. Three problems. First, **labor is heterogeneous** — an hour of a surgeon and an hour of a porter cannot simply be added; you need “conversion rates” between kinds of labor, and for those you need prices again. Second, **what about non-labor factors?** Land, mineral deposits, and time itself (a tree that takes five years versus fifty) are not labor but are genuinely scarce. Third, even a correct hour-total measures **how much went in**, not **how much the output is worth** — [[diamonds-water|Stage 1.1]] already made the point: a thousand hours carving a figurine nobody wants is worth zero.

**Calculation in kind (Naturalrechnung).** After the First World War, Otto Neurath proposed dropping the common unit altogether: count in physical units — tons of steel, hours, calories — and let the planner “weigh them together.” Mises's reply: “weigh them together” is precisely the problem. Without a common unit, your weighing is a shrug; with one, you are back to prices.

**Copying someone else's prices.** Mises himself noted in 1920 that a socialist state surrounded by a capitalist world could borrow **world-market prices** for its accounts. The Soviet Union reportedly did so for decades (accounts differ, hence “reportedly”): pricing imports and exports off international prices and using them as reference points internally; and, reportedly, a large gray economy of “fixers” (tolkachi) brokering inter-factory swaps, alongside black markets — all of which amounted to **rebuilding prices outside the official system**. That confirms Mises rather than refuting him: the plan limped along by living off markets elsewhere. Socialize the whole world, and there are no prices left to copy.

**“Let the computer solve the equations.”** Pareto and Enrico Barone (1908) showed that the socialist “optimum” can be written as a system of simultaneous equations. Oskar Lange said in 1967 that one could feed those equations into an electronic computer and have the solution in under a second. Mises's reply (which Hayek later developed at length): **the coefficients do not exist.** Prices are not natural constants waiting to be measured; they are **generated** when people trade. You cannot use “what the price would be if there were a market” as an input, because with no market there is no such number. [[lange-market-socialism|Stages 7.3]] and 7.5 take up that thread.

### ⑤ Generalizing: bureaus, nonprofits, “one big firm,” and computers

This is a “systems”-tier lesson because the argument does not stop at the Soviet Union.

**Government agencies and nonprofits.** In *Bureaucracy* (1944), Mises distinguished “profit management” from “bureaucratic management.” A public health department **buys** its inputs on the market — it knows doctors' salaries and drug prices — but its **output** has no price: nobody pays for “public health.” So it cannot answer “another million on vaccines or on health education?” It must decide by budget rules, superiors' instructions, and political pressure. That is not the officials' fault; it is structural: **if the output side has no price, prices on the input side cannot complete the calculation.** Nonprofits are similar — donors' valuations substitute in part, but donors are not the people using the service.

**“One big firm.”** In Chapter 9 of *Man, Economy, and State*, Rothbard ran the argument in reverse: **what if a single firm swallowed the whole economy?** Between its internal plants there would be no market exchange, only “transfer prices” — but transfer prices are meaningful only by reference to external market prices for comparable goods. When the external market disappears, transfer prices become arbitrary entries, and the firm's internal “cost accounting” loses its footing. Rothbard's conclusion: **the market itself sets an upper limit on firm size** — a firm must leave at least one layer of external market intact, or it becomes a miniature socialism, blind in the same spot. Peter Klein (1996) developed this into a theory of “economic calculation and the limits of organization.” It is also a deeper reason [[monopoly-question|Stage 6.4]] does not fear “big”: a firm that truly absorbed every external price would die of the calculation problem first.

**Any planner, however powerful.** This is the setup for [[bigdata-planning|Stages 7.5]] and 18.1. It is tempting to read Mises as saying “too much data to crunch.” He is not. His point is that **the numbers you would crunch are byproducts of exchange; forbid exchange and the numbers do not exist.** Hand the planner an infinitely fast computer and it still faces tons of steel and megawatt-hours of electricity, because nobody is bidding for steel or electricity. This is not quite the same as Hayek's “knowledge problem” in [[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]] — Austrians disagree among themselves about how the two relate, and the next lesson is fair to both sides — but on the one point that “a faster computer is not the answer,” the two lines run in the same direction.

A fair closing note. Mises's argument has a soft spot: he stated “impossible” in absolute terms, yet real socialist states did persist for decades — by copying prices, tolerating black markets, and wasting on a colossal scale. Critics such as Lange took that persistence as proof that “it obviously is possible.” The Austrian reply is that **existing is not calculating** — a system that survives on other people's prices and internal smuggling is evidence *for* the argument, and so, in the end, was its collapse. [[lange-market-socialism|Stage 7.3]] puts Lange's strongest version on the table and gives it a fair hearing.

## @analogy
Imagine you are the purchasing manager at a restaurant, and the owner gives you a strange assignment: **stock tonight's menu in the “cheapest” way — but you are not allowed to look at any price.**

The supplier's delivery list: 20 kg beef, 15 kg salmon, 200 g truffle, 50 kg potatoes, 8 hours of the chef's time. The chef says menu A uses beef and potatoes; menu B uses salmon and truffle. Either menu feeds the room. Which is “cheaper”?

Your instinct says: “Truffle is obviously expensive!” — Stop. **How do you know truffle is expensive?** Because you have lived in a market and your head stores the afterimage of its prices. Now erase that afterimage: 200 g of truffle versus 20 kg of beef, with no conversion between grams and kilograms; and the chef's 8 hours are used differently by each menu. You are left with “feasible” and no “cheaper.”

What is a price? It is **the competing claims of every other restaurant, every home cook, every food factory in the city on that piece of beef, compressed into one number.** A glance at “beef 80 per kg, truffle 30 per gram” is an indirect consultation with the whole city: they are telling you that 20 kg of beef (1,600) takes less away from other people's wants than 200 g of truffle (6,000).

The socialist restaurateur owns every cow, every truffle field, and every chef in the city. Nobody sells him anything, so nobody tells him how much these things matter to others. He can only “weigh it all together.” That is Mises's point: **it is not that he does not want to economize; it is that he has no ruler that measures what economizing means.** And [[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]] will show that even if you handed him a ruler, its markings would shift every day — that is Hayek's part of the story.

## @misconceptions
- **“Mises's point is that planned economies lack incentives — nobody works for the state.”** — That is a different (real but secondary) argument. Mises explicitly assumed selfless planners and diligent workers, then showed they **still cannot do the sums**: with no exchange of producer goods there are no producer-goods prices and hence no common unit. Solve the incentive problem and the calculation problem is untouched.
- **“The calculation problem is just too much data; a supercomputer fixes it.”** — The data are not “too much”; they are **absent**. Prices are byproducts of exchange; forbid exchange and there is nothing to input. A computer can process tons and hours, but it still faces a list of physical quantities with no common unit. [[bigdata-planning|Stages 7.5]] and 18.1 develop this.
- **“The USSR lasted seventy years, so calculation was obviously possible.”** — Existing is not calculating. The Soviet system reportedly leaned on world-market prices for its accounts and on a vast gray economy and black markets — all ways of rebuilding prices outside the official system. A regime parasitic on external prices confirms the argument rather than refuting it.
- **“Use labor-hours as the common unit and the problem is solved.”** — Three holes: labor is heterogeneous (a surgeon's hour is not a porter's hour, and the conversion rate needs a price); non-labor factors (land, minerals, time) have nowhere to go; and hours measure what went in, not what the output is worth — a thousand hours carving an unwanted figurine are worth zero.
- **“This argument is only about socialist states; it has nothing to do with us.”** — The opposite: any organization whose output has no price goes blind in the same place — government agencies, nonprofits, and a firm that swallows all its external markets (Rothbard's “one big firm”). It is a general tool for understanding bureaucracies and the boundaries of firms.

   - [x] Because its output (public health) has no price — the cost side is priced but the revenue side is not, so it cannot compare “more on vaccines vs more on health education”
   - [ ] Because officials are lazy
   > The core of *Bureaucracy* (1944): if the **output** has no market price, accurate input prices cannot yield a “worth it or not.” Bureaucratic management substitutes budget rules and directives — a structural fact, not a character flaw.
