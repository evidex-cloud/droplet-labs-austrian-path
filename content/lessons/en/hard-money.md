# Fixed Supply, Time Preference & 'Hard Money': The Case and the Critique

## @hook
The “hard money” argument is the most Austrian card in the Bitcoin supporter's hand: supply is capped forever, nobody can print more, so there are no Cantillon effects, no inflation tax, no artificially suppressed interest rate; people dare to save, time preference falls, civilization accumulates. That is the core narrative of Saifedean Ammous's *The Bitcoin Standard* (2018), and its raw materials are borrowed almost entirely from Mises, Rothbard and Hoppe. This lesson first states the case at full strength, then audits it with Austrian tools: **is time preference the cause of saving or its effect? Is stock-to-flow a description or a law? Is Mises's criterion for “sound money” price stability or political insulation? Did Mises and Hayek actually advocate a fixed supply?** The verdict: half of the hard-money case is solid Austrian monetary theory; the other half mistakes descriptions for laws. (Nothing here is investment advice.)

## @intuition
Recall two beads on the string. [[time-preference|Stage 3.1]] said interest comes from **time preference**: people value present satisfaction more than future satisfaction, so postponing consumption requires compensation, and that compensation is interest; the lower a society's time preference, the more it saves, the lower the natural rate, the faster capital accumulates. [[cantillon-inflation|Stage 4.3]] said that under fiat money new money does not fall evenly on everyone; it flows out from the “first stops” — the central bank, the banks, the government — so that early receivers buy at old prices while late receivers only see prices rise. That is the Cantillon effect: a hidden transfer from late to early, from poor to rich, from savers to borrowers.

String those two beads together and you have the skeleton of the hard-money argument:

**If the supply of money cannot be increased by anyone, the Cantillon chain has no starting point.** Nobody can stand at the “first stop.” Savers are not diluted, borrowers are not subsidized, governments cannot fund wars by printing ([[economics-of-state|Stage 8.4]]). Since saving is no longer punished, people save more; more saving means capital deepening, higher wages, and more room to plan for a distant future — **time preference falls**. Hoppe, in *Democracy: The God That Failed* (2001), defines the process of civilization as precisely a sustained fall in time preference; Ammous connects that line to money: the low interest rates, cathedrals and long-horizon investments of the classical gold era (roughly 1870–1914) versus the fast consumption, high debt and short-termism of the fiat era differ, he argues, in the “hardness” of the money.

Gold is history's hardest money: annual mining adds roughly 1.5–2% to the existing stock, and nobody can double it overnight. Bitcoin is harder than gold: **new supply declines on a fixed schedule to zero around 2140, and the supply curve is vertical** — no matter how high the price, no more can be dug. So, says the hard-money case, Bitcoin is “digital gold,” and more golden than gold.

Every step of that argument uses Austrian concepts, and each step, taken alone, holds. So where is the problem?

The problem is that **it treats several “descriptions” as “laws,” and draws several causal arrows backwards.** What this lesson does is what [[abct-critiques|Stage 5.5]] does to the critics of ABCT and [[common-mistakes|Stage 14.4]] does to Austrians' own mistakes: audit the school's most popular application with the school's own methodology ([[apriori-empirical|Stage 2.2]]). Five places specifically:

- Time preference is a **category** of action and the **cause** of saving — money's hardness can remove a penalty on saving, but it cannot “lower time preference” on anyone's behalf; conflating the two conflates incentives with preferences.
- “Higher stock-to-flow, higher price” is an **empirical regression**, not a law; Austrian methodology is precisely the refusal to read necessity out of statistical correlation.
- Mises's criterion for “sound money” is **political insulation** — money the government cannot manipulate — not price stability; so “Bitcoin is volatile” does not refute its soundness, but “Bitcoin's supply is fixed” does not make it sound money either.
- Under a fixed supply, **deflation** comes in two kinds: “growth deflation” from productivity, which Austrians do not fear, and “credit-contraction deflation” from bank runs, which they do. The hard-money case tells only the first story.
- **Neither Mises nor Hayek was a fixed-supply absolutist**: Mises wanted a gold standard (supply growing 1.5–2% a year); Hayek in 1976 wanted competitively issued private currencies of stable purchasing power. Both would admire Bitcoin's “no issuer”; neither would necessarily regard “21 million” as optimal.

Finally we look at something the hard-money case does not like to discuss: **who holds it.** If early holders hold a large share of the supply, then the process of “Bitcoinization” is itself a vast wealth transfer — not by printing, but by adoption. Is that a “Cantillon effect in reverse”? Austrian property theory ([[why-property|Stage 9.1]]) says it is legitimate gain from voluntary exchange, but an honest economist cannot pretend the distributional fact is not there.

**In this lesson we break it into six pieces:**

- **① The hard-money case at full strength: from Mises and Rothbard to Ammous**
- **② Stock-to-flow: a description or a law?**
- **③ The direction of causation: does hard money lower time preference, or do low-time-preference people choose hard money?**
- **④ Volatility vs soundness: Mises's criterion is political insulation, not price stability**
- **⑤ Two kinds of deflation, and why Mises and Hayek were not fixed-supply men**
- **⑥ The gold comparison and “who holds it”: Cantillon in reverse and an honest conclusion**

## @mechanics
### ① The hard-money case at full strength: from Mises and Rothbard to Ammous

By this course's rule ([[writing-argument|Stage 14.2]]), state the other side — here, a faction inside the Austrian School — at its strongest.

**Step one: the quality of money is not in its quantity.** Mises showed in *The Theory of Money and Credit* (1912) that any quantity of money suffices to perform money's functions: more money, a little less purchasing power per unit; less money, a little more. Increasing the supply does not make society richer; it only redistributes. Rothbard, in *What Has Government Done to Our Money?* (1963), turned that into a slogan: there is no such thing as “too little” money. So the **only** economic effect of issuing money is a Cantillon-style transfer ([[cantillon-inflation|Stage 4.3]]).

**Step two: the best money is the hardest to issue.** Menger said money grows out of the most saleable commodity ([[origin-of-money|Stage 4.1]]); a core component of “saleable” is **saleability across time** — accept it today and you can sell it next year at roughly the same ratio. Anything that can be issued at will lacks that property. Shells, glass beads and aluminium each served as money somewhere and each collapsed once outsiders could supply them cheaply in bulk. **Gold won because its stock-to-flow ratio was the highest**: above-ground stock (about 200,000 tonnes) relative to annual mining (about 3,000–3,500 tonnes) gives a ratio near 60, meaning that even a doubling of output adds only about 3% to the stock.

**Step three: hard money and time preference.** Hoppe (2001) extends time preference from the individual to civilization: the safer property is and the more predictable the future, the lower people's time preference and the more they save, educate and invest long-term; war, confiscation and inflation push time preference up. Ammous, in Chapter 5 of *The Bitcoin Standard* (2018), connects the line to money: fiat erodes savings, so people consume, borrow and chase short-term returns; hard money preserves savings, so people save, invest and plan for grandchildren. He cites the art, architecture and long-horizon capital projects of the gold era.

**Step four: Bitcoin is hard money's limiting case.** Stock-to-flow after the 2024 halving: about 160,000 new coins a year against a stock of about 19.7 million, a ratio around 120, already above gold and doubling every four years. More important still, **supply elasticity is zero**: a higher gold price stimulates mining (slowly); a higher Bitcoin price stimulates hashpower only, and the rate of issuance does not budge (the difficulty adjustment of [[bitcoin-regression|Stage 17.1]]). No issuer means no Cantillon first stop; no first stop means no inflation tax; no inflation tax means governments must live on taxes and honest borrowing ([[economics-of-state|Stage 8.4]]).

So far, steps one and two are standard Austrian monetary theory and step four is an accurate description of Bitcoin's mechanics. **The dispute is over step three, and over the promotion of step two's “description” into a “law.”**

### ② Stock-to-flow: a description or a law?

Stock-to-flow (S2F) is the quantitative shopfront of the hard-money case. In 2019 an anonymous analyst writing as PlanB published a model regressing Bitcoin's price on its S2F, claimed an extremely tight fit, and projected an average price of about $100,000 for the 2020–24 cycle and much more for the next. The price peaked near $69,000 in late 2021 and fell to about $16,000 in 2022; the model clearly failed for that cycle. In late 2024 the price crossed $100,000 — supporters said “merely late,” critics said “a model that may be three years late predicts nothing.”

The tool needed here is the methodology of [[apriori-empirical|Stage 2.2]], applied to one's own side.

**What S2F is.** A description: gold's S2F is high and its price high; silver's lower and its price lower; copper's very low, and it is nearly a pure consumption good. The pattern is real, and the Mengerian explanation is that **a high S2F is one necessary condition of saleability across time**, because a low S2F means supply can respond fast to price and wreck the store-of-value function.

**What S2F is not.** It is not a law that “S2F determines price,” for three reasons:

1. **It speaks only of supply.** Purchasing power is set by supply and demand together ([[money-demand|Stage 4.2]]). Something with infinite S2F — the one and only napkin I signed — has a price of zero, because nobody wants it. High S2F says only “supply will not drag”; demand is a separate matter, arising from millions of subjective valuations that no supply parameter can generate.
2. **Historical correlation is not a praxeological law.** The core Austrian claim is that economic laws come from the logic of action, not from data fitting; data can *illustrate* theory, not *establish* it ([[empirical-method|Stage 13.1]]). An Austrian who sneers at the goodness-of-fit of a DSGE model ([[vs-neoclassical|Stage 11.3]]) and then genuflects to a Bitcoin price regression is applying a double standard.
3. **It has a built-in mechanical problem.** Bitcoin's S2F is **known and certain** — everyone knows about the 2028 halving. If price were determined by S2F and S2F is fully anticipated, then the price path would be fully anticipated too — which contradicts the whole framework of entrepreneurial judgment and uncertainty ([[entrepreneur-alertness|Stage 6.1]]), and the ordinary observation that market prices embed expectations.

The correct statement is: **a high stock-to-flow ratio is a property of hard money that qualifies it as a candidate store of value; whether the candidate succeeds depends on demand.** Compressing that into “higher S2F, higher price” dresses the methodological error Austrians most oppose in Austrian clothing.

### ③ The direction of causation: does hard money lower time preference, or do low-time-preference people choose hard money?

This is the most important block, because it touches the core concept of [[time-preference|Stage 3.1]].

First, say precisely what time preference is. Mises, in Chapter XVIII of *Human Action*, treats time preference as a **category of action**: every action aims to reach its end sooner rather than later, otherwise nobody would act at all — they would postpone forever. So positive time preference is a priori; its **degree** varies by person and circumstance, a matter of psychology and environment. Saving is a **consequence** of time preference: the lower it is, the more willingly a person trades present consumption for more future consumption. Interest is a consequence too.

Now examine the hard-money sentence: “hard money **lowers** time preference.” There is a version of it that holds and a version that does not.

**The version that holds: hard money removes the penalty on saving.** Under fiat inflation of 8% a year, 100 units saved for a year retain 92 units of purchasing power; under hard money they retain 100 (perhaps 102 with growth deflation). What changes is **the return to saving** — the **price** the actor faces. At the same time preference, a higher return to saving means more saving — an ordinary marginal result. This version is entirely sound, and it is just the other face of the Cantillon effect in [[cantillon-inflation|Stage 4.3]]: inflation is a tax on money holders; abolish the tax and money holding rises.

**The version that does not hold: hard money changes people's preferences themselves.** That confuses incentives with preferences. A person who saves more when saving pays better has not changed his time preference; his optimal choice under an unchanged preference has changed. Just as driving less when petrol gets dearer does not make you “greener.” The hard-money narrative slides quietly from the first version to the second — “Bitcoin makes people patient,” “fiat corrupts” — and at that point it is no longer economics but moral storytelling.

**Worse is reverse causation.** Who put savings into an asset with 80% drawdowns in 2012 or 2016 and held on? People who already had low time preference and high risk tolerance. Observing that “Bitcoin holders are more patient” is very likely **self-selection**, not money reshaping people. In methodology this is a selection effect, familiar to anyone who does empirical work ([[empirical-method|Stage 13.1]]); Austrians are not exempt.

**Hoppe himself is more careful.** His argument is about **institutions** — the security of property, the extent of government predation — shaping the **environment** in which time preference forms; he never claimed that a particular money turns people into different people. Compressing Hoppe's civilization thesis into “buy Bitcoin = lower your time preference” turns an institutional claim into an advertisement for an asset.

In one sentence: **hard money can change the price of saving; it cannot change anyone's time preference for them. It removes a penalty; it does not dispense a virtue.** This lesson's demo refuses to compute any causal coefficient from “hardness” to “time preference,” and this is why.

### ④ Volatility vs soundness: Mises's criterion is political insulation, not price stability

The most common objection to hard money is “it is too volatile”; the most common reply is “volatility is the cost of early adoption.” Neither lands, because neither first says what **“sound money” means.**

In Part IV of the 1953 edition of *The Theory of Money and Credit*, “Monetary Reconstruction,” Mises writes that the sound-money principle has two aspects: **affirmatively, it approves the market's choice of a commonly used medium of exchange; negatively, it obstructs the government's propensity to meddle with the currency system.** He adds that its purpose is the protection of civil liberties against despotic inroads — it is a **political** principle, and has nothing to do with “price stability.” Mises in fact explicitly rejected schemes to “stabilize” money by index number (Fisher's plan), holding that “stability” is an illusion: there is no “price level” to stabilize, only innumerable individual prices in motion ([[aggregates-limits|Stage 10.2]]).

Measure Bitcoin by that yardstick and the result is split:

- **On the negative aspect, Bitcoin qualifies more completely than any money in history**: no government can issue more of it, no central bank can suppress its interest rate. In that respect its “soundness” is untouched by volatility — volatility is a change in market valuation, not the result of political manipulation.
- **On the affirmative aspect, Bitcoin does not yet qualify**: it is not the market's *generally* chosen medium of exchange ([[bitcoin-regression|Stage 17.1]], block ④). Mises's sound money is not “the thing with fixed supply” but “the thing the market chose”; [[stablecoins-cbdc|Stage 17.3]] shows that what the market has chosen so far in the currency competition is **the dollar** (in stablecoin form), not gold and not Bitcoin.

So the correct formulation is: **Bitcoin is the soundest candidate on the “political insulation” dimension, but the other half of sound money — general adoption by the market — has not happened. Volatility is not a refutation of its soundness; it is a refutation of its money-ness.** The two get muddled constantly.

There is a deeper point: **hardness is not stability.** Purchasing power is where supply meets demand. A fixed supply means **every fluctuation in demand lands 100% on the price** — gold at least has a faint supply response (more mining when the price rises) as a cushion; Bitcoin has not even that. So in pure theory, a money with perfectly fixed supply must have a *more* volatile purchasing power than a money whose supply responds to price, other things equal. Hard-money writers treat “hard” and “stable” as synonyms; they are not — and this is exactly why Hayek in 1976 did **not** want a fixed supply, as the next block shows.

### ⑤ Two kinds of deflation, and why Mises and Hayek were not fixed-supply men

**The deflation debate.** Suppose a fixed-supply money became society's money and the economy grew 3% a year; prices would fall roughly 3% a year. Is that bad? Steelman the mainstream worry first:

- **Fisher's debt deflation (1933)**: falling prices raise the real burden of existing debt; debtors default, dump assets, push prices lower, and the spiral continues.
- **Keynes's sticky wages**: nominal wages resist cuts, so falling prices raise real wages and unemployment.
- **The zero lower bound**: expected deflation gives cash a positive real return the central bank cannot push below zero — which for Austrians is a feature, not a bug.

The Austrian reply begins with a distinction (Selgin, *Less Than Zero*, 1997; Hülsmann, *Deflation and Liberty*, 2008): **growth deflation** is price decline from rising productivity — computers and televisions have experienced it for thirty years and nobody stopped buying computers; it is **anticipated, gradual, and matched by real wealth growth**. It produces no debt spiral, because both parties price it into nominal interest rates when they sign (just as they price in expected inflation). **Credit-contraction deflation** is different: the sudden shrinkage of money substitutes when the fractional-reserve system of [[fractional-reserve|Stage 4.4]] suffers runs — fast, unanticipated, accompanied by bank failures. The US money supply shrank by about a third in 1930–33; that was the second kind. **The hard-money story tells only the first kind, but the second kind can happen under a Bitcoin standard too** — as long as fractional-reserve custodians exist (the subject of [[bitcoin-cycles|Stage 17.5]]).

Austrians disagree among themselves here as well: Hayek in the 1930s feared that “secondary deflation” would turn recession into depression and admitted late in life that his earlier tolerance of deflation had been a mistake; Rothbard held that deflation is part of liquidation ([[bust-liquidation|Stage 5.3]]). [[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]] has more on that internal argument.

**Is fixed supply an Austrian doctrine?** Not the masters':

- **Mises** wanted a **gold standard** — he advocated gold his whole life, and gold grows 1.5–2% a year. He never proposed a money of absolutely fixed quantity; he proposed money the government cannot touch.
- **Hayek (1976)**, in *Denationalisation of Money*, wanted **competitive private issue**, and explicitly expected issuers to keep their currencies **stable in purchasing power** against a commodity basket, because that is what the public would prefer — he thought supply should **respond** to demand, not be fixed. Hayek would have liked Bitcoin's denationalisation; his ideal money is not Bitcoin.
- **Rothbard** comes closest to fixed supply: any quantity of money is optimal, and under 100% gold reserves the supply should grow only with mining. His position is the most compatible with the hard-money case.
- **Selgin** (the free-banking wing, [[free-banking|Stage 9.4]]) argues for a “productivity norm”: stable nominal spending, with prices falling as productivity rises.

So “fixed supply is the best monetary rule” is the view of **one wing** of the school, not its consensus. Presenting it as Mises–Hayek orthodoxy is a small historical revision in the hard-money narrative.

### ⑥ The gold comparison and “who holds it”: Cantillon in reverse and an honest conclusion

**Against gold.** [[central-banks-fiat|Stage 4.5]] covered how the gold standard worked. Set the two side by side:

- **Verification cost**: a gold bar needs instruments; a bitcoin needs a computer and a few seconds. Bitcoin wins.
- **Custody cost**: gold must be stored somewhere, historically in banks, which produced paper gold and fractional reserves ([[fractional-reserve|Stage 4.4]]); Bitcoin can be self-custodied, but most people leave it on exchanges — Mt. Gox in 2014 and FTX in 2022 proved that custody risk has not gone away ([[bitcoin-cycles|Stage 17.5]]). A draw.
- **Supply elasticity**: gold weakly positive, Bitcoin zero. On “resistance to issuance” Bitcoin wins; on “purchasing-power stability” gold edges it.
- **History**: gold has five thousand years of price memory, Bitcoin a decade and a half. Both chains connect under the regression theorem ([[bitcoin-regression|Stage 17.1]]), but the length of the chain shapes confidence that “I can still sell it next year.” Gold wins.
- **Existential dependencies**: gold needs no internet, no miners, no unbroken protocol. Gold wins.

The conclusion is not “which is better” but **that they are not two versions of the same thing**: gold is a commodity whose money-ness came from physical scarcity and historical inertia; Bitcoin is a protocol competing for money-ness through mathematical scarcity and network effects ([[network-effects|Stage 15.1]]).

**Who holds it.** The hard-money case promises “no Cantillon effect” — nobody can stand beside the printing press. In the sense of **issuance**, that is true. But there is another transfer: if an asset goes from zero to world money, the wealth early holders gain equals the “entry fee” everyone else pays to adopt it. Estimates vary, but it is safe to say a substantial share is held by early miners, early exchanges and a small number of addresses (addresses are not people, many are exchanges; no precise figure is needed). Some call this a “Cantillon effect in reverse” — not the printer getting new money first, but the far-sighted getting old money first.

Austrians have two replies, and both must be given. First, that wealth came from voluntary exchange and risk-bearing, which is **morally and legally** unlike Cantillon gains obtained through state coercion (the property logic of [[why-property|Stage 9.1]]; the entrepreneurial judgment of [[entrepreneur-alertness|Stage 6.1]] — early holders bore the risk of it going to zero). Second, **economics cannot pretend the distributional question does not exist**: for a money to be generally adopted, millions must be willing to trade their labor for it; if they perceive that as a gift to a few anonymous early holders, adoption meets political and psychological resistance. That is not a refutation of the hard-money case; it is a real-world constraint on its “inevitable victory” narrative.

**The honest conclusion:**

- The **monetary-theory part** of the hard-money case (issuance only redistributes; Cantillon effects; money the government cannot touch is the soundest) is solid Mises–Rothbard tradition.
- Its **time-preference part** swaps “removing a penalty on saving” for “changing people's preferences” and ignores reverse causation.
- Its **S2F part** treats a description as a law, violating Austrian methodology.
- Its **stability promise** confuses “hard” with “stable”: fixed supply means demand swings land entirely on price.
- Its **historical narrative** drafts Mises and Hayek into the fixed-supply camp, where neither belongs.

[[bitcoin-cycles|Stage 17.5]] continues the thread: even under a Bitcoin standard, fractional-reserve custodians can still generate cycles. [[ai-bubble-abct|Stage 18.5]] applies the same “steelman, then audit” method to AI capital spending. This lesson and all of [[bitcoin-regression|Stage 17]] are not investment advice — theory can tell you the **properties** of a monetary rule, never the **price** of any asset.

## @analogy
Think of money as a **reservoir**, with purchasing power as the water level.

The fiat reservoir's inlet valve is controlled by a committee; some years it lets in 7%, in 2020 it let in 25%. The level falls year after year (purchasing power declines), and the people standing next to the valve always scoop first — that is the Cantillon effect. The gold reservoir's inlet is a mountain spring that adds 1.5–2% a year, and nobody can open or close it. The Bitcoin reservoir's inlet is a pipe that narrows on a schedule until it shuts entirely.

The hard-money case says: weld the valve shut and the level will be steady. **That is half right.** The level depends not only on inflow but on **outflow** — that is, demand. A reservoir with a welded inlet, in a drought (everyone wants to hold), rises violently; in a storm (everyone wants out), falls violently; and there is **no cushion at all**. The gold reservoir at least has a spring that trickles a little more in dry seasons. So “the hardest reservoir” is precisely “the reservoir whose level swings most” — hard and stable are not the same thing.

Now time preference. The hard-money case says: once the level stops falling every year, people store more water and become “patient.” The first half is right: nobody is siphoning their water any more, storing pays better, so they store more. The second half is wrong: they store more because **storing became worthwhile**, not because they turned into more patient people — as driving less when petrol is dear does not make you love the planet. And the people who stored water in a brand-new reservoir still in trial operation were the most patient sort to begin with; the reservoir did not make them so.

Finally: the first water in this new reservoir was collected by a few early people at almost no cost. If the whole world switches to it, they become very rich. It is not stolen — they bore the risk that the reservoir would run dry — but it is a fact one cannot pretend not to see.

## @misconceptions
- **“A money with fixed supply has the most stable purchasing power.”** — Confuses “hard” with “stable.” Purchasing power is set by supply and demand together; a fixed supply means every swing in demand lands entirely on the price, whereas a money with elastic supply (gold) has at least a faint cushion. Hayek in 1976 rejected fixed supply for exactly this reason and proposed competing currencies of stable purchasing power.
- **“Hard money lowers time preference, so Bitcoin makes people more patient and civilized.”** — Time preference is the cause of saving, not an effect of money. Hard money removes the penalty on saving (changing its return) and so increases saving at a given preference; it does not change the preference. Observing that “holders are more patient” is very likely self-selection: only patient people hold a volatile asset for years. Hoppe's civilization thesis concerns institutions, not any particular asset.
- **“Stock-to-flow determines price, so the price must rise after each halving.”** — S2F is a description (a high ratio is a necessary condition for a store-of-value candidate), not a law. It speaks only of supply, not demand; treating a statistical correlation as necessity is what Austrian methodology ([[apriori-empirical|Stage 2.2]]) opposes; and since S2F is fully anticipated, if it set the price, the price path would be fully anticipated too, contradicting entrepreneurial judgment and uncertainty. The PlanB model's failure in the 2021–22 cycle is a reminder.
- **“Mises and Hayek advocated a money of fixed supply.”** — Mises advocated a gold standard (gold grows about 1.5–2% a year), the essence being money the government cannot touch; Hayek in 1976 advocated competitive private issue with stable purchasing power and supply responding to demand. Rothbard comes closest to fixed supply. “Fixed supply is optimal” is the view of one wing, not the school's consensus.
- **“Bitcoin has no Cantillon effect, so its adoption raises no distributional issue.”** — There is indeed no issuance-based Cantillon effect. But when an asset goes from zero to general adoption, early holders' gains equal everyone else's entry fee. It is legitimate gain from voluntary exchange and risk-bearing ([[why-property|Stages 9.1]], 6.1), unlike the coerced inflation transfer — but an honest economist cannot pretend the distributional fact away; it is one of the real constraints on adoption.

## @quiz
1. In Mises's 1953 formulation, what is the negative aspect of the sound-money principle?
   - [ ] The purchasing power of money must be kept stable
   - [ ] The money supply must be fixed
   - [x] Obstructing government interference with and manipulation of the monetary system
   - [ ] Money must consist of gold
   > Mises: the affirmative aspect approves the market's choice of medium of exchange; the negative aspect obstructs government meddling. It is a principle of political insulation, not price stability — Mises explicitly rejected index-number “stabilization.”

2. Which reading of “hard money lowers time preference” holds within Austrian theory?
   - [ ] Hard money changes people's preferences, making them innately more patient
   - [x] Hard money removes inflation's penalty on saving, so at a given time preference the return to saving is higher and saving rises
   - [ ] Hard money abolishes interest, so people no longer need time preference
   - [ ] Hard money forces people to save by law
   > Time preference is a category of action and the cause of saving; hard money changes the “price” (return) saving faces, not the preference itself. Mistaking incentives for preferences is the hard-money case's most common slide.

3. Why is Austrian methodology wary of the stock-to-flow model?
   - [ ] Because Austrians think the money supply is unimportant
   - [x] Because it is a data-fitted correlation, speaks only of supply and not demand, and makes a fully anticipated variable the determinant of price
   - [ ] Because its price forecasts are too low
   - [ ] Because an anonymous analyst proposed it
   > [[apriori-empirical|Stage 2.2]]: economic laws come from the logic of action, not regression lines. S2F is a descriptive property of hard money, not a pricing law; purchasing power is set by supply and demand.

4. Which statement about deflation matches the Austrian distinction?
   - [ ] All deflation is a disaster and must be avoided by printing
   - [ ] All deflation is good, including money contraction caused by bank runs
   - [x] Growth deflation (productivity-driven, anticipated) is harmless; credit-contraction deflation (sudden shrinkage of a fractional-reserve system, unanticipated) is harmful
   - [ ] Credit-contraction deflation is impossible under a fixed-supply money
   > Selgin, Hülsmann and others distinguish the two kinds; under a Bitcoin standard the second can still occur as long as fractional-reserve custodians exist — the subject of [[bitcoin-cycles|Stage 17.5]].

5. Comparing Bitcoin with gold, which is accurate?
   - [ ] Both have zero supply elasticity
   - [x] Bitcoin's verification cost is far lower than gold's, but zero supply elasticity means demand swings land entirely on price, and its existence depends on miners and the network
   - [ ] Neither gold nor Bitcoin carries custody risk
   - [ ] Gold satisfies the regression theorem and Bitcoin does not
   > They are not two versions of one thing: gold relies on physical scarcity and five thousand years of history, Bitcoin on mathematical scarcity and network effects; each wins on some dimensions, see the item-by-item comparison in ⑥.
