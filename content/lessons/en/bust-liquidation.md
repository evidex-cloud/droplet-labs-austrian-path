# The Bust: Liquidation Is the Cure, Not the Disease

## @hook
Everyone sees sickness in a bust: bankruptcies, layoffs, falling prices. Austrians see **a fever breaking**. The boom pushed capital and people into places consumers were never willing to wait for; the bust is the market **repricing and redeploying** them back toward what consumers actually want. It has to happen — not because Austrians are cold-hearted, but because a half-dug mine does not finish itself just because you refuse to call it half-dug. Rothbard's *America's Great Depression* (1963) lists six ways governments stretch a liquidation into a decade; Hayek's disagreement with Röpke and Lachmann over “secondary deflation,” and Hayek's own later regret about his 1930s advice, are told here without varnish. And the hardest question — “but people are suffering” — gets a straight answer.

## @intuition
You have a fever. 39.5°C, aching all over, no energy for anything.

Is the fever the disease? Your doctor will tell you: no. The fever is your immune system **working** — raising temperature to kill the pathogen. The disease is the infection; the fever is the body's way of fighting it. If you take a huge dose of antipyretics and force your temperature down to 36.5°C, you will feel better for a while, but the infection is still there and you have disarmed your immune system.

That is how Austrians see a bust. **The disease is the boom** — the long projects of [[boom-malinvestment|Stage 5.2]] started on a false rate, the people dragged into the wrong industries, the capital paid out as if it were income. **The bust is the immune response**: the market discovers these projects do not pencil out and stops transfusing them; their assets are sold cheap and put to other uses; their workers are released to find jobs consumers will actually pay for. This process is called **liquidation** — not “destruction,” but “repricing plus redeployment.”

Why must it happen? Because malinvestment is a **physical fact**, not a bookkeeping entry. A mine dug halfway that nobody will ever take over at a 5% rate is, simply, a mine dug halfway. You can have the central bank cut rates again, have the government subsidize it, have the bank pretend the loan is still good — but you cannot make consumers genuinely willing to wait 8 years for that mine. The resources either rot in the mine or get moved to making what consumers want. The latter is liquidation. **Liquidation does not create the loss; the loss happened in the boom. Liquidation merely admits it.**

Then why does a bust hurt so much? Because admitting a loss hurts. But the core Austrian claim is this: **how long and how deep the pain runs depends largely on whether you obstruct the liquidation.** In 1920–21 the United States went through an extremely sharp depression — wholesale prices fell by roughly a third in little over a year, and unemployment, by various estimates, ran somewhere between about 5% and 12% (1920s statistics are rough) — and the government did almost nothing (the Harding administration actually cut spending), while the Fed did not rescue anyone. Within about 18 months the economy was recovering strongly, into the Roaring Twenties. After 1929, by contrast, Hoover and Roosevelt did nearly everything that counts as “helping” — propped up wages, propped up prices, bailed out firms, launched public works, restricted imports — and the depression lasted more than a decade. Rothbard's *America's Great Depression* is one long explanation of that contrast ([[case-great-depression|Stage 13.2]] does the full case).

Of course, all of this must face an honest objection: “People are suffering — how can you say ‘do nothing’?” The end of this lesson answers it head-on, and the heart of the answer is **time**: every quarter of “help” pushes the loss into the next quarter, and manufactures fresh malinvestment on the way.

To be candid, Austrians themselves disagree about the bust. In the 1930s Hayek argued for letting liquidation run its course; Röpke and Lachmann pointed out that liquidation can spin out of control into a “secondary deflation” — money demand spikes, healthy firms get dragged down too — and at that point it is no longer cure but complication. Hayek late in life admitted he had underestimated this. This lesson lays out that internal argument without covering for anyone.

- **⑤ Zombie firms: what is not allowed to die will not live either**
- **⑥ “But people are suffering”: the time argument and 1920–21**

## @mechanics
### ① Liquidation does three things: reprice, redeploy, relearn

“Liquidation” sounds like a courtroom bankruptcy procedure, but in Austrian usage it is a phase of the market process ([[competition-process|Stage 6.2]]) that does three things:

**Repricing.** Boom-time asset prices were computed at a 2% rate: the office tower discounted over 30 years of rent, the startup discounted from profits ten years out, the mine discounted on a copper price that never falls. When the rate goes back to 5%, every one of those discountings has to be redone. A tower valued at 100 may be worth 60; a company valued at a billion may be worth 200 million. **Falling prices are not the market “failing”; they are the market resetting the discount rate to genuine time preference.** The post-fall price is the true price — it tells the next buyer what the tower is worth under actual saving.

**Redeployment.** Once assets have true prices, someone buys them at those prices and puts them to a different use. The half-finished mall becomes a warehouse; the mine's equipment is stripped and sold to another mine; the startup's engineers go to a consumer-goods firm. This is Lachmann's **capital regrouping** from [[heterogeneous-capital|Stage 3.4]]: heterogeneous capital re-assembled by entrepreneurs. It takes time, because the best new use of every capital good has to be discovered by someone (the alertness of [[entrepreneur-alertness|Stage 6.1]]).

**Relearning.** Entrepreneurs, bankers and workers update their expectations during the bust: which projects pencil out at a true rate, which skills are wanted, which loans go bad. This is a correction of knowledge — prices transmitting knowledge again, in Hayek's sense ([[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]]).

Add the three together and you have the erasing of the red on [[boom-malinvestment|Stage 5.2]]'s “malinvestment map”: each project is either completed (if real saving suffices), transferred to someone else at a true price, or scrapped.

### ② Why there is no way around it: malinvestment is a physical fact

Someone will ask: can't we skip the liquidation — have the central bank cut once more and “revive” those projects?

Back to the numbers. The economy earns 1,000; saving is 100, consumption 900. The boom started 200 units of projects, of which 100 can never be completed. Now the central bank pushes the rate down again and releases another 100 of credit. What happens?

- The original 100 units of half-finished projects may get life-support funding — but consumers still consume 900 and real saving is still 100. **The resources needed to finish them have not appeared.**
- The new 100 of credit also attracts **new** long projects — now 300 units of projects are fighting over 100 units of saving.
- Factor prices rise another round, consumer prices rise another round, and then the rate rebounds again — this time with 200 units to liquidate.

This is what Mises meant by “the boom can only be sustained by ever-larger doses of credit expansion.” Every “rescue” postpones the liquidation and enlarges what must be liquidated. There are only two endpoints: a round in which the central bank no longer dares to expand (the bust), or a public that no longer trusts the money (the crack-up boom, [[cantillon-inflation|Stage 4.3]]).

Put it even more physically: finishing a half-dug mine takes five years of engineers, steel and electricity. Those resources are right now making the phones and bread consumers want. For the mine to be completed, people would have to eat less bread and upgrade fewer phones **now** — that is, genuinely save more. And “save more” is exactly what they were unwilling to do from the start. So the only options are: **reprice the mine at its true value (possibly scrap)**, or **force consumers to consume less** — and the latter is inflation, the forced saving of [[abct-one-picture|Stage 5.1]], which is itself another round of forgery.

### ③ Rothbard's list: six ways governments stretch a liquidation into a decade

At the end of Chapter 1 of *America's Great Depression*, Rothbard lists the typical ways governments “help” in a depression and why each one prolongs it. Organized into six items:

- **① Prevent or delay liquidation** — lend to shaky firms, tell banks to roll over loans, bail out institutions “too big to fail.” Effect: malinvestments are not repriced, capital and people stay locked in the wrong places, and healthy firms cannot get the funds that should have flowed to them.
- **② Inflate further** — cut rates again, lend again, quantitative easing. Effect: see the arithmetic above — the liquidation is postponed and the next round enlarged, while fresh malinvestment is created. Austrians regard the dozen years after 2008 as the textbook case ([[case-2008-qe|Stage 13.4]]).
- **③ Keep wage rates up** — moral suasion on firms not to cut pay (which Hoover did in 1929), higher minimum wages, stronger union bargaining. Effect: when demand falls and wages cannot, firms can only shed workers — **mass unemployment in a bust is precisely the product of propped-up wages.** The logic of price controls ([[price-controls|Stage 8.2]]) applies to the labor market too.
- **④ Keep prices up** — farm purchases, output restrictions, tariffs (Smoot–Hawley, 1930). Effect: inventories cannot clear, resources do not move, consumers are forced to pay more.
- **⑤ Stimulate consumption and discourage saving** — raise taxes (especially on capital and high earners), public works, transfer payments. Effect: the scarcest thing in a bust is real saving (the only raw material for completing the remaining projects and rebuilding the capital structure), and these policies consume it. The Keynesian diagnosis of “under-consumption” is, to Austrians, exactly backward ([[vs-keynes|Stage 11.1]]).
- **⑥ Subsidize unemployment** — extend benefits without touching wage rigidity. Effect: it lengthens the time workers take to move from the wrong industries to the right ones.

Notice Rothbard's careful wording: he does not say these policies are ill-intentioned, but that each of them **attacks one link of the liquidation** — repricing (①②④), redeployment (③⑥), real saving (⑤). This is the cycle version of Mises's “dynamics of interventionism” from [[intervention-logic|Stage 8.1]]: every intervention creates the problem that calls for the next.

### ④ Disagreement inside the school: secondary deflation and Hayek's regret

Everything up to here is Austrian consensus. What follows is **disagreement**, told straight.

Hayek's 1930s position (and Lionel Robbins's 1934 book *The Great Depression*) was that liquidation is necessary and any attempt to “stop the fall” with monetary expansion only delays the adjustment. At the time this was called “liquidationism.”

But Wilhelm Röpke (*Crises and Cycles*, 1936) and later Lachmann identified a problem: liquidation can **spin out of control**. When banks fail in waves, money demand spikes (everyone hoards cash) and prices spiral downward, what gets dragged under is not only malinvestment but **firms that are perfectly healthy at the true rate** — they merely happened to need refinancing while credit was frozen. This is “**secondary deflation**” (or secondary depression): no longer cure, but complication. Röpke's conclusion: during a secondary deflation, using monetary means to stabilize nominal spending is legitimate — **but only that**, and only after the primary liquidation has run.

Hayek himself later conceded the point. In interviews and lectures in the 1970s he said his 1930s advice had underestimated the secondary-deflation problem, and that if he had it to do again he would have opposed the contraction of the money stock more clearly. Robbins went further and, in his 1971 autobiography, explicitly repudiated the thesis of his 1934 book. Scholars have since shown that Hayek's theoretical norm was always “keep the flow of money (MV) stable,” not “let money contract” — his 1930s **tone** was more liquidationist than his **theory**.

The argument is still alive today. Rothbardians hold that “secondary deflation” is exaggerated — the deflation of 1920–21 did not spin out of control; Hayekians and the more “monetary-equilibrium” Austrians (Selgin, White) hold that a central bank (or a free-banking system, [[free-banking|Stage 9.4]]) should prevent a collapse of nominal spending when money demand spikes. **What both sides agree on** is that nothing should be done to block the repricing and redeployment of malinvestment — the disagreement is only over whether the “money-demand shock” of the liquidation phase should be offset. [[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]] surveys these internal debates systematically.

### ⑤ Zombie firms: what is not allowed to die will not live either

The most visible consequence of blocking liquidation is the **zombie firm**: a company kept alive by low rates and rolled-over loans, whose profits cannot even cover its interest, which neither fails nor grows.

Japan is the textbook case. After the bubble burst in 1990, Japan's banking system — with the government's tacit consent — rolled over vast amounts of bad debt again and again. Those firms occupied factories, workers and credit lines while producing nothing consumers wanted. Healthy new firms could not get funding, productivity growth stalled — the “lost decades” ([[case-japan|Stage 13.3]]).

After 2008 the same pattern appeared in Europe and America. Research at the Bank for International Settlements estimates that the share of “zombies” among listed companies in advanced economies rose from very low levels in the 1980s to roughly 12–15% by the late 2010s (definitions differ across studies; treat the number as an order of magnitude). The Austrian reading is direct: **zero rates mean the liquidation never happens**; malinvestment is frozen in place, and the capital structure never returns to the shape consumers want. [[debt-zombies|Stage 10.4]] connects this to “secular stagnation.”

Zombies also reveal a neglected function of liquidation: it releases not only capital but **credit lines, talent and market share** to the firms that ought to be growing. If the old are not allowed to die, the new have nowhere to stand. This is the other face of competition as a “discovery procedure” in [[competition-process|Stage 6.2]]: discovering who should exit and discovering who should enter are the same process.

### ⑥ “But people are suffering”: the time argument and 1920–21

Now the honest objection, stated at full strength first:

> “Your theory may be logically right. But liquidation means real people losing jobs, homes and savings. How can a responsible government stand aside and call it ‘the cure’? Even if intervention prolongs the slump, spreading the pain over ten years is more humane than concentrating it in two years and letting it fall on the most vulnerable.”

This is a serious argument, and the Austrian reply is not “pain doesn't matter.” It is three things:

**First, the time argument.** Intervention does not spread the same pain thinner; it **adds to the total while stretching it out.** Every quarter without liquidation is a quarter in which resources sit unproductive in the wrong places, healthy firms go unfunded, and workers wait for jobs that are not coming back. Reflation manufactures new malinvestment, so the next liquidation is bigger. In the figure above, the area under the red line is larger — **that is not thinning, it is accumulation.** Japan's thirty years of stagnation were an entire generation's working lives.

**Second, the 1920–21 contrast.** After the First World War the United States went through a sharp deflationary depression: wholesale prices fell by roughly a third in little over a year, and unemployment by various estimates ran somewhere between about 5% and 12% (there were no modern statistics; estimates differ widely). The Harding administration cut federal spending and taxes; the Fed, having raised the discount rate to about 7% in 1920, mounted no rescue. By mid-1922 production and employment were rising strongly — the whole depression lasted about 18 months. No stimulus, no bailouts, no wage props (wages in fact fell sharply); the liquidation ran and the recovery came. Austrians do not claim this one case “proves” the theory (the methodology of [[apriori-empirical|Stage 2.2]] does not allow it), but it shows at least this: **the premise that “no intervention = endless depression” is false.**

**Third, what a truly humane policy looks like.** Austrians do not oppose helping people who lose their jobs — they oppose helping them by **blocking price adjustment.** Direct, temporary relief not tied to wages or prices is a completely different thing from “no pay cuts,” “bail out the banks” and “reflate”: the former does not obstruct liquidation, the latter attacks it. If you want to help people, help people — don't prop up prices.

The last word belongs to Mises's position in *Human Action*: **once credit expansion has happened, the bust is already unavoidable; the only choice is whether it comes sooner and shorter, later and longer, or — if it is never allowed to come — ends in the collapse of the currency.** In [[abct-history|Stage 5.4]] we look at four episodes of history and which of these each country chose.

## @analogy
Think of a bust as a **mass relocation.**

During the boom a crowd of people was led by a false map (the interest rate) to the wrong city — one with no work they can do, only some half-built towers. On the day the map is exposed, everyone realizes: we have to move back, to the city where consumers actually need us. That is liquidation: **the pain is not the move itself but the discovery that you came to the wrong place** — and that error was made on the day you set out.

Now the government arrives to “help,” with several options:

- **Pay people to stay in the wrong city** (bailouts, reflation): nobody moves, the half-built towers stay empty, people still have no real work. The moment the payments stop, the problem is back unchanged — and meanwhile more people have been drawn in.
- **Forbid moving companies from cutting their prices** (propped wages and prices): a price cut would have let people afford a truck; now they can't, and they are stuck where they are.
- **Pay benefits to those who stay** (subsidized unemployment): staying becomes more comfortable, and fewer people leave.
- **Do nothing and let the market reprice**: movers cut their rates, rents in the wrong city collapse, wages in the right city rise — prices point the way again, and people complete the move within a year and a half, messily but decisively.

The “secondary deflation” dispute is this: if, during the move, panic shuts down the bus system even in the *right* city (money demand spikes, credit freezes), should the buses be kept running? Röpke and the older Hayek say yes — but only the buses, never a subsidy for the wrong city. Rothbard says the buses don't stop that easily; don't use it as an excuse.

The genuinely humane policy is to give the movers **a ticket home** (direct, temporary relief), not to **make the wrong city more comfortable.**

## @misconceptions
- **“Austrians say ‘do nothing’ in a bust — they are heartless.”** — What Austrians oppose are policies that attack the liquidation: propped wages and prices, bailouts, reflation, the consumption of saving. They do not oppose direct, temporary relief not tied to prices. “Helping people” and “propping up prices” are two different things, and the second prolongs everyone's pain — including the most vulnerable.
- **“Liquidation creates losses, so liquidation is bad.”** — The loss happened the moment the projects broke ground in the boom: a half-dug mine does not finish itself because you refuse to admit it. Liquidation merely reprices it at true value and sends the resources back to where consumers want them. Without liquidation the loss does not disappear; it is frozen and compounded.
- **“Rate cuts can bring those projects back to life.”** — The real resources needed to finish them (engineers, steel, time) are right now producing what consumers want; for the projects to be completed, consumers would have to genuinely consume less. Another round of credit only attracts new long projects — 300 units chasing 100 units of saving — and makes the next liquidation larger. This is Mises's “ever-larger doses” argument.
- **“The Great Depression proves non-intervention leads to catastrophe.”** — 1929–33 was precisely the period of most intense intervention: Hoover propped wages, raised tariffs, raised taxes and created the Reconstruction Finance Corporation to bail out firms. The sharper deflationary depression of 1920–21, with almost no intervention, ended in about 18 months. Which one is the sample of “non-intervention” deserves a second look ([[case-great-depression|Stage 13.2]]).
- **“Austrians all agree on bust policy.”** — They don't. Hayek and Röpke/Lachmann genuinely disagreed about secondary deflation; Hayek later admitted he underestimated it in the 1930s, and Robbins repudiated his 1934 book. Today Rothbardians and monetary-equilibrium Austrians still argue over whether a spike in money demand during liquidation should be offset. The consensus is one point only: do not block the repricing and redeployment of malinvestment.
