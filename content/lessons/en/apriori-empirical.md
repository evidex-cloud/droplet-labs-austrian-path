# A Priori vs Empirical: Why Austrians Don't Run Experiments

## @hook
A physicist measures the speed of light and every laboratory on earth reproduces the same number. An economist measures the “price elasticity of gasoline demand,” measures again a decade later, and the number has shifted by a factor of four. From that difference Mises drew a radical conclusion: **there are no constants in human action**, so economic laws cannot be obtained by measurement — only by deduction from the action axiom. They are **a priori**. This lesson states that position clearly, then invites its strongest opponents on stage: Friedman's 1953 “Methodology of Positive Economics” and Popper's falsificationism. Then it answers three questions honestly: how do Austrians reply? Where does evidence genuinely matter for them? And why is even the Austrian camp itself (Hayek vs Mises) not fully united on this?

## @intuition
Look at two sets of numbers.

**Set one: the speed of light.** In 1676 the Danish astronomer Ole Rømer, timing eclipses of Jupiter's moons, estimated it at roughly 220,000 km/s. In 1862 Foucault's rotating mirror gave about 298,000. In 1926 Michelson measured about 299,796. Today the speed of light is *defined* as exactly 299,792,458 m/s. Over three centuries the measurements got sharper and **converged on a single point.**

**Set two: the price elasticity of gasoline demand** — when the price rises 1%, how much less gasoline do people buy in the short run? Studies from the 1970s and 80s typically found about −0.2 to −0.3: a 10% price rise, 2–3% less consumption. But a well-known 2008 paper (Hughes, Knittel and Sperling) re-estimated the number on 2001–2006 data and found only about −0.03 to −0.08 — **a quarter to a tenth of the earlier figure.** Later work using higher-frequency data came back with something like −0.3 again. Thirty years on, the measurements have not converged. The number **drifts.**

Why does it drift? Because light is the same light, but Americans in 2005 were not Americans in 1978. They lived farther out, drove different cars, earned different incomes, held different expectations about whether the price rise would last — and, above all, they **learn and change.** An “elasticity” is nothing more than a statistical summary of the choices some group of people made over some stretch of time. It is a piece of **history**, not a **law**.

Mises put it bluntly in *Human Action*: **“In the field of human action there are no constant relations, and consequently no measurement is possible.”** He did not mean numbers are useless. He meant that any number you measure is a historical fact about some past period; it will not, like the speed of light, “hold tomorrow too.” And if measurement cannot yield laws, yet economics does have laws — things that *do* hold tomorrow — then the laws must come from somewhere else. They come from **deduction** out of the action axiom of [[action-axiom|Stage 2.1]].

The law “quantity demanded falls as price rises” was never obtained by measuring elasticities. It comes from this: an actor allocates scarce means to ranked ends; a higher price means giving up more of other things to get this one; so **some marginal uses get cut.** The reasoning does not depend on 1978 versus 2005, or on America versus China. It is **a priori** — known true before you look at the data — and, precisely because it is, you can *read* the data at all. You recognize a price-quantity scatter as “a demand curve” only because you bring that law with you when you look.

At this point anyone with standard training frowns: “Isn't that just refusing to test? Isn't falsifiability the mark of science? Didn't Friedman say a theory is judged only by whether its predictions are accurate?” These are good questions, and behind them stand two of the twentieth century's heavyweight thinkers, Karl Popper and Milton Friedman. Half of this lesson lets them challenge the Austrians in their **strongest** form, and then watches the Austrians answer. You will find the answer is not “we don't need evidence,” but “**evidence occupies a different place in economics than it does in physics**” — a point Hayek made better than anyone in his 1974 Nobel lecture.

**In this lesson we break it into six pieces:**

- **① Kant's “synthetic a priori”: about the world, yet not from observation**
- **② Mises: no constants, hence no measurement, hence laws are a priori**
- **③ Methodological dualism and Hayek's critique of “scientism”**
- **④ The opponents at full strength: Friedman 1953 and Popper's falsificationism**
- **⑤ The Austrian reply — and where evidence really does matter**
- **⑥ The honest part: this stance is contested even among Austrians**

## @mechanics
### ① Kant's “synthetic a priori”: about the world, yet not from observation

Get the philosophical vocabulary straight first, or the rest of the lesson will trip over it. In the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) Kant sorts propositions along two axes:

- **Analytic vs synthetic.** In an analytic proposition the predicate is already contained in the subject (“bachelors are unmarried”) — true, but it tells you nothing new. A synthetic proposition adds information (“this bachelor lives in Vienna”).
- **A priori vs a posteriori.** An a priori proposition can be known true without experience; an a posteriori one requires observation.

Everyone grants that there are analytic a priori truths (logical tautologies) and synthetic a posteriori truths (empirical facts). Kant's bold claim was that there is also a **synthetic a priori**: propositions that tell you something substantive about the world *and* are known without observation. His examples were arithmetic and geometry (“7 + 5 = 12,” “a straight line is the shortest path between two points”) and the principle of causality itself.

Why does this concern economics? Because Austrians claim that economic laws are of exactly this kind. **“Both parties to a voluntary exchange expect to gain beforehand”** is not a word game (it rules out the substantive claim that exchange is zero-sum), and it was not obtained by surveying ten thousand transactions (you know it holds before the survey; otherwise you would not know what to count as a “voluntary exchange”). It is synthetic, and it is a priori. The previous lesson laid out the three slightly different accounts Mises, Rothbard and Hoppe give of *how* a proposition can be both; the conclusion is shared.

### ② Mises: no constants, hence no measurement, hence laws are a priori

Mises's argument compresses into three steps.

**Step one: physics can measure because there are constants.** The speed of light, the gravitational constant, the charge of the electron — natural objects have no purposes, do not learn, do not change their minds, so the relation between two magnitudes can be stable, repeatedly measurable, convergent.

**Step two: human action has no constants.** Acting people have purposes, and purposes change. They learn, and what they learn alters their next choice. They hold expectations, and expectations shift as they watch what others do. So the *x* in “price up 1%, quantity down *x*%” is not a constant of nature but the sum of choices made by particular people, in a particular period, with particular knowledge and expectations. Mises explicitly named the econometric work of his day: those elasticity estimates **are economic history, not economics** — they tell you how Americans bought potatoes in the 1930s, not any law.

**Step three: laws must come from elsewhere.** If measurement cannot give laws, and economics nonetheless has laws (we really do know that “a binding price ceiling causes a shortage,” and we know it more reliably than any elasticity estimate), then the laws can only come from deduction — from the logic of action, step by step. That is the chain the demo of [[action-axiom|Stage 2.1]] walked you through.

The gasoline example ties the three steps together:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Law (a priori):} &\quad \text{price up} \;\rightarrow\; \text{some marginal uses are cut} \\
&\quad \;\rightarrow\; \text{quantity lower than it would otherwise be} \\[6pt]
\text{History (a posteriori):} &\quad \text{1975--80, elasticity} \approx -0.2 \text{ to } -0.3; \\
&\quad \text{2001--06, } \approx -0.03 \text{ to } -0.08
\end{aligned}
$$

The law holds in both periods (the elasticity is negative in both); the **magnitude** is entirely different. In Mises's terms, the first line is economics, the second is history. History is valuable, but it cannot replace the first line — and if you take one of the second-line numbers, call it a “constant,” and plug it into a model to forecast 2030, you are passing off history as law.

### ③ Methodological dualism and Hayek's critique of “scientism”

Mises summarized the conclusion as **methodological dualism**: the natural sciences study purposeless matter, the social sciences study purposeful people, and the two require **fundamentally different methods.** This is not a claim that humans escape the laws of physics (obviously they do not); it is a claim that to explain *why* a person did something you cannot avoid “what did he want, what did he believe” — and those have no counterpart in the world of atoms. Importing the whole toolkit of physics into economics is a category error.

Hayek attacked the same thing from a different angle. His essays “Scientism and the Study of Society,” serialized in *Economica* in 1942–44 and collected in *The Counter-Revolution of Science* (1952), gave the error a name: **scientism** — the “slavish imitation of the method and language of Science,” mistaking *looking like physics* for *being scientific.* He pointed out that the “facts” of social science (a transaction, a price, a firm) are themselves **constituted by the opinions and purposes of participants.** Whether a piece of paper is “money” depends on whether people treat it as money; whether a building is a “factory” depends on someone intending to produce in it. You cannot measure these things the way you measure a rock, without asking what people think.

Thirty years later Hayek stood on the Stockholm podium to accept the Nobel Prize, and titled his lecture **“The Pretence of Knowledge”** (1974). What he said grated on the macroeconomics of the day: economists had “made a mess of things” on inflation and unemployment because, imitating physics, they **admitted as evidence only what could be measured**, and so built their theories on measurable variables (aggregate demand, the money stock) rather than on what actually drives events but cannot be quantified (the relative prices and expectations of millions of people). He called this the “scientistic attitude,” and warned that pretending to know what one does not is far more dangerous than admitting ignorance. He did not say mathematics was useless; he said that **when your subject is a complex phenomenon, theory can give you patterns, not numbers.** [[individualism-history|Stage 2.3]] develops that idea of *pattern prediction*.

### ④ The opponents at full strength: Friedman 1953 and Popper's falsificationism

Now switch sides and give the opponents their best lines.

**Popper (*The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, German 1934, English 1959).** The line between science and non-science is **falsifiability.** To count as science, a theory must dare to say “if you observe X, I am wrong.” Astrology and psychoanalysis can explain anything and therefore say nothing; Newtonian mechanics dares to say “planetary orbits must be ellipses,” and is therefore science. The virtue of the criterion is obvious: **it forces theories to take risks and stops them hiding in the comfort zone of “any outcome fits.”** By that standard, an economics that “cannot be overturned by data” sounds more like astrology than like Newton.

**Friedman (“The Methodology of Positive Economics,” 1953).** A theory's worth lies not in whether its assumptions are “realistic” but in whether its **predictions are accurate.** Leaves arrange themselves *as if* maximizing sunlight; an expert billiards player shoots *as if* solving differential equations — we do not need leaves to know optics or players to know physics; if the “as if” assumption yields good predictions, the theory is good. Likewise in economics: no need to argue whether people “really” maximize utility; if the predictions derived from that assumption survive the data, that is enough. The appeal is that **it drags the argument away from the endless philosophical quarrel “are your assumptions true?” back to the decidable question “are your predictions right?”** The Chicago School's empirical tradition ([[vs-chicago|Stage 11.2]]) rests on it.

Put the two together and the charge against Austrians is sharp: **you say your laws are a priori, you reject falsification, you don't check predictions — so how would you ever know you were wrong? Is a discipline that cannot be wrong still a discipline?**

The charge is serious and deserves a serious answer.

### ⑤ The Austrian reply — and where evidence really does matter

The reply has four layers.

**Layer one: in the social world, “falsification” cannot actually be carried out.** Popper's criterion works in physics because you can control variables and repeat experiments. Economics has no laboratory in which “other things stay equal.” If employment fails to fall after a minimum-wage hike, maybe the law is wrong — or maybe a simultaneous expansion swamped the effect, employers cut hours and benefits instead, or the data definitions changed. **Any single “falsification” can be absorbed by an auxiliary assumption.** That is not Austrians cheating; it is a structural feature of social data (philosophers call it the Duhem–Quine problem). The upshot: mainstream economics in practice never abandons a theory over one failed prediction either; it, too, *explains* rather than *falsifies.* Austrians merely decline to pretend they are running experiments.

**Layer two: Friedman's “as if” buys prediction without understanding.** A model that is “as if” right may predict well under the conditions that generated its data and collapse when conditions change — because you never learned *why* it worked. The Phillips curve of the 1970s is the textbook case: in 1960s data, inflation and unemployment traded off beautifully; policy tried to “buy” jobs with inflation; in the 1970s both soared together. The curve held “as if” for a decade, then vanished — because people's expectations changed, and there was no term for “people learn” in the model. The Austrian point: **you want mechanism, not fit**; mechanism comes from the logic of action. Lucas's 1976 critique ([[abct-critiques|Stage 5.5]]) said almost the same thing from inside the mainstream.

**Layer three: you cannot “test” a law you are using to read the data.** Want to test the law of demand? First you must find “the demand curve” in the data — and interpreting a cloud of points as “demand” rather than “supply” or noise is done *with* the very law you set out to test. Econometrics' “identification problem” is the technical form of this. **The law is logically prior to the data** — not an Austrian preference, but a precondition of reading any data at all.

**Layer four: evidence matters enormously to Austrians — in a different place.** This is the layer most often missed. Austrians do not reject experience; they reject *using experience to establish or overturn laws.* Evidence is **indispensable** for:

- **Deciding which law applies.** To explain 1929 with ABCT you must first check: was there credit expansion in the 1920s? Was the rate pushed down? Did investment concentrate upstream? All empirical questions ([[abct-history|Stages 5.4]], 13.2).
- **Describing history.** “How the 2021–23 inflation happened” is a historical narrative requiring data, documents, and an understanding of the intentions of the actors (the Fed, the Treasury, households) ([[case-2008-qe|Stage 13.4]]).
- **Auxiliary assumptions.** “Labor has disutility,” “there are many kinds of resources,” “people use money” are empirical facts the theory must be fitted onto before it touches the ground.
- **Magnitude and urgency.** The law says a ceiling causes a shortage; how severe, how fast, who is hurt most are empirical questions — and policy debate cares about exactly those.
- **Persuasion and illustration.** An a priori law paired with one good case is ten times more convincing than logic alone.

So the right division of labor is: **theory tells you what to look for; history tells you what you found.** [[empirical-method|Stage 13.1]] sets out this “explain, don't test” approach to empirical work in full.

### ⑥ The honest part: this stance is contested even among Austrians

If this lesson has left you thinking Austrians speak with one voice on method, it has misled you.

**Hayek was far more empiricist than Mises.** His 1937 “Economics and Knowledge” states outright that the pure “logic of choice” is a priori, but that **propositions about how people acquire and transmit knowledge are empirical** — and that the interesting part of economics lies in the latter. He was a lifelong friend of Popper, dedicated *Studies in Philosophy, Politics and Economics* to him, and broadly accepted falsificationism — insisting only that in the realm of complex phenomena what can be falsified is a *pattern*, not a *point.*

**Fritz Machlup, Ludwig Lachmann, and contemporaries such as Peter Boettke** each settled at different points between “a priori” and “empirical.” Many Austrian scholars of the George Mason lineage today do empirical work, case studies, even experiments and econometrics; they think Mises's “extreme apriorism” overstated the case, even though its core insight (no constants, laws by deduction) is right.

**Outside critics deserve a hearing too.** The methodologist Mark Blaug, in *The Methodology of Economics* (1980), called Mises's position “cranky and idiosyncratic” and argued it hands Austrians a permanent “never wrong” pass. Bryan Caplan's “Why I Am Not an Austrian Economist” (1999), written as a sympathizer, argued that apriorism keeps Austrians right about *why* demand curves slope downward but takes them out of the game on policy questions that turn on magnitudes. Some of this lands: **Austrian empirical work is genuinely thin**, and “a priori” has indeed served some people as an excuse for laziness.

**Experimental economics exists and must be faced.** Vernon Smith (Nobel 2002) showed in laboratory markets that even with very little information, double auctions converge quickly on equilibrium prices — and he credited Hayek's insight for the result. Interestingly, those experiments did not “falsify” any Austrian law; they *demonstrated* them. But they also remind Austrians that dismissing “experiments” wholesale is saying too much.

So the conclusion of this lesson needs careful wording: **the core Austrian claim — that human action has no constants and that economic laws come from deduction out of the logic of action rather than from statistical induction — stands up to its strongest opponents; but pushing it to the extreme “experience is irrelevant” is neither what Mises meant nor what most Austrians accept.** The next lesson ([[individualism-history|Stage 2.3]]) turns to the other methodological pillar — only individuals act — and the distinction between theory and history that grows from it. [[vs-math-models|Stage 2.4]] then puts the fight over mathematics and equilibrium on the table. And when [[data-new-oil|Stage 15.4]] asks whether data is the new oil, and [[ai-central-planning|Stage 18.1]] asks whether AI can central-plan, you will recognize today's debate as this lesson's debate in new clothes: **can you dig “constants” about acting people out of past data at all?**

## @analogy
Picture two people studying the same river.

**The physicist** measures the water's density, flow rate and gradient. She measures today, tomorrow, and at another bend, and the numbers agree — because water is water and does not change its mind about being measured. She can safely put her numbers into an equation and predict how deep a valley the river will cut in a century.

**The social scientist** is studying the **market on the riverbank**: how many people come each morning and what they will pay for fish. He measures too: Monday 200 people, average price $30; Tuesday 190 people, $31. He fits a “demand curve,” elasticity about −0.5. Then one year a bridge opens nearby, or a viral video makes the market famous, or every fish seller switches to the same pricing software — and the whole curve shifts. His “constant” has drifted away. **Not because he measured badly, but because the thing he measured learns, talks, and changes its mind.**

Yet there is one thing the social scientist knows without measuring, and it will never drift: **when fish gets dearer, some people will buy a little less** — not everyone, not by a fixed proportion, but someone, at the margin, will give something up. That “law” comes not from Monday's and Tuesday's data but from the logic of a person spending limited money on ranked wants. He uses the law *to read* the data (that is how he knows to call the scatter “demand”), rather than using the data to “prove” the law.

The Austrian stance is the social scientist's stance: **measure carefully, record carefully, but never mistake the market's statistical summary for the density of water.** [[data-new-oil|Stage 15.4]] will ask: what if we had the market's data second by second? The answer is the same — more data is still only past people's past choices.

## @misconceptions
- **“Austrians reject all empirical evidence and reason in a sealed room.”** — What they reject is using statistics to establish or overturn laws, not evidence itself. Deciding which law applies to a period, describing that period, gauging magnitudes and urgency, checking auxiliary assumptions — all require evidence. Mises himself wrote a great deal of economic history. The difference is the division of labor: theory says what to look for, history says what was found.
- **“Elasticities, velocity, multipliers are economics' constants — we just haven't measured them precisely enough.”** — Their drift is not measurement error; the people being measured changed their knowledge, expectations and circumstances. Light-speed measurements converged over three centuries; gasoline-elasticity estimates have wandered for thirty years. In Mises's words: these numbers are economic history, not economics.
- **“Unfalsifiable means unscientific (Popper).”** — In the social world any single ‘falsification’ can be absorbed by an auxiliary assumption because there is no ceteris-paribus laboratory; mainstream economics in practice never drops a theory over one failed forecast either. And logic, mathematics and geometry are unfalsifiable by experiment without being pseudoscience. The real issue is the nature of the subject, not whether experiments are possible.
- **“Friedman settled it: assumptions don't matter, only predictions.”** — That is Friedman's actual claim and his strongest argument, and it deserves respect. The Austrian reply: an ‘as if’ model predicts well under the conditions that produced its data and collapses when they change (the 1970s Phillips curve), because you never knew why it worked. You need mechanism, not just fit.
- **“Mises's apriorism is the official Austrian position.”** — Hayek was much more empiricist and held that ‘how people acquire knowledge’ is an empirical question; many contemporary Austrians do case studies, econometrics, even experiments. Some Austrians concede that critics like Blaug and Caplan land real hits on ‘extreme apriorism.’ The core insight (no constants, laws by deduction) commands broad agreement; where its boundary lies is disputed.

## @quiz
1. The short-run price elasticity of gasoline demand was estimated at about −0.27 for 1975–80 and about −0.05 for 2001–06. On Mises's view, what are these two numbers?
   - [ ] Two measurements of the same constant, the later one more accurate
   - [x] Two pieces of economic history: each summarizes some people's choices in some period, and neither is a law
   - [ ] Proof that the law of demand stopped working in the 2000s
   - [ ] Proof that gasoline is a Giffen good
   > Human action has **no constants**: an elasticity is a summary of particular people's choices in a particular period. The sign is negative in both (the law holds); the magnitude differs (the history differs).

2. What is a “synthetic a priori” proposition in Kant's sense?
   - [ ] A tautology whose predicate is contained in the subject
   - [ ] An empirical fact confirmable only by observation
   - [x] A proposition that says something substantive about the world yet is known true without observation
   - [ ] A proposition true only within certain cultures
   > “Synthetic” = substantive content (not a word game); “a priori” = not from experience. Austrians hold that “both parties to a voluntary exchange expect to gain” is such a proposition.

3. What is the strongest form of Friedman's 1953 “as if” argument?
   - [ ] People really do maximize utility, so the model is realistic
   - [x] Whether assumptions are realistic is irrelevant; a theory's worth is its predictive accuracy — which drags the debate back to a decidable question
   - [ ] Economics needs no assumptions at all
   - [ ] Only mathematical models count as economics
   > Friedman's point is methodological: leaves “as if” seek light, billiards players “as if” solve equations. The Austrian reply: “as if” buys prediction without understanding, and the model collapses when conditions change.

4. Which of the following is a place where Austrians hold that empirical evidence is INDISPENSABLE?
   - [ ] Establishing the law that quantity demanded falls as price rises
   - [x] Determining whether credit expansion occurred in a given period, and so whether ABCT applies
   - [ ] Refuting “both parties to a voluntary exchange expect to gain”
   - [ ] Proving diminishing marginal utility
   > Laws come by deduction; but **which law applies to which period**, how the period is described, and how large the effects are, are all empirical. Theory tells you what to look for; history tells you what you found.

5. What is the central criticism of Hayek's 1974 Nobel lecture “The Pretence of Knowledge”?
   - [ ] Economists use too much mathematics and should switch entirely to prose
   - [x] Economists imitate physics by admitting only measurable things as evidence, and so build theories on measurable but unimportant variables while ignoring what really matters but cannot be quantified
   - [ ] Economics should give up prediction altogether
   - [ ] Austrians should embrace econometrics
   > Hayek's critique of scientism: pretending to know what you do not is the most dangerous thing. He did not oppose mathematics; he argued that with complex phenomena theory yields patterns, not numbers.
