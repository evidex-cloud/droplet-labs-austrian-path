# The Boom: How Malinvestment Piles Up

## @hook
The most dangerous thing about a boom is that it does not look dangerous. Wages are rising, profits are rising, stocks and houses are rising, and every quarterly report says “you got it right.” The Austrian asks you to look not at *how much* but at *where*: new money follows the Cantillon path — banks first, borrowers next — and steers capital toward the projects **furthest from the consumer with the longest payback**: real estate, mines, chip fabs, cash-burning platforms, debt-funded mergers. This is not *too much* investment; it is investment in the **wrong place** — malinvestment. This lesson dissects the boom from the inside: which organs malinvestment grows in, how labor gets dragged into the wrong industries, how book profits disguise capital consumption as income, and which gauges Austrians watch.

## @intuition
Picture a city throwing a carnival.

Music is blaring, neon is flashing, and everyone has money to spend — because the organizers handed out a stack of “carnival vouchers.” Restaurants have queues out the door, craftsmen land big orders, young people quit steady jobs to open stalls. The mayor goes on television: “Look — this is prosperity.”

An Austrian economist standing in the crowd asks a different question from everyone else. Not “how much is everyone making?” but: **what do these vouchers correspond to?** If they are money the citizens actually saved, the carnival is a celebration bought with real thrift — sustainable. If the organizers simply printed them, then everyone has more “money” while the city's timber, flour and labor hours **have not increased by a single plank, pound or hour.**

Where does the extra spending go? To wherever the organizers handed out vouchers first. In a real economy, the people who get new money first are always **borrowers** — credit expansion *is* lending. And who borrows most? The projects with the longest payback: the developers, the miners, the factory builders, the platforms that “turn profitable in ten years,” the firms using cheap debt to buy their rivals. [[abct-one-picture|Stage 5.1]]'s duration arithmetic told you why: the lower the rate, the more a long project's numbers improve. So new money does not sprinkle evenly across the economy; it flows **with a bias** toward the places that most need “society is willing to wait” to be true — and society is not, in fact, willing to wait.

That is the anatomy of a boom:

- **Capital** is steered into the wrong stages (too far upstream, too long).
- **Labor** follows capital: construction workers, finance hires and startup engineers multiply into jobs that, under real saving, should not exist in those numbers.
- **Profits** look beautiful — because assets are appreciating, because depreciation is booked at old prices, because customers are also ordering with borrowed money. Part of the “profit” is actually eating the seed corn (capital consumption, first met in [[heterogeneous-capital|Stage 3.4]]).
- **Asset prices** become fuel: rising house prices make people feel richer, so they borrow and spend more, which pushes house prices up again.

Austrians have a precise word for this: **malinvestment**. Not “overinvestment.” The difference is fatal. If the problem were merely investing *too much*, the bust would just be a period of digesting spare capacity — no big deal. But if the problem is investing in the *wrong place* — and a printing press cannot be turned into a tractor (the heterogeneous capital of [[heterogeneous-capital|Stage 3.4]]) — then the half-dug mine and the empty office tower are not “temporarily idle.” They are **real, irrecoverable social loss.**

This lesson opens the boom up: the path new money takes, the organs malinvestment grows in, how labor is dragged into the wrong places, how illusory profits are computed, why the boom feels like prosperity, and which dashboards Austrians watch. [[bust-liquidation|Stage 5.3]] covers the liquidation, [[markets-bubbles|Stage 10.3]] the financial markets during a boom, [[case-2008-qe|Stage 13.4]] the full case of 2008 and QE, and [[ai-bubble-abct|Stage 18.5]] applies this anatomy to AI capital spending.

**In this lesson we break it into six pieces:**

- **① The path new money takes: from Cantillon effects to the structure of production**
- **② Wrong place, not too much: malinvestment vs overinvestment**
- **③ The organs malinvestment grows in: six hot spots**
- **④ The people who follow the capital: labor misallocation**
- **⑤ Illusory profits: how capital consumption is disguised as income**
- **⑥ Why the boom feels like prosperity — and the gauges Austrians watch**

## @mechanics
### ① The path new money takes: from Cantillon effects to the structure of production

[[cantillon-inflation|Stage 4.3]] introduced the Cantillon effect: new money is not rain falling evenly on everyone; it is a river that enters at one point and follows a channel. The entry point for credit expansion is **the bank's loan desk**. So the question becomes: who most wants to borrow, and who can?

[[abct-one-picture|Stage 5.1]]'s duration arithmetic gives the answer. Let the rate fall from 5% to 2%:

- a project with a 2-year payback improves its NPV by about 4.5;
- a project with an 8-year payback improves by about 12.3;
- a project with a 20-year payback (a commercial tower, a railway) improves by far more.

**A rate cut “subsidizes” long projects far more than short ones.** So the queue of borrowers naturally sorts itself by payback, longest first — new money flows first and most into the stages furthest upstream and furthest from the consumer. Hayek drew this in *Prices and Production* as the base of the triangle being stretched; Mises put it more bluntly in *Human Action*: credit expansion steers production in directions that would only be justified if people had saved more.

In numbers: an economy earns 1,000; people save 100 and consume 900. Banks conjure 100 of new loans, so “investment funds” total 200. Real saving can complete only 100 units of projects, but 200 units break ground — **at least 100 of them can never be finished** unless consumers change their minds midway and actually cut consumption from 900 to 800. They won't.

### ② Wrong place, not too much: malinvestment vs overinvestment

This is the most important conceptual distinction in the lesson, and the place where Austrians part ways with almost every other school.

**Overinvestment theories** (many pre-Keynesian cycle theories, and some of today's “overcapacity” narratives) say: too much investment happens in the boom, capacity outruns demand, and it must be digested. On this view the bust is a digestion period; the capacity is fine, there is just temporarily too much of it.

**The malinvestment theory** says: the problem is not quantity but **direction**. New money steers capital into a structure of production that does not match consumers' time preference: too much upstream, too little downstream; too many 8-year projects, too few 2-year ones. These projects are not “temporarily excessive”; they **can never be completed under actual saving, or if completed, produce things nobody is willing to wait for.**

Why is this distinction fatal? Because capital is heterogeneous ([[heterogeneous-capital|Stage 3.4]]). If capital were putty that could be reshaped at will, “wrong direction” could be corrected at zero cost, and malinvestment would collapse into overinvestment. But a half-dug mine cannot become a restaurant; a cleanroom built for one chip process cannot become a logistics warehouse; a team that only knows leveraged buyouts cannot pivot to consumer goods overnight. **Get the direction wrong and part of the capital never comes back** — it can only be repriced at scrap value or at its value in some inferior alternative use, and the gap is society's real loss.

Mises had a precise formulation: credit expansion does not *create* capital; it only *misdirects* it. The aggregate-minded see investment rising in a boom and say “the capital stock has grown.” Austrians reply: on the books, yes — but part of that is doomed half-finished structures, and the real capital stock may actually have **shrunk**, because building those structures consumed resources that would otherwise have maintained existing machines.

### ③ The organs malinvestment grows in: six hot spots

The theory says “upstream, long payback.” In a real economy, what does that mean? The list is strikingly similar in every credit boom:

- **Real estate and land.** The longest payback (a building is underwritten over 30 years), the highest rate sensitivity, and usable as collateral to borrow still more. The Florida land boom of the 1920s, American subprime in the 2000s, Tokyo land in the 1980s — all here ([[abct-history|Stage 5.4]]).
- **Capital goods and heavy industry:** mining, steel, shipbuilding, energy, chip fabs. Hayek's prediction that “upstream swings more” is most visible here — in every bust, machine-tool orders fall several times harder than retail sales.
- **Technology and “burn now, profit later” platforms.** A company that turns profitable in ten years is, in substance, a 10-year-payback project; the lower the rate, the higher its valuation. The dot-coms of 1999 and the “growth stocks” of 2021 belong here ([[markets-bubbles|Stage 10.3]]).
- **Long-duration financial assets:** 30-year bonds, perpetual-growth valuation models, private equity. Their price sensitivity to rates is, literally, mathematical duration.
- **Mergers and financial engineering.** Cheap debt makes “borrow to buy a competitor” and “borrow to buy back our own shares” pencil out — creating no new capacity, just converting a rate subsidy into book gains.
- **Luxury and “lifestyle” industries.** The consumption of the first recipients of new money (finance, property and tech workers): fine dining, luxury cars, art. These are the downstream end of the Cantillon effect.

Note carefully: **nothing on this list is bad in itself.** Houses, mines, chips and startups are all good investments when real saving stands behind them. What is wrong is not the industries but **the portion** that broke ground on a false rate — the red beyond the blue dashed box.

### ④ The people who follow the capital: labor misallocation

Where capital goes, people go. The most overlooked malinvestment of a boom is in **human capital**.

In the mid-2000s United States, construction employment grew far faster than total employment, and finance salaries pulled large numbers of top engineering graduates away from engineering. These people were not “unemployed” — quite the opposite: they were well paid and very busy. But their skills were being shaped to fit **an unsustainable structure of production**: mortgage brokers, structured-product traders, agents specializing in new-build condo sales.

When the liquidation came, what they faced was not “find another firm” but **the repricing of an entire skill set**. This is why Austrians hold that bust-phase unemployment cannot simply be cured by “boosting demand”: a mortgage broker laid off in 2009 did not need more mortgage demand (that was the source of the malinvestment); he needed time to find his place in a **new structure of production better matched to consumers' time preference**. [[abct-critiques|Stage 5.5]] returns to this when it addresses Krugman's “hangover theory” objection.

Incidentally, this is also the truth behind boom-time “wars for talent”: talent has not suddenly multiplied; **two structures of production are competing for the same people** — the one consumers actually want and the one the low rate has conjured.

### ⑤ Illusory profits: how capital consumption is disguised as income

Why do boom-time financial statements look so good? Three mechanisms.

**First, historical-cost depreciation.** A factory bought a machine in 2003 for 100 and depreciates it over 10 years, booking 10 a year. By 2006 the boom has pushed the replacement price of the same machine to 150, so true annual depreciation is 15. The books still charge 10. Hence:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Books:} &\quad \text{revenue } 40 - \text{depreciation } 10 = \text{profit } 30,\ \text{dividend } 30 \\
\text{Truth:} &\quad \text{revenue } 40 - \text{depreciation } 15 = \text{profit } 25; \\
&\quad \text{the extra } 5 \text{ paid out} = \text{capital eaten}
\end{aligned}
$$

Five units of capital consumption a year get distributed as “income.” Mises called this **illusory profit** — the most insidious feature of inflation, because it lets firms grow poorer while celebrating ([[heterogeneous-capital|Stage 3.4]] and [[aggregates-limits|Stage 10.2]] both return to it).

**Second, customers are spending borrowed money too.** A building-materials firm's orders come from developers; the developers' money comes from new bank loans. The orders are real and the profits are real, but their **source** is credit expansion. When credit tightens, the orders vanish along with their source. Part of the “demand” an entrepreneur sees is the echo of the rate illusion.

**Third, asset appreciation booked as gains.** Firms holding land, shares or inventory all show “unrealized gains” in a boom. Those gains were “pre-paid” the moment the rate was pushed down — they are not value produced, but the same future cash flows re-priced at a lower discount rate. When the discount rate rises again, the gains go back the way they came.

Stack the three together and boom-time “profit” becomes a compound: partly real value creation, partly capital consumption, credit echo and discount-rate illusion. **The trouble is that the income statement does not separate them** — this is the deepest root of Austrian distrust of aggregate statistics ([[aggregates-limits|Stage 10.2]]).

### ⑥ Why the boom feels like prosperity — and the gauges Austrians watch

If malinvestment is real loss, why does everyone feel good during a boom? Because **the loss is in the future and the gains are now.** The workers on the half-dug mine got paid today; the unfinished tower raised GDP today; the platform that turns profitable in ten years issued stock options today. Malinvestment is a bill made out to the future; the boom sees only the consumption, not the bill.

Asset prices amplify everything: house prices rise → collateral is worth more → borrow more → house prices rise again. Stocks rise → wealth effect → consumption rises → earnings rise → stocks rise again. This feedback loop is what makes people believe “this time is different” — [[memes-reflexivity|Stage 16.5]] covers its reflexive version.

So Austrians do not watch “sentiment indexes.” They watch a few gauges built to capture **structure** rather than **aggregates**:

- **The gap between the policy rate and the natural rate.** The natural rate cannot be observed directly, but it can be approximated by proxies — trend nominal growth, changes in real saving — and a policy rate held far below those for years is a signal.
- **Credit growth vs saving growth.** When bank credit (or broad money, M2) grows far faster than household saving, loans are not saving passed along but money created from nothing. In 2020–21 US M2 grew by roughly 40% cumulatively, while the jump in the measured “saving rate” was built from transfer payments, not a change in time preference ([[case-2008-qe|Stage 13.4]]).
- **The yield curve.** Late in a boom the central bank is forced to hike; short rates overtake long rates and the curve inverts — the market's version of “the rate has rebounded and long projects no longer pencil out.” Austrians do not worship it, but it agrees with the theory.
- **The ratio of capital-goods output to consumer-goods output.** This is a direct observation of the Hayekian triangle's base: a rising ratio means the structure is lengthening.
- **Gross Output.** A statistic championed by Mark Skousen: instead of counting only final goods (GDP), count sales at every stage of production. It makes upstream expansion and contraction visible — [[aggregates-limits|Stage 10.2]] covers it properly.

None of these gauges gives you a date. They answer the question [[abct-one-picture|Stage 5.1]] ended on: **what is buried in the boom.** As for when what is buried gets dug up — see [[bust-liquidation|Stage 5.3]].

## @analogy
Think of a boom economy as a **banquet with one kitchen.**

The ingredients in the kitchen (labor, steel, energy, time) are fixed. Normally the head chef plans the menu around how long the guests are actually willing to wait: half quick dishes (consumer goods), half slow-braised ones (long projects) — ordered by guests who are prepared to wait for them. That is the structure of production under real saving.

Now somebody (the bank) hands every table an extra stack of “meal tickets.” The guests have more tickets, but **the kitchen has not gained a single ingredient.** The chef sees a flood of orders for slow dishes — because the tickets make slow dishes look especially good value — and commits most of the ingredients to pots that need eight hours.

For the first two hours the banquet is spectacular: the kitchen is at full tilt, the number of assistant cooks has doubled, the waiters are flying, and the quick dishes arriving at the tables have not thinned out yet. That is the **boom**.

In the third hour the trouble starts: ingredients for quick dishes run short (consumer prices rise), the assistants' wages double (factor prices rise), the slow dishes still need five more hours — and the guests never intended to wait that long. They never agreed to; the tickets made the chef **believe** they had. The chef has no choice but to tip out some half-cooked braises and shift the remaining ingredients back to quick dishes — that is **liquidation**.

The pots tipped out are **malinvestment**: not “too many dishes,” but ingredients committed to pots the guests were never willing to wait for. The extra assistant cooks are **labor misallocation**. The first two hours' record “table turnover” is **illusory profit** — it counted the tipped-out ingredients as sales.

What the Austrian in the dining room was staring at from the first hour was the source of that stack of tickets: did the guests save them, or did somebody print them? That decides whether the braises can ever be finished.

## @misconceptions
- **“Malinvestment just means overinvestment — digest the excess capacity in the bust and you're done.”** — Wrong place is not too much. Capital is heterogeneous: a half-dug mine cannot become a restaurant, a dedicated cleanroom cannot become a warehouse. The misdirected portion can only be repriced at scrap value or at its value in an inferior use; the gap is real, irrecoverable social loss, not “temporary idleness.”
- **“Houses, mines, chip fabs and startups are malinvestments, so Austrians are against those industries.”** — All of them are good things and good investments when real saving stands behind them. What is wrong is only the portion that broke ground on a false rate — the red beyond the blue box. The Austrian target is the forged price signal, not any particular industry.
- **“Profits are high and everyone is employed, so the economy is healthy.”** — Boom-time profit is a compound of real value creation, capital consumption (depreciation at old prices), credit echo (customers ordering with borrowed money) and discount-rate illusion (unrealized gains). The income statement does not separate the four; that is the root of Austrian distrust of aggregates. Malinvestment is a bill made out to the future, and the boom sees only the consumption.
- **“Nobody is unemployed in the boom, so labor isn't being misallocated.”** — The misallocated are not unemployed; they are well paid and busy building the skills an unsustainable structure needs: mortgage brokers, structured-product traders. When the liquidation comes they face not a change of employer but the repricing of an entire skill set. That is why bust-phase unemployment cannot be cured just by “boosting demand.”
- **“Watching the yield curve and credit growth means Austrians are market-timing.”** — Those gauges measure how distorted the structure of production has become; they answer “what is buried in the boom,” not “which day it blows.” Credit expansion can run longer than anyone expects; the Austrian permabear habit is exactly mistaking a gauge for an alarm clock ([[common-mistakes|Stage 14.4]]).
