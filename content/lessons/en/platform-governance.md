# Platform Governance, Speech & Private Property

## @hook
Who gets to decide whether a post stays or goes? The Austrian starting point is [[why-property|Stage 9.1]]: **a platform is private property, and the landlord sets the house rules** — Rothbard argued there is no “right to speak” separate from property, and a shopping mall is not a public square. But the starting point is only a start: [[network-effects|Stage 15.1]]'s network effects make “exit” expensive, [[economics-of-state|Stage 8.4]]'s state pressure blurs the line between private and public, and the old common-carrier argument is being carried over to new platforms. This lesson treats moderation as part of the product ([[profit-loss|Stage 6.3]]), explains Section 230 neutrally and runs it through [[five-step-analysis|Stage 14.1]]'s five steps, uses [[externalities-coase|Stage 9.2]] to show why “harm from speech” needs a property baseline, and then gives the Austrian remedies — competition, interoperability, federated protocols, user-chosen moderation layers — along with the genuine disagreement inside the school.

## @intuition
Start small. In your living room, a guest begins loudly saying things you do not want to hear. You ask him to leave. He says: “You're violating my freedom of speech!” You would find that absurd — **it is your living room.** He is free to speak; he is not free to speak in your living room. He can speak in the street, in his own house, in the house of anyone willing to listen.

Rothbard, in The Ethics of Liberty (1982), pushed that small case all the way: **there is no such thing as a “right of free speech” independent of property rights.** Freedom of speech is always the freedom to speak somewhere, using some resource, and that place and that resource always have an owner. Whoever owns the hall decides who takes the stage; whoever owns the press decides what gets printed. That is the property logic of [[why-property|Stage 9.1]]: scarce things need someone to decide their use, or there is conflict.

Swap the living room for a social platform. The servers are its, the software is its, the domain is its. When it removes a post, that is you asking a guest to leave: not a violation of free speech, but an exercise of property. American courts said the same thing about shopping malls in the 1970s: **a mall is private property, not a public square,** and it may forbid leafleting inside. The reasoning is clean, consistent and backed by precedent. Most Austrians stop here.

This lesson cannot stop here, because the other side has three genuinely strong arguments, each of which uses an earlier lesson:

First, **network effects make “go and speak elsewhere” no longer cheap** ([[network-effects|Stage 15.1]]). A guest evicted from a living room can go next door; a person banned from a platform with a billion users finds no billion users next door. When everyone is in one place, the cost of “you can go elsewhere” is roughly the cost of “you can go to the moon.”

Second, **the state is backstage** ([[economics-of-state|Stage 8.4]]). If a platform removed a post because a government official phoned — “suggesting” certain content be removed, and hinting at trouble otherwise — is that still private property at work? Several lawsuits and one public letter in the 2020s turned that question from a hypothesis into a record. When private action is taken under state coercion, the word “private” is discounted.

Third, the old **common-carrier** argument. A telephone company may not cut your line because it dislikes your views; a railway may not refuse you for who you are — a centuries-old common-law tradition that certain “businesses open to the public” owe a duty not to discriminate. Some say today's platforms are that telephone company.

How do Austrians answer? This lesson's claim is: **the property starting point remains correct, but it obliges Austrians to actually answer the three arguments rather than repeat “it's private property.”** To network effects, the answer is competition and interoperability ([[platforms-winner|Stage 15.2]]) — make “elsewhere” cheap again rather than turning one place into a public square. To state pressure, the answer is the harshest one: **the problem is not the platform removing posts; it is the government making phone calls** — Austrians should aim their fire at the phone-call end. To common carriage, the answer is a distinction: a carrier transmits **without looking at the content** (the phone company does not listen), whereas a social platform's product **is** the sorting and filtering of content ([[algorithms-order|Stage 16.2]]) — you cannot demand it be both a phone company and an editor.

There is also a technical question buried under the slogans: moderation is **part of the product.** A space free of spam, harassment and fraud is itself something users and advertisers will pay attention and money for — [[profit-loss|Stage 6.3]]'s consumer sovereignty shows up here as: too lax and users leave; too strict and users also leave. What a platform is looking for between those two is not “the correct speech policy” but a product that keeps both sides. This lesson's demo lets you run a platform yourself.

Finally, the school's own split: **most Austrians oppose treating platforms as common carriers; a minority favor it** — on the ground that platforms are already so entangled with the state (immunity clauses, government pressure, subsidies) that they have forfeited pure private status. That is a real dispute, and we write both sides. [[defi-code-order|Stage 17.4]] will show another answer: write governance rules directly into code and protocols, so that “who sets the rules” matters less.

**In this lesson we break it into six pieces:**

- **① Platforms are private property: whose place, whose rules**
- **② Moderation is part of the product: consumer sovereignty of users and advertisers**
- **③ The other side at full strength: network lock-in, state pressure, common carriage**
- **④ Section 230: what it says, what it does not, and the five-step test**
- **⑤ The “externalities” of speech: why harm needs a property baseline**
- **⑥ Austrian remedies and Austrian disagreements: competition, interoperability, federated protocols, chosen moderation layers**

## @mechanics
### ① Platforms are private property: whose place, whose rules

[[why-property|Stage 9.1]]'s reasoning: scarce resources need someone to decide their use, or there is conflict; property is the set of rules about “who decides”; it grows out of first use and voluntary transfer. A server is scarce (it holds only so many posts, handles only so many requests); a domain name is scarce; a “position” in a user interface is scarce (there is only one front page). So they must have owners, and owners decide use.

Rothbard, in Chapter 15 of The Ethics of Liberty, “Human Rights as Property Rights,” took this to its conclusion: **so-called “freedom of speech” is not an independent right but a corollary of property.** You may say anything on your own property, and spread it with your own resources (or resources voluntarily lent to you); you have no right to someone else's hall, press or server. The correct meaning of “freedom of speech violated” is: **someone (usually the state) used force to stop you speaking on your own or voluntarily provided resources.** A landlord showing a guest the door is not a violation; a state forbidding the landlord from hosting certain guests is.

American law largely agrees with Rothbard here, along a clear line of precedent (years approximate): in 1946 the Supreme Court held that a **company town** (an entire town owned by one firm) could not forbid leafleting in its streets — because it had assumed all the functions of a town; in 1972 and 1976 the Court twice held that **shopping malls** may forbid leafleting or protest — a mall is open to the public, but it is not a public square; it is private property, opened in order to do business. In 2019 the Court reaffirmed: a private company operating public-access channels is not a “state actor,” and its programming choices are not constrained by the First Amendment. **“Open to the public” is not “public”** — that is the core of the precedent, and the core of the Austrian position.

So the starting point is clear: when a platform removes posts, suspends accounts or reduces reach, it is exercising property on its own property. You may call it stupid, unfair, biased — as you may call a restaurant's menu terrible — but “violating freedom of speech,” in property theory, is aimed at the wrong target.

### ② Moderation is part of the product: consumer sovereignty of users and advertisers

Political slogans call moderation “censorship”; economics calls it **product design.** Imagine a space with no moderation at all: spam, fraud, harassment, child sexual abuse material, bot accounts. The open mail relays of the 1990s and the anonymous forums of the 2000s tried “no moderation,” and the result was **users left** — not because they were censored, but because the space became unusable. The spam filter was the earliest and least controversial “content moderation” ever built, and nobody calls it a violation of free speech.

So moderation is part of the product, like a restaurant's dress code, a library's quiet rule, a club's membership review. [[profit-loss|Stage 6.3]]'s consumer sovereignty has two voters here:

- **Users** vote with time. Too lax, and ordinary users are driven off by harassment; too strict or too arbitrary, and the wrongly caught users and those who value “being able to speak” leave. The platform looks for the point between them that **keeps as many users as possible** — not a philosophy of speech, but a retention rate.
- **Advertisers** vote with money. Brands do not want to appear beside extreme content ([[creator-economy|Stage 16.3]] described the 2017 “adpocalypse,” a moderation tightening triggered by advertisers pulling out en masse). A coordinated advertiser boycott of one platform in 2020, and the large-scale advertiser exodus from another after its 2023 change of ownership, are instances of this vote. For an ad-funded platform, **advertisers' preferences about moderation are usually stricter than users'** — which explains why standards shift with the business model: subscription platforms typically moderate more loosely than ad platforms, because their paying side is users rather than brands.

One corollary follows directly: **there is no “correct moderation standard,” only different products.** A strictly moderated platform and a loosely moderated one are like a vegetarian restaurant and a steakhouse, serving different customers. [[subjective-value|Stage 1.2]]'s subjective value: some people will pay the price of “can't say certain things” for “clean”; others the reverse. The market should grow diversity rather than converge on one standard — which is exactly what piece ③ has to handle: **if network effects let the market grow only one platform, where does diversity come from?**

One more detail that is often overlooked: the **execution** of moderation is itself a vast knowledge problem ([[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]]). A platform makes judgments on millions of items a day, using outsourced reviewers and classifiers; it cannot know the context of each item (satire or threat? quotation or advocacy?). So moderation necessarily errs — in both directions. “Appeal processes” and “transparency reports” are not charity; they are the platform insuring against its own knowledge problem: an appeal is the channel through which the user sends the context back. In this lesson's demo, appeals and transparency are two dials, and you will see how they move the variable called “trust.”

### ③ The other side at full strength: network lock-in, state pressure, common carriage

Now the three counter-arguments at their strongest.

**Argument one: network effects make exit prohibitively expensive** ([[network-effects|Stage 15.1]]). Property theory says “you can go elsewhere.” But a social platform's value comes from other people being on it (Metcalfe's old point), so “elsewhere” has value only if the others go too. Being banned from a billion-user platform is not being thrown out of a restaurant; it is being thrown out of **the only restaurant with anyone in it.** Hirschman's 1970 framework in Exit, Voice, and Loyalty is useful here: when exit is very costly, people can only rely on “voice” (complaint, appeal, public pressure) — and why should a platform that need not fear exit listen to voice?

**Argument two: state pressure blurs the private/public line** ([[economics-of-state|Stage 8.4]]). If a platform removed a post because a government official “suggested” it — with hints of antitrust investigation, changes to immunity, or hearings otherwise — then the removal is private in form and state action in substance. In 2022 a batch of one platform's internal communications was published; in 2024 a platform CEO admitted in a letter to Congress that certain content had been removed under government pressure; the same year the Supreme Court heard a case alleging federal officials coerced platforms (dismissed for lack of standing, without reaching the merits, but another unanimous decision that year held that **government may not punish speech by threatening third parties**). This is called “jawboning.” The argument is especially lethal for Austrians, because what Austrians hate most is the state doing through private agents what it may not do directly — [[intervention-logic|Stage 8.1]] described how intervention disguises itself layer by layer.

**Argument three: common carriage.** For centuries the common law has imposed a duty of non-discrimination on certain businesses: an inn may not refuse a traveler, a ferry may not refuse a passenger, a telephone company may not cut a line over opinions. The rationale: these businesses are “open to the public, of public necessity, and often monopolistic.” In 2021 a Supreme Court justice's concurrence explicitly suggested today's large platforms may fit that description; the same year two states passed laws forbidding large platforms from removing content by viewpoint (in 2024 the Supreme Court held that **moderation is itself protected expressive activity** and sent the cases back, without a final resolution). A prominent free-speech scholar wrote a long 2021 article seriously arguing the feasibility of “platforms as carriers” — this is not a fringe view.

The Austrian replies, in the same order:

To argument one: **agree with the problem, reject the conclusion.** Lock-in is real, but turning one platform into a public square **entrenches** the lock-in — it grants the incumbent a permanent, legally protected “public” status. The Austrian direction runs the other way: **lower the cost of exit.** [[platforms-winner|Stage 15.2]] covered interoperability and multi-homing; piece ⑥ covers protocols that take identity and data out of the platform. The goal is to make “elsewhere” cheap again, not to concede that “elsewhere does not exist.”

To argument two: **complete agreement, and more radical than the critics.** State pressure by telephone is state action and should be constrained — Austrians have no reservations here. But mind the direction of the logic: the problem is **the end making the call**, and the remedy is **to forbid the government making such calls** (the “anti-jawboning” rules some legal scholars propose), not to strip platforms of property. Using the fact that platforms were coerced to argue “therefore the state should regulate platforms more” is fixing one state intervention with another — Mises's intervention spiral from [[intervention-logic|Stage 8.1]].

To argument three: **a crucial distinction.** The carrier's duty rests on a premise: the carrier **does not look at the content.** The phone company does not listen; the railway does not ask where you are going — so demanding non-discrimination is cheap; it never meant to discriminate. But a social platform's product **is** the selection of content ([[algorithms-order|Stage 16.2]]: the ranking rule is taxis); demanding that it “not look at content” is demanding that it not make its product. You can ask a phone company to be a carrier because it is a pipe; you cannot ask a newspaper to be a carrier because it is an editor. Which is a platform more like? **Its feed is like a newspaper; its direct messages are like a telephone** — so one possible scheme, which some Austrians are willing to accept, is: **impose carrier duties on the “pipe” parts (messaging, basic transport, DNS, payments) and keep full property over the “editorial” parts (feed, recommendation, community rules).** That distinction turns argument three from “accept it all or reject it all” into a question that can be debated layer by layer — the five layers in piece ①'s figure look more like pipes the further down you go.

### ④ Section 230: what it says, what it does not, and the five-step test

Section 230 of the 1996 Communications Decency Act is the most misquoted law in this debate. First, neutrally, what it says.

The background is two early-1990s cases: an online service that did no moderation was held not liable for a user's defamatory post (like a bookstore, it did not know what was in the books); another service that **did** moderate was held liable (like a publisher: having reviewed content, it owned what slipped through). That produced an absurd incentive: **moderate and you are liable; don't and you are immune.** Two congressmen (one Republican, one Democrat) drafted Section 230 to fix it. The two key sentences:

- **(c)(1)**: a provider of an interactive computer service shall not be treated as the “publisher” of information provided by a third party. — A user's defamation is not the platform's defamation (the author bears it).
- **(c)(2)**: a provider is not liable for good-faith restriction of material it considers obscene, harassing or “otherwise objectionable.” — A platform may moderate without thereby becoming a publisher.

What it does **not** say (common misreadings): it does not require platforms to be “neutral” — the statute contains no “neutrality” and no “platform vs publisher” dichotomy; that dichotomy is a later invention of political rhetoric. It does not immunize content the platform itself creates; it does not immunize federal criminal law or intellectual property (and a 2018 amendment added an exception for sex trafficking). In 2023 the Supreme Court had an opportunity to narrow it and chose not to.

Now run “repeal or gut Section 230” through [[five-step-analysis|Stage 14.1]]'s five steps:

1. **Goal**: make platforms “responsible” for content (the supporters' goals point opposite ways: one side wants platforms to **remove less** — immunity lets them remove with impunity; the other wants them to **remove more** — immunity lets them ignore harmful content. **The same proposal is backed by two opposite goals**, which is itself an alarm).
2. **Instrument**: repeal (c)(1) or (c)(2), or attach a “neutrality” condition.
3. **Who pays**: if platforms bear defamation liability for every user post, the rational response is **far more removal** (better to over-delete than under-delete) or **shut the comments** — the 1990s incentive returns. Paying are small platforms (no legal team) and marginal speech (the first to be swept out by “better safe than sorry”).
4. **Who collects the rent**: large platforms that can afford compliance — [[taxes-regulation|Stage 8.3]]'s old story, regulation as the incumbent's moat. That a large platform's CEO publicly supported amending Section 230 should surprise no one.
5. **Next round**: a “neutrality” condition needs someone to define neutrality — that is a speech regulator, and [[economics-of-state|Stage 8.4]] tells you who captures it.

The Austrian conclusion is not “Section 230 is perfect” but: **it is a rule that lets property work normally online** — it lets a platform, like a landlord, avoid liability for guests' conduct while, like a landlord, setting house rules. Repealing it would not bring “neutrality”; it would bring back the 1990s “moderate and be liable.” The real problem (piece ③'s state pressure) lies outside Section 230.

### ⑤ The “externalities” of speech: why harm needs a property baseline

The most common case for more regulation is “harmful content”: misinformation harms public health, hate speech harms groups, incitement harms democracy. That is an **externality** argument — [[externalities-coase|Stage 9.2]] handled its general form; here is only the part specific to speech.

[[externalities-coase|Stage 9.2]]'s core: the word “externality” is empty without a property baseline. A factory's smoke invades a neighbor's lungs — harm, because the neighbor owns her lungs. A new restaurant takes an old restaurant's customers — no harm, because the old restaurant does not own its customers. **The same “effect” is or is not harm depending on whether the thing affected is yours.**

Apply that baseline to speech:

- **Threats, fraud, incitement to imminent violence**: these invade specific persons' rights over body or property. Rothbardian property theory recognizes them as harms, and the law always has.
- **Defamation**: here Austrians disagree among themselves. Rothbard held that “reputation” is not property (it is what other people think, and you do not own their minds), so defamation should not be an actionable harm; most legal traditions, and most Austrians, are not that extreme. A genuine internal debate.
- **“Misinformation,” “hate speech,” “offense”**: what is affected is **the listener's beliefs and emotions.** Does the listener own his beliefs in a way that can be invaded? No one can invade them — the listener formed them himself. This is not to say such speech has no consequences, but that **its consequences pass through the listener's free action** (believing, sharing, being offended), and [[action-axiom|Stage 2.1]]'s praxeology does not allow a consequence that passed through free action to be counted as the prior speaker's “harm.”

This line of analysis has its hardness and its soft spot. Its hardness: it gives a clear criterion rather than “any effect I dislike is an externality.” Its soft spot: **large-scale coordinated misinformation** (say, systematic promotion of fake cures in a pandemic) really can, through countless listeners' “free actions,” produce statistically measurable casualties, and the property baseline can only say “each listener was free.” The honest Austrian answer is that this is a problem of a **market in trust and reputation** (whom to believe?), not a property problem — and the market handles it through media reputation, fact-checking services and user-chosen moderation layers (piece ⑥), not through the state defining “true.” Whether that answer is sufficient depends on your estimate of how fast reputation markets work — Austrians lean optimistic, critics lean pessimistic, and the empirical evidence ([[algorithms-order|Stage 16.2]], piece ④) is so far inadequate for either.

### ⑥ Austrian remedies and Austrian disagreements: competition, interoperability, federated protocols, chosen moderation layers

The Austrian remedies point in one direction: **make exit cheap and rules plural.** Four layers:

- **Competition** ([[platforms-winner|Stage 15.2]]): not an empty word. The old platforms of the 2010s lost young users; new ones entered through video, photos, anonymity, interests — each time “exit” at work. [[creative-destruction|Stage 15.5]] covered the speed of incumbent turnover.
- **Interoperability and data portability**: let users take their graph and content with them. The EU's Digital Markets Act (2022) requires some messaging interoperation — interoperability compelled by law, assessable with the five steps; Austrians prefer the spontaneous versions below.
- **Federated and decentralized protocols** (described accurately):
  - **Mastodon / ActivityPub** (software released 2016; the protocol became a W3C recommendation in 2018): no single company; thousands of independent servers (“instances”) each set their own rules and moderate themselves, relaying to one another. Choosing an instance is choosing a set of house rules; if dissatisfied you can move to another instance with your followers. Moderation is **server-level**: an instance's admin can block entire other instances. The costs: discovery is hard, moving has friction, and admins have great power within their instance.
  - **Nostr** (proposed 2020): a minimal protocol. Identity is a key pair (you own your identity; no platform issues it); signed content is published to multiple “relays”; any relay may refuse to carry it, but no relay can delete your identity or stop other relays carrying it. Moderation happens at **the client and relay** layers, chosen by users and relay operators respectively. Costs: spam is hard to manage, and the user experience is rough.
  - **Bluesky / AT Protocol** (open beta 2023; federation opened 2024): portable identity (a domain name can be your handle), data held on personal data servers that can be migrated, feeds written by third parties ([[algorithms-order|Stage 16.2]]'s “algorithm marketplace”), and **moderation split into composable “labeling services”**: anyone can run a labeler (“spam,” “hate,” “spoiler”), and users choose which labelers to subscribe to and what to do with each label (hide / warn / ignore). This is the most direct implementation of this lesson's claim: **moderation becomes a user-chosen, contestable service rather than one platform decision.** Costs: the default labeler is still run by the developing company, the network is small, and a sustainable business model is unproven.
- **User-chosen moderation layers**: the third item above has already turned this into a product. Its logic is piece ②'s “no correct moderation standard” implemented in architecture — you subscribe to a strict labeler, I subscribe to a lenient one. It is also the market answer to piece ⑤'s misinformation problem: fact-checking becomes a subscribable, competitive service.

Finally, the disagreement within the school, stated plainly. **Most Austrians** (and this lesson) oppose treating platforms as common carriers: property is the starting point, exit costs are lowered by competition and protocols, and state pressure is solved by constraining the state. **A minority of Austrians** (especially some writers in the Rothbardian tradition who focus on state–business entanglement) argue: since large platforms enjoy special status from the state (immunity clauses, government contracts, acting under government pressure), they are no longer purely private property but part of a “public–private partnership,” so imposing non-discrimination duties on them does not violate liberal principles and may even **withdraw** a state privilege. The argument's strength is that it seizes on piece ③'s state pressure; its weakness is that its conclusion is still more state intervention (who decides “non-discrimination”?), and Section 230 is not a “privilege” — it merely lets online property work like offline property. What both sides agree on: **the root of the problem is the entanglement of state and platform, not the platform's private character.** [[defi-code-order|Stage 17.4]] will show another road: write the rules into immutable code, so that “who sets the rules” is replaced by “the rules are public in advance and no one can change them” — which solves some problems and creates new ones.

## @analogy
Picture a **private club**: membership is free, and the club makes money from the advertising boards inside.

The club has a members' code: no fighting, no soliciting, no harassment. The owner wrote it — it is his house. Someone gets shown the door and shouts “you're violating my freedom of speech”; the owner says: the door is there, the street is free. Up to this point Rothbard and the courts both side with the owner.

But this club is peculiar: **the whole town is in it.** The person shown the door walks into the street and finds it empty — everyone he wanted to talk to is inside. “The door is there” suddenly sounds less honest.

Now look at the owner's books. An advertiser phones: “My board can't hang next to people brawling, or I pull it.” The code tightens. Members complain it is too strict; some leave for a new little club on the corner that polices nothing — which quickly fills with touts and con men, so the respectable people leave that one too. The owner adjusts the code between the two ends, tuning not “correct speech” but **retention.**

Then a man from City Hall drops by. He issues no order — he merely “suggests” the owner clean up certain topics, and mentions in passing that next year's licence is up for review. The owner complies. Now ask: is this still the owner's code?

Three kinds of people prescribe three remedies. The first says: make the club a public square; no one may be evicted. The second says: pass a law forbidding City Hall to make that kind of call — **the problem is the call, not the code.** The third says: make membership cards carry your relationships — your friends list is yours, not the club's, so the day the corner club opens you can bring your friends along; and turn the code into a subscribable service — you want strict, subscribe to strict; I want lenient, subscribe to lenient; same room, each sees his own.

Austrians prescribe the second and the third. They reject the first — while admitting that on the point “the whole town is in it,” the other side is telling the truth.

## @misconceptions
- **“The platform deleted my post; it violated my freedom of speech.”** — Rothbard: there is no right of speech separate from property; freedom of speech is the freedom to speak on your own or voluntarily provided resources. The platform's servers are its property, and deleting a post is the landlord showing a guest the door. The mall cases (1972/76) say the same: open to the public is not public. You may call it stupid or biased, but “violating free speech” is, in property theory, aimed at the wrong target — the real violation happens when the state uses force to stop you speaking on your own resources.
- **“Since it's private property, network effects and state pressure are not questions Austrians need to answer.”** — The opposite. Lock-in is real: being banned from a billion-user platform is not being thrown out of a restaurant but out of the only restaurant with anyone in it; state pressure by phone is real: a platform CEO's 2024 letter admitted it. The Austrian answer is not to repeat “private property” but to lower exit costs (interoperability, federated protocols) and constrain the state (forbid jawboning). The direction differs from the critics', but the questions must be answered head-on.
- **“Section 230 requires platforms to be neutral; a platform that moderates is a publisher and should lose immunity.”** — The statute contains no “neutrality” and no platform-vs-publisher dichotomy; that is later political rhetoric. Section 230 was written precisely to fix the 1990s incentive “moderate and be liable”: (c)(1) platforms are not liable for user content, (c)(2) platforms may moderate in good faith without becoming publishers. Repeal would not bring neutrality; it would bring “better over-delete than under-delete” and closed comment sections, with small platforms and marginal speech paying.
- **“Social platforms are today's telephone company and should bear common-carrier non-discrimination duties.”** — The carrier duty presupposes the carrier does not look at content: the phone company does not listen. A social platform's product is the sorting and filtering of content ([[algorithms-order|Stage 16.2]]); demanding it not look at content is demanding it not make its product. The workable distinction: carrier duties can be debated for the “pipe” parts (messaging, transport, DNS, payments); property remains over the “editorial” parts (feed, recommendation, community rules). In 2024 the Supreme Court also held that moderation is itself protected expression.
- **“Misinformation and hate speech are externalities, so they need regulation.”** — [[externalities-coase|Stage 9.2]]: without a property baseline, “externality” is an empty word. Threats, fraud and incitement to imminent violence invade specific persons' rights; “misinformation” and “offense” affect listeners' beliefs and emotions, which the listeners formed by their own free action. That does not make such speech consequence-free; it makes it a problem for a market in trust and reputation (fact-checking services, chosen labeling layers), not a property problem. Austrians should admit the soft spot: large-scale coordinated misinformation can produce measurable harm through countless free actions, and whether reputation markets are fast enough is empirically unsettled.
