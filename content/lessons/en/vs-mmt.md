# vs MMT & Post-Keynesians: Can Printing Money Buy Prosperity?

## @hook
Modern Monetary Theory says: a government that issues its own currency can never “run out of money”; it spends first and taxes later; deficits are not the problem; the only constraint is inflation — so stop asking “where will the money come from?” and ask “are there idle real resources?” Part of this is **true about operations** (the central bank and the Treasury really do keep their books this way, and banks really do create money when they lend), and part of it is **wrong about economics**. The Austrian reply is not “printing money means Zimbabwe.” It is three more precise sentences: **“cannot default” is not “cannot impoverish”; the “real-resource limit” needs someone to judge it, and MMT never says who or how; and who receives the new money first decides who wins and who loses — which is exactly what MMT's framework has no place for.**

## @intuition
First, MMT stated so that Stephanie Kelton would nod.

Kelton's *The Deficit Myth* (2020) opens by demolishing an intuition: **a government is not a household.** A household must earn before it spends; a government that issues its own currency (the United States, Britain, Japan — not eurozone members or countries borrowing in dollars) **spends first and taxes afterward.** Every dollar it spends is a dollar it created; taxes merely pull some of them back and destroy them. So “the government is out of money” is a logical impossibility, like a scoreboard running out of points. That is MMT's first proposition: **the currency issuer cannot go broke.**

The second comes from Chartalism (Knapp, 1905; rediscovered by Randall Wray in 1998): **money gets its value from taxes.** The state declares “taxes may be paid only in this paper,” and people must acquire the paper — that is why fiat money is demanded at all. The function of taxes is not to “raise money” but (a) to create demand for the currency and (b) to drain purchasing power from the private sector to control inflation.

The third is an accounting identity: **the government's deficit is the non-government sector's surplus.** Every dollar the government spends beyond what it taxes adds a dollar to private net financial assets. So “cutting the deficit” means “cutting private saving.” (This is Godley's sectoral-balances analysis, and it is true and uncontroversial.)

Fourth: **the only real constraint is inflation.** As long as there are idle workers, machines and materials, government spending merely puts them to work and does not raise prices; only when real resources are exhausted does further spending inflate. At that point, cool things down with taxes (or less spending).

Fifth: **the job guarantee** (Mosler, 1990s). The government hires anyone who wants work at a fixed wage (say $15 an hour) for public-service jobs; in good times the private sector poaches from this “buffer stock,” in bad times people flow back into it. It is both an automatic stabilizer and a price anchor (the fixed wage sets a floor under labor).

If you have done [[fractional-reserve|Stage 4.4]], you will notice that the MMT and Post-Keynesian view of **endogenous money** — **bank lending creates deposits; deposits do not come first** — is one Austrians share. Mises said in 1912 that fractional-reserve banks create “fiduciary media” out of nothing. The Bank of England's 2014 paper “Money Creation in the Modern Economy” says what Austrians said a century earlier. **On how the books are kept between the central bank, the Treasury and the commercial banks, MMT is broadly right.**

So what do Austrians reject?

**First, money's value does not come from taxes.** The regression theorem of [[origin-of-money|Stage 4.1]]: a thing can serve as money because it had purchasing power yesterday, traced back to its value as a commodity. Taxes can **sustain** demand for an existing money; they cannot **create** one. History is full of tax-receivable currencies the market abandoned anyway (Weimar, Zimbabwe, Venezuela — and, more mildly, Turkey and Argentina, whose citizens refuse to save in lira or pesos although taxes must be paid in them).

**Second, the “real-resource limit” needs a judge — who, and by what means?** The economic calculation problem of [[mises-1920|Stage 7.1]] returns in full. Whether an economy has “idle resources” is not an aggregate visible from above; it is a dispersed, constantly shifting set of relative judgments in the minds of firms and workers. An idle machine tool may be “a resource government spending can activate,” or “malinvestment from the last boom that should be scrapped.” MMT assumes someone can tell the difference — and assumes that someone is Congress.

**Third, who receives the money first.** The Cantillon effect of [[cantillon-inflation|Stage 4.3]]: new government spending reaches defense contractors, infrastructure firms and job-guarantee positions first, then ripples outward; early receivers buy at old prices, late receivers at new ones. Before the aggregate “inflation” shows up, the distribution has already shifted. MMT's framework contains only “total spending vs total capacity,” never “who got it first.”

**Fourth, the natural experiment of 2020–23.** In 2020–21 U.S. federal deficits totaled about $6 trillion and broad money grew by about 40% — an MMT-style operation (“spend first, don't ask where the money comes from”). MMT's prediction: no inflation while idle resources remain. Result: CPI inflation exceeded 9% year on year in 2022, a four-decade high. MMT's answer — “supply chains and energy shocks” — is partly true, but **countries facing the same shocks with far smaller monetary expansion had markedly lower inflation** (Switzerland, Japan), and the cooling tool MMT prescribes — higher taxes — was never used; the Fed raised rates, Congress did not raise taxes.

**Fifth, “cannot default” and “cannot impoverish” are different claims.** A government can always pay its debts with money it prints — true. But what that money buys shrinks — also true. The Venezuelan government never “ran out of money”; it merely made money useless.

Finally the job guarantee — the most ambitious and most revealing part of MMT: a fixed-wage scheme in which the government decides “which work is valuable” is exactly the **production without calculation** that Mises described in 1920. The demo lets you sit in the planner's chair.

**In this lesson we break it into six pieces:**

- **① MMT at full strength: the currency issuer, Chartalism, sectoral balances, the job guarantee**
- **② Where Austrians agree: endogenous money and the operational truths**
- **③ Regression theorem vs Chartalism: taxes can sustain a money, not create one**
- **④ The “real-resource limit”: who judges? Cantillon effects and economic calculation**
- **⑤ 2020–23: a natural experiment (approximate figures)**
- **⑥ “Cannot default” ≠ “cannot impoverish”: political economy and the Post-Keynesian bridge**

## @mechanics
### ① MMT at full strength: the currency issuer, Chartalism, sectoral balances, the job guarantee

Write MMT's skeleton as five propositions, each with its canonical text:

- **Proposition 1: the currency issuer faces no financial constraint** (Mosler, *Seven Deadly Innocent Frauds*, 2010; Kelton, 2020). Technically, the U.S. Treasury holds an account at the Fed; once Congress authorizes spending, the Fed credits the recipient bank's reserve account. Taxes and bond sales are ways of draining reserves *afterward*. “Spend first, tax later” describes the operational order.
- **Proposition 2: taxes drive money** (Chartalism; Knapp, *The State Theory of Money*, 1905; Wray, *Understanding Modern Money*, 1998). The state requires taxes to be paid in the currency it issues, creating a base demand for it. “Money is a creature of the state.”
- **Proposition 3: the sectoral-balances identity** (Godley). Domestic private balance + government balance + foreign balance = 0. A government deficit necessarily corresponds to an increase in non-government net financial assets.
- **Proposition 4: the constraint is real resources, not money.** The ceiling on government spending is inflation — the exhaustion of real capacity. Before that point, every “we can't afford it” mistakes a monetary constraint for a resource constraint.
- **Proposition 5: the job guarantee** (Mosler, 1997–98; Wray; Tcherneva). Government as employer of last resort hires everyone willing to work at a fixed wage, forming a counter-cyclical “buffer stock”; the fixed wage doubles as a price anchor.

MMT also inherits the Post-Keynesian theory of **endogenous money** (Moore, 1988; Lavoie): bank lending creates deposits, the central bank can only influence this indirectly through the interest rate, and the money supply is *pulled* out by credit demand rather than *pushed* by the central bank. This is the exact opposite of monetarism's “the central bank controls M” ([[vs-chicago|Stage 11.2]]).

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{government deficit} &\equiv \text{increase in non-government} \\
&\phantom{{}\equiv{}}\text{net financial assets} \\[6pt]
\text{the sustainability boundary} &= \text{real capacity (inflation),} \\
&\phantom{{}={}}\text{not the quantity of money}
\end{aligned}
$$

To be fair: on **describing the operating mechanics of a modern fiat system**, MMT is more accurate than most textbooks. It rightly mocks “the government will run out of money like a household,” rightly notes that under fiat money taxes are not the “funding source” of spending, and rightly exposes the confusion in “the national debt burdens our grandchildren” (the debt is a private-sector asset). **Its strength is accounting; its trouble is economics.**

### ② Where Austrians agree: endogenous money and the operational truths

There is a genuine overlap between Austrians and MMT, and ignoring it is a straw man:

- **Banks create money.** Mises's *Theory of Money and Credit* (1912) distinguished “money substitutes” from “fiduciary media” — the latter being deposits banks create without full reserves. Rothbard and Huerta de Soto (1998) treat fractional-reserve banking as the engine of credit expansion. MMT and Post-Keynesians say “loans create deposits”; Austrians said it a century ago. The difference is evaluation: MMT calls it normal, Austrians call it the root of the cycle ([[fractional-reserve|Stages 4.4]] and 5.1).
- **The central bank cannot precisely control monetary aggregates.** The free-banking Austrians (Selgin, White) long ago conceded that monetarism's k% rule fails under endogenous money ([[vs-chicago|Stage 11.2]]).
- **A fiat government cannot default in nominal terms.** An operational fact; Austrians do not deny it. What they deny is “therefore deficits don't matter.”
- **“The debt burdens our grandchildren” is too simple.** The Austrian criticism is more precise: the burden of debt is that it draws resources away from private uses *now* ([[taxes-regulation|Stage 8.3]]), and that it will be repaid either by taxes or by inflation — both resource transfers, not “mailing the bill to the future.”
- **Unemployment is not laziness.** Austrians and MMT both regard involuntary unemployment as real; they differ on cause (Austrians: liquidation of malinvestment plus wages pinned by policy; MMT: deficient aggregate demand).

So the Austrian reply to MMT should not be the one-liner “printing = Zimbabwe.” It should concede: **MMT keeps the books correctly; the trouble is that it thinks correct bookkeeping is the same as understanding the economy.**

### ③ Regression theorem vs Chartalism: taxes can sustain a money, not create one

MMT's Proposition 2 says a currency's value comes from the tax obligation. The Austrian regression theorem ([[origin-of-money|Stage 4.1]]) says a currency's purchasing power today comes from people's memory of its purchasing power yesterday, traced back to its use value when it was still an ordinary commodity. The two theories answer the same question differently: **why does anyone hold a piece of paper that is useless in itself?**

- **MMT:** because you need it to pay taxes. Taxes create the “base demand” for the currency, and the rest derives from it.
- **Austrians:** because it bought things yesterday. Taxes are **one source of demand among many**, and not the decisive one.

Which is right? Look at counterexamples. **Tax-receivable currencies the market abandoned:** the mark of 1923, the Zimbabwe dollar of 2008, the bolívar after 2018 — the governments kept collecting taxes; the people fled to dollars, gold and cigarettes. The milder cases are more persuasive: residents of Turkey, Argentina and Egypt **pay taxes in the local currency and save in dollars.** Taxes sustain a minimum transactional use of the local money, but cannot stop it being abandoned as a store of value — and of money's three functions, store of value is the most fragile and the truest gauge of trust.

Conversely, **things that became money with no tax obligation behind them:** cigarettes in the POW camp (Radford, 1945), Bitcoin ([[bitcoin-regression|Stage 17.1]] examines in detail whether it satisfies the regression theorem), and the Spanish silver dollar that circulated across borders for centuries — no state collected taxes in them, and they circulated anyway.

The more accurate statement, then: **taxes are a floor under demand for a currency, not the source of its value.** As long as the government does not over-issue, the floor is never touched; once it does, the floor cannot save it. MMT mistook a boundary condition for the essence.

The disagreement has practical consequences. If money's value comes from taxes, the state's control of money is complete and inflation is merely a “dial-setting” problem. If money's value comes from a chain of trust, then **trust can break** — and it breaks non-linearly: ten years of printing without incident, then in the eleventh everyone runs at once. That is Mises's “crack-up boom” ([[cantillon-inflation|Stage 4.3]]). MMT's framework has no event called “trust suddenly breaks,” because in its model demand for the currency is guaranteed by the tax code.

### ④ The “real-resource limit”: who judges? Cantillon effects and economic calculation

MMT's Proposition 4 is its most persuasive: money is not the constraint, resources are. Austrians agree with the second half entirely. The problem: **“idle resources” is an aggregate illusion.**

Three specific problems:

**First, the Cantillon effect.** The government spends 100 billion; the new money lands first with defense contractors, infrastructure firms, job-guarantee positions, and the subsidized industries of the day. They buy — housing, equipment, labor — at **old prices**, bidding those prices up; months later suppliers and landlords receive the money and spend it onward; a year later the pensioner and the fixed-salary worker notice prices have risen. **Before CPI sounds the alarm, distribution has already shifted:** early receivers won, late receivers lost. MMT says “no harm as long as there's no inflation,” but the harm precedes the inflation. This is [[cantillon-inflation|Stage 4.3]]: inflation is not a uniform rise in the water level; it is a wave rolling through in layers.

**Second, “idle” is a judgment, not a fact.** In 2009 the United States had many “idle” construction workers and machines. Were they resources government spending could put to work, or malinvestment from the housing boom that needed to be reallocated to other industries? The two readings lead to opposite policies. MMT treats “idle” as an observable aggregate (unemployment, capacity utilization); Austrians treat it as a **judgment about the structure of production** ([[bust-liquidation|Stage 5.3]]), and government is precisely the party least able to make it — it bears no losses and has no price signal telling it whether this machine tool should be restarted or scrapped.

**Third, economic calculation.** The job guarantee is the starkest version of the problem: the government hires five million people for “public service” — who decides what they do? If roads, which roads? If care work, caring for whom? Without prices and profit-and-loss ([[profit-loss|Stage 6.3]]), these questions can only be answered politically — and [[economics-of-state|Stage 8.4]] already showed how politics answers them: for organized voters, not for the most urgent uses. The fixed $15 wage is itself a price control ([[price-controls|Stage 8.2]]): it puts a floor under labor that crowds out private employers in low-productivity regions and is meaningless in high-productivity ones.

One concession Austrians should make: MMT's claim that “government spending may not inflate during mass unemployment” is possible **under specific conditions** (a large stock of homogeneous idle resources, sticky prices, a surge in money demand) — the United States of 1933 was probably close. The Austrian reply is that those conditions are usually themselves the consequence of prior credit expansion and obstructed liquidation, and that “spend first” postpones the liquidation for another round.

### ⑤ 2020–23: a natural experiment (approximate figures)

MMT reached the peak of its influence in spring 2020 — *The Deficit Myth* on the bestseller lists, the mainstream discussing “stop worrying about deficits,” and governments doing MMT-shaped things under the pandemic: Treasuries spending on a vast scale, central banks buying government bonds on a vast scale. It was as close to a natural experiment on the MMT prescription as we are likely to get. All numbers are approximate:

- **The United States:** federal deficits for fiscal 2020–21 combined **about $6 trillion** (roughly 25% of GDP), most of it indirectly monetized by Fed asset purchases; M2 grew **about 40%** from early 2020 to early 2022 — unprecedented in peacetime.
- **Prices:** CPI inflation rose from about 1% in 2020 to **about 9%** in June 2022, the highest since 1981. Core PCE stayed above 5% for more than a year.
- **The cooling:** not MMT's “raise taxes” — Congress raised no taxes and passed further spending in 2022; the cooling came from the Fed lifting rates from zero to above 5%. **MMT's brake (fiscal) was never pressed; the one it calls unimportant (monetary) was.**
- **The control group:** economies hit by the same supply-chain and energy shocks but with far more modest monetary and fiscal expansion saw markedly lower inflation — Switzerland peaked around 3.5%, Japan around 4%. **Same shock, different monetary expansion, different inflation** — exactly what MMT's framework does not predict.

MMT's defense has three strands, which deserve to be taken seriously: (1) the inflation came mainly from supply shocks (chips, shipping, energy), not demand; (2) 2020 was emergency relief, not the MMT-designed “resource-targeted” spending; (3) they never said “printing doesn't inflate,” only “not until idle resources are exhausted” — and in late 2021 they were.

The Austrian reply: (1) the supply shocks were real, but they explain **relative prices** (energy got dearer), not **all prices rising together** — the latter needs money; (2) “emergency relief” is exactly what MMT logic looks like in political reality: Congress will always find this time to be an emergency; (3) the “resources exhausted” signal arrives too late and too vaguely, and **nobody pressed the brake on it anyway** — which is section ④'s point: the constraint is a lagging indicator and the brake does not exist politically.

Austrians have their own bill to settle. Many Austrian writers predicted severe inflation after the quantitative easing of 2009–15, and **it did not come** ([[common-mistakes|Stage 14.4]] explains why: most of that money stayed in bank reserves and never entered spending). What differed in 2020 was that fiscal spending sent the money straight to households and firms — which is precisely the MMT prescription. **Austrian theory explains 2021–22, but the Austrian forecasting record of the 2010s is not flattering.** Both belong in the account.

### ⑥ “Cannot default” ≠ “cannot impoverish”: political economy and the Post-Keynesian bridge

MMT's central truth: a fiat government cannot be forced to default. Austrians grant it fully. But finish the sentence:

- The government pays its debt with newly printed money → repaid in nominal terms → but the money's purchasing power falls → creditors (pension funds, savers, foreign holders) get back less in real terms. **That is a default in a different form** — “not paying” has become “paying in paper worth less.” From 1946 to 1951 the United States inflated away roughly a third of the real value of its wartime debt; in accounting terms that was “paid in full,” in economic terms it was a tax on savers.
- Deeper: **between “cannot go broke” and “cannot impoverish” lies economic calculation.** The governments of Venezuela, Argentina and Zimbabwe never “ran out of money” — they kept spending. They impoverished their people not by defaulting but by making prices meaningless, wiping out savings, and leaving entrepreneurs unable to calculate ([[mises-1920|Stage 7.1]]). MMT says those are “extreme cases of runaway inflation, nothing to do with MMT”; Austrians say **they are the natural terminus of “spend until inflation” once the brake is not credible.**

Then political economy ([[economics-of-state|Stage 8.4]]). MMT casts “raise taxes to cool down” as the brake. But Buchanan and Wagner's 1977 critique of Keynesianism ([[vs-keynes|Stage 11.1]]) applies here with double force: **a theory that lets politicians “spend first and not ask where the money comes from” will be adopted in full; the half that requires politicians to “raise taxes when the economy overheats” will never be executed.** MMT proponents themselves concede that relying on Congress to raise taxes when inflation appears is “politically difficult” — which amounts to conceding that the framework has no brake. The Austrian conclusion is not “MMT people are villains” but that **any arrangement that entrusts monetary discipline to the self-restraint of a legislature loses that discipline at the first election** — which is exactly why Hayek proposed denationalizing money in 1976 ([[free-banking|Stage 9.4]]).

Finally, a bridge. Post-Keynesians (not only MMT) and Austrians genuinely stand together on two things: **endogenous money** (banks create it; the central bank cannot precisely control it) and **radical uncertainty** (the Lachmann–Shackle overlap of [[vs-keynes|Stage 11.1]]). Minsky's “stability breeds instability” — a long calm tempts people to lever up until they break — and ABCT's “a suppressed rate tempts people to lengthen projects until they break” are structurally the same story; Minsky locates the source in the endogenous impulses of private finance, Austrians in the central bank's price signal. **An honest Austrian should read Minsky; an honest Post-Keynesian should read Mises.** [[case-2008-qe|Stage 13.4]] sets 2008 and 2021–23 side by side under both frameworks; [[stablecoins-cbdc|Stage 17.3]] asks whether “taxes drive money” survives once stablecoins and private digital currencies begin competing with fiat.

One sentence to close: **MMT keeps the books of the fiat system correctly, then leaps from “the government cannot run out of money” to “the government should spend until inflation” — skipping three things on the way: money's value comes from trust, not the tax code (the regression theorem); the path of new money decides who wins and who loses (Cantillon); and “idle resources” is a judgment that only prices and entrepreneurs can make, not an aggregate visible from the Capitol (economic calculation).**

## @analogy
Imagine a buffet restaurant that prints its own meal tickets.

**The MMT manager** says: we can never “run out of tickets” — if we want more people fed, we print more. The only limit is the kitchen's capacity: as long as there are empty tables and idle cooks, printing tickets just gets more people fed, and nothing goes wrong; when the kitchen gets swamped and the line gets long, we withdraw some tickets (“tax”). On the **accounting of tickets**, this is entirely correct.

The Austrian waiter sees a different picture:

First, **who gets the newly printed tickets?** The manager's friends, the regulars, the people at the front of the line. They go in first and take the best steak; those who get tickets later find only salad. The aggregate — “total tickets vs kitchen capacity” — shows nothing, but **who got the steak has already changed.**

Second, **who decides the kitchen “still has capacity”?** The manager stands at the door counting empty tables — but what he cannot see is that three of those tables are empty because of last week's failed “seafood buffet” experiment and have not yet been reset. Should they be “put to use” or torn down and re-laid? Only the people actually cooking, watching which dishes come back uneaten, know.

Third, “withdraw tickets when the line gets long” — **who will do that unpopular thing?** The manager's bonus depends on the crowd at the door. When the line really does lengthen, he will say “the suppliers were slow with deliveries” and print another batch.

Fourth, the restaurant can never “default on tickets” — true. But if a ticket goes from buying a steak to buying a slice of bread, the ticket-holder **has already been defaulted on**; nobody just uses the word.

Finally, the job guarantee: the manager announces “anyone who wants work can work at the restaurant at a fixed hourly rate.” Five hundred people show up. What do they do? Wipe tables — which ones? Chop vegetables — how many? Without an order slip (a price), the head cook can only guess — and the cost of guessing wrong is spread across everyone holding a ticket.

## @misconceptions
- **“MMT advocates unlimited money printing.”** — A straw man. MMT explicitly names inflation as the constraint and prescribes taxes to cool the economy. The Austrian critique targets more precise points: the constraint is a lagging indicator (CPI) and the damage precedes it (Cantillon effects); “idle resources” must be judged, not observed; and the “raise taxes” brake has never been pressed politically.
- **“Austrians deny that banks create money or that governments ‘spend first, tax later.’”** — The reverse: Mises showed in 1912 that banks create fiduciary media from nothing; Austrians fully accept the operating mechanics of fiat money, on which MMT is more accurate than most textbooks. What Austrians deny is the leap from “the books are right” to “deficits don't matter.”
- **“Taxes create demand for money, so the state has full control over its value.”** — Taxes are a floor under demand, not the source of value. Weimar, Zimbabwe, Venezuela, and the milder cases of Turkey and Argentina all show tax-receivable currencies abandoned by the market as stores of value. The regression theorem says value rests on a chain of trust, and the chain can break — non-linearly.
- **“The 2021–22 inflation was entirely supply shocks, nothing to do with money.”** — Supply shocks explain relative prices (energy dearer), not all prices rising together. Economies facing the same shocks with modest monetary expansion (Switzerland about 3.5%, Japan about 4%) inflated far less than the U.S. (about 9%). The honest statement: both mattered, and monetary expansion set how far the shock was amplified. Austrians must also concede their failed inflation calls of the 2010s.
- **“Austrians and Post-Keynesians are sworn enemies.”** — On endogenous money and radical uncertainty they stand together; Minsky's “stability breeds instability” is structurally akin to ABCT, differing only in where it locates the source (private finance's endogenous impulses vs the central bank's price signal). The disagreements are over the source of money's value, whether “idle resources” can be judged centrally, and whether monetary discipline can be entrusted to legislative self-restraint.

   - [ ] Money's value comes from its gold content
   > Taxes can **sustain** a minimum use of an existing money; they cannot **create** its value. Turks and Argentines pay taxes in local currency and save in dollars — taxes cannot anchor the store-of-value function.

2. MMT says “as long as idle resources exist, government spending will not inflate.” What is the core Austrian rebuttal?
   - [ ] Idle resources never exist
   - [x] Inflation is a lagging indicator; under Cantillon effects distribution and relative prices shift before CPI alarms; “idle” is a judgment about the structure of production (activate or liquidate?), not an observable aggregate; and government is the party least able to make that judgment
   - [ ] Government spending always causes hyperinflation
   - [ ] Austrians agree with MMT on this
   > Were the idle construction workers of 2009 “resources to activate” or “malinvestment from the last boom”? The two readings lead to opposite policies, and without prices and profit-and-loss the government cannot tell — the calculation problem of [[mises-1920|Stage 7.1]] again.

3. Regarding the 2020–23 natural experiment, which statement is accurate?
   - [ ] U.S. inflation was entirely a supply shock, unrelated to $6 trillion of deficits and about 40% M2 growth
   - [ ] MMT's fiscal brake (higher taxes) was applied in time and controlled inflation
   - [x] Under the same supply shocks, Switzerland and Japan with modest monetary expansion inflated far less; the cooling came from Fed rate rises, not congressional tax rises — MMT's brake was never used
   - [ ] Every Austrian inflation forecast of the 2010s came true
   > Supply shocks explain relative prices, not a general rise; the control group shows monetary expansion set how far the shock was amplified. Austrians must also concede that their post-2009 QE inflation forecasts failed (the money stayed in reserves).

4. How do Austrians regard the claim “a fiat government cannot default”?
   - [ ] They deny it
   - [ ] They accept it and agree that deficits therefore do not matter
   - [x] They accept that nominal default is impossible but note that paying in depreciated money is default in another form (the U.S. inflated away about a third of its war debt in 1946–51); between “cannot go broke” and “cannot impoverish” lies economic calculation
   - [ ] They hold that governments cannot default only under a gold standard
   > Venezuela's government never “ran out of money”; it impoverished people by making prices meaningless and wiping out savings. “Cannot default” is an operational fact; “cannot impoverish” is an economic claim — MMT conflates them.

5. Where is the genuine overlap between Austrians and Post-Keynesians (including MMT)?
   - [ ] Both advocate a job guarantee
   - [ ] Both hold that taxes create money
   - [ ] Both advocate precise central-bank control of monetary aggregates
   - [x] Endogenous money (bank lending creates deposits) and radical uncertainty; Minsky's “stability breeds instability” is structurally akin to ABCT, differing only in where it locates the cycle's source
   > Mises showed in 1912 that banks create fiduciary media from nothing; Lachmann and Shackle overlap on uncertainty ([[vs-keynes|Stage 11.1]]). An honest Austrian reads Minsky; an honest Post-Keynesian reads Mises.
