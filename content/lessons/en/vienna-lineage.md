# From Vienna to Today: A 150-Year Lineage

## @hook
A school of thought is not a book; it is a **chain of people teaching people**. Menger taught Wieser and Böhm-Bawerk; Mises sat in Böhm-Bawerk's seminar; Hayek sat in Mises's private seminar; Hayek taught Lachmann in London; Mises taught Rothbard and Kirzner in New York; and in 1974 those three re-lit a nearly extinguished tradition in a small Vermont town. This lesson compresses 150 years into one timeline: who wrote what and when, who passed the flame to whom, how the school almost vanished and how it came back, and how it reached China. **Know the lineage and you know which person's which book every concept grew out of.**

## @intuition
One of the cheapest ways to learn a discipline is to know **its family tree**: who was the first generation, who learned from whom, where the branches split. The Austrian School's tree is unusually legible — because it has always been small, small enough that every generation can be counted by name, and small enough that it very nearly died.

The 150 years in one sentence: **three Viennese generations (1871–1934) → exile and near-extinction (1934–1974) → American revival (1974 to now) → global spread, including China (1990s to now).**

**First generation: Menger** (1840–1921). In 1871 he published *Principles of Economics*, a slim book that moved value out of objects and into minds ([[diamonds-water|Stage 1.1]]). In 1883 he started the Methodenstreit ([[what-is-austrian|Stage 0.2]]) and fixed the school's method. In 1892 he wrote *On the Origin of Money*: money was not invented by kings but evolved from the most saleable commodity in the market ([[origin-of-money|Stage 4.1]]). Menger himself was cautious and, in later life, silent — he revised a second edition of the *Principles* for thirty years and never published it.

**Second generation: Böhm-Bawerk and Wieser** (both born 1851, and brothers-in-law). Böhm-Bawerk wrote the three-volume *Capital and Interest* (1884–1889), putting **time** into economics: interest is not the capitalist's exploitation but the price of preferring the present to the future ([[time-preference|Stage 3.1]]); he also served three times as Austria's finance minister. Wieser coined the word “marginal utility” (Grenznutzen) and developed the theories of **opportunity cost** and **imputation** ([[opportunity-cost|Stage 1.4]], [[diamonds-water|Stage 1.1]]). Between them they turned Menger's intuitions into a teachable theory.

**Third and fourth generations: Mises and Hayek.** Mises (1881–1973) extended marginal-utility theory to money in 1912 (*The Theory of Money and Credit*) and in 1920 wrote the famous essay: without private property there are no prices, without prices there is no economic calculation, therefore socialism cannot function ([[mises-1920|Stage 7.1]]). From 1920 to 1934 he ran a **private seminar** in his office at the Vienna Chamber of Commerce — not a university course, but an evening gathering every two weeks — and out of it came Hayek, Machlup, Haberler, Morgenstern and others. Hayek (1899–1992) went to the London School of Economics in 1931, crossed swords with Keynes, became world-famous with *The Road to Serfdom* in 1944, and won the Nobel Prize in 1974.

Then came **thirty years of near-disappearance**. In 1934 Mises left Vienna for Geneva; in 1938 the Nazis entered Austria and the school ceased to exist in Europe as a living body. In 1940 Mises escaped to New York, where no university would give him a regular chair; he taught an evening seminar at New York University as a “visiting professor” from 1945 to 1969, his salary paid by private foundations. Hayek went to Chicago in 1950 — not to the economics department but to the Committee on Social Thought. The Keynesian revolution swept the world, and by the late 1950s “the Austrian School” was, to most economists, a historical term.

The **revival** happened in very concrete steps. In 1949 a 23-year-old New Yorker named Murray Rothbard walked into Mises's seminar. In 1957 a young man from South Africa, Israel Kirzner, took his doctorate under Mises. The German-born Ludwig Lachmann had studied under Hayek in London in the 1930s and later moved to Johannesburg. **In June 1974** these three lectured for a week to about 50 young scholars in a Vermont village called South Royalton — the event later counted as the start of the “Austrian revival.” **That October** the news came that Hayek had won the Nobel Prize. In 1982 the Mises Institute was founded in Auburn, Alabama; in the 1980s George Mason University built a second center. After that comes today's generation: Huerta de Soto in Madrid, Garrison at Auburn, Salerno, Hoppe, Hülsmann and Klein in the Mises Institute orbit, Boettke at George Mason.

Why is the line worth learning? Because **every concept has a birthplace.** When you later meet “time preference,” you will know it comes from Böhm-Bawerk 1889; “economic calculation,” from Mises 1920; “the knowledge problem,” from Hayek 1937 and 1945; “entrepreneurial alertness,” from Kirzner 1973. The lineage is the address book of the concepts.

**In this lesson we break it into five pieces:**

- **① Three Viennese generations (1871–1914): Menger, Böhm-Bawerk, Wieser**
- **② Mises and Hayek (1912–1950): from the private seminar to London and New York**
- **③ Near-extinction (1934–1974): exile, the Keynesian revolution and one evening seminar**
- **④ Revival (1974 to now): South Royalton, the Mises Institute, George Mason and today's people**
- **⑤ Reaching China: translations, readers and sympathizers**

## @mechanics
### ① Three Viennese generations (1871–1914): Menger, Böhm-Bawerk, Wieser

**Carl Menger (1840–1921).** Born in Galicia (now southern Poland), trained in law in Vienna, worked as a financial journalist. *Principles of Economics* in 1871; professor at the University of Vienna from 1873; tutor in economics to Crown Prince Rudolf in 1876–78, accompanying him on travels across Europe. In 1883 the *Investigations into the Method of the Social Sciences* opened the Methodenstreit; in 1884 *The Errors of Historicism* answered Schmoller. *On the Origin of Money* in 1892. He retired in 1903 and published almost nothing afterward — he kept revising a second edition of the *Principles* that was never finished (his son Karl Menger, the famous mathematician, edited the manuscript for publication in 1923).

Menger left three things: **subjective value and marginal utility** ([[diamonds-water|Stage 1.1]]); **the orders of goods and imputation** (value flows upward from consumer goods to producer goods); and **the spontaneous evolution of institutions** (money, law and language are “the result of human action, but not of human design,” [[spontaneous-order|Stage 7.4]]). The third drew little attention in his lifetime and later became the theme of the whole second half of Hayek's career.

**Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk (1851–1914).** A follower of Menger rather than a student — he read the *Principles* and sought Menger out. The three volumes of *Capital and Interest*: volume one, *History and Critique of Interest Theories* (1884), demolishes every previous theory of interest one by one; volume two, *The Positive Theory of Capital* (1889), gives his own answer — **interest arises because people value present goods more than future goods**, and **roundabout production** is more productive but requires waiting ([[time-preference|Stage 3.1]], [[roundabout-production|Stage 3.2]]). His *Karl Marx and the Close of His System* (1896) remains one of the best-known critiques of the labor theory of value ([[vs-marx-behavioral|Stage 11.5]]). He served three times as Austria's finance minister (1895, 1897–98, 1900–04), known for balanced budgets and defense of the gold standard. From 1905 he ran a seminar at the University of Vienna attended by Mises, Schumpeter, Otto Bauer and Hilferding — one room holding the future head of the Austrian School, the future theorist of “creative destruction,” the future leader of Austrian social democracy and the future German finance minister.

**Friedrich von Wieser (1851–1926).** Böhm-Bawerk's brother-in-law and classmate. He coined “marginal utility” in 1884; his *Natural Value* (1889) systematized **imputation** — the value of factors of production is imputed back from the value of their products; and he originated the concept of **opportunity cost** (he called it “alternative cost”): the cost of a thing is the other use it displaces ([[opportunity-cost|Stage 1.4]]). *Social Economics* in 1914. Austrian minister of commerce in 1917–18. Politically he stood closer to “social liberalism” than the other two, more tolerant of a role for the state — an early counterexample to “Austrian = libertarian” ([[what-is-austrian|Stage 0.2]]). Among his students at Vienna was a young man named Hayek.

### ② Mises and Hayek (1912–1950): from the private seminar to London and New York

**Ludwig von Mises (1881–1973).** Born in Lemberg (now Lviv, Ukraine); doctorate in law from Vienna (1906); member of Böhm-Bawerk's seminar. From 1909 an economic adviser at the Vienna Chamber of Commerce — his actual job for most of his life; the university gave him only an unpaid “extraordinary professor” title. Three foundational works: **The Theory of Money and Credit (1912)**, which extended marginal utility to money, stated the regression theorem ([[origin-of-money|Stage 4.1]]) and first tied credit expansion to the business cycle ([[abct-one-picture|Stage 5.1]]); **the 1920 essay “Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth,”** arguing that without prices there is no economic calculation ([[mises-1920|Stage 7.1]]), expanded in 1922 into *Socialism*; and **Human Action (1949)**, which re-derives the whole of economics from praxeology ([[action-axiom|Stage 2.1]], [[read-human-action|Stage 12.2]]).

The **Mises private seminar** of 1920–1934 deserves its own paragraph: every other Friday evening in his office at the Chamber, no credit, no exams, continued afterward at a café. Participants included Hayek, Machlup, Haberler, Morgenstern, Schütz (phenomenological sociology), Feigl (philosophy) and Kaufmann (law). Almost the entire fourth generation of the school came out of that room. In 1927 Mises and Hayek co-founded the Austrian Institute for Business Cycle Research, with Hayek as director.

**Friedrich von Hayek (1899–1992).** Doctorates in law and political science from Vienna (1921, 1923); student of Wieser; member of Mises's seminar. *Monetary Theory and the Trade Cycle* in 1929. **In 1931**, at Robbins's invitation, he lectured at the London School of Economics — the lectures became *Prices and Production* — and accepted a chair there. In 1931–32 he fought a fierce exchange with Keynes over the *Treatise on Money* and another with Sraffa: round one of Austrians versus Keynes ([[vs-keynes|Stage 11.1]]). When Keynes's *General Theory* appeared in 1936 Hayek did not answer it, a silence he later called a lifelong regret. **“Economics and Knowledge” (1937)** was his turning point: from cycle theory toward “how is knowledge used in society when it is dispersed?” ([[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]]); **“The Use of Knowledge in Society” (1945)** made that theme one of the most-cited papers in the history of economics. **The Road to Serfdom (1944)** made him famous overnight — and, in his colleagues' eyes, “no longer an economist.” In 1947 he convened the Mont Pelerin Society. In 1950 he moved to the Committee on Social Thought at the University of Chicago (the economics department did not hire him). *The Constitution of Liberty* (1960), *Law, Legislation and Liberty* (1973–79), *Denationalisation of Money* (1976, [[free-banking|Stage 9.4]]), *The Fatal Conceit* (1988). **The 1974 Nobel Prize in economics** — shared with Sweden's Gunnar Myrdal, a social democrat, a pairing that pleased neither side at the time.

The difference between Mises and Hayek became the most important fork inside the school ([[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]]): Mises stresses a priori deduction and economic calculation, Hayek stresses dispersed knowledge and spontaneous order; Mises is a rationalist, Hayek an evolutionist. They respected each other, but they walked different roads.

### ③ Near-extinction (1934–1974): exile, the Keynesian revolution and one evening seminar

In 1934 Mises took a post at the Graduate Institute in Geneva; in 1938, after the Anschluss, the Gestapo seized his library and papers in Vienna (they were rediscovered in a Moscow archive in the 1990s). In 1940 he and his wife reached New York by way of Portugal — he was 59, had little experience writing academic English, and no university would hire him. **From 1945 to 1969 he taught an evening seminar at NYU's business school as a “visiting professor,”** his salary paid by the William Volker Fund and other private donors. That seminar and *Human Action* (1949) were the school's only embers for thirty years.

In the same period most of the fourth generation moved into the mainstream: Machlup (Princeton), Haberler (Harvard), Morgenstern (Princeton, co-author with von Neumann of *Theory of Games and Economic Behavior*). They became distinguished economists but were no longer “Austrian.” Schumpeter, though a product of Böhm-Bawerk's seminar, is generally not counted as one either. Keynes's *General Theory* (1936) and Samuelson's textbook (1948) remade the discipline; by the late 1950s macroeconomics meant Keynesianism, microeconomics meant mathematical neoclassicism, and the Austrian School was a footnote.

To be fair: this was not only outside pressure. The school bore responsibility too — Mises's absolutism on method kept many sympathizers at a distance; after turning to political philosophy Hayek published little economics for decades; the school had no journal, no doctoral program, nowhere to give a young scholar a degree. **A school that cannot offer the young a career path disappears within a generation** — and that very nearly happened.

### ④ Revival (1974 to now): South Royalton, the Mises Institute, George Mason and today's people

The revival rested on three people:

- **Murray Rothbard (1926–1995).** Columbia PhD; attended Mises's seminar from 1949. **Man, Economy, and State (1962)** rewrote *Human Action* as a systematic treatise usable as a textbook, and pushed the theories of monopoly and the state further than Mises had; **America's Great Depression (1963)** explained 1929 with ABCT ([[case-great-depression|Stage 13.2]]). *For a New Liberty* (1973) and *The Ethics of Liberty* (1982) made him the standard-bearer of anarcho-capitalism. He helped found the Mises Institute in 1982 and taught at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas from 1986.
- **Israel Kirzner (born 1930).** Doctorate at NYU under Mises in 1957. *The Economic Point of View* (1960); **Competition and Entrepreneurship (1973)** — the engine of the market process is the entrepreneur's **alertness** to unnoticed opportunities ([[entrepreneur-alertness|Stage 6.1]]). He stayed at NYU his whole career and, in a gentle, academic register, kept the Austrian School within the mainstream's field of view.
- **Ludwig Lachmann (1906–1990).** Born in Berlin; studied under Hayek at LSE in the 1930s; from 1948 at the University of the Witwatersrand in South Africa. **Capital and Its Structure (1956)** stresses the heterogeneity of capital and the entrepreneur's recombination of it ([[heterogeneous-capital|Stage 3.4]]); from the 1970s he pressed “radical subjectivism” and the unpredictability of expectations — the school's most restless branch. From 1975 to 1987 he spent a term each year at NYU.

**June 1974, the South Royalton conference.** The Institute for Humane Studies ran a week-long summer school in a Vermont village; the three lecturers were Rothbard, Kirzner and Lachmann, and the audience of about 50 graduate students and young faculty contained almost the entire sixth generation of the school. The lectures were published in 1976 as *The Foundations of Modern Austrian Economics*. **Four months later Hayek won the Nobel Prize**, handing the revival an unexpected legitimacy.

**In 1982 the Mises Institute** was founded in Auburn, Alabama (founder Lew Rockwell; Rothbard as academic vice president; Mises's widow Margit as chairman), built around publishing, a summer university and journals (the *Quarterly Journal of Austrian Economics* from 1998). **From the 1980s George Mason University** became the other center: the Center for the Study of Market Processes, the *Review of Austrian Economics*, a doctoral program — more academic in style and more engaged with the mainstream (Lavoie, Vaughn, later Boettke, Leeson, Coyne). There is real tension between the two centers on method and politics — [[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]] goes into it. The Kirzner–Rizzo colloquium at NYU continues to this day.

**Today's generation** (an incomplete list): Jesús Huerta de Soto (Rey Juan Carlos University, Madrid; *Money, Bank Credit, and Economic Cycles*, 1998; the center of European Austrian economics); Roger Garrison (Auburn University; *Time and Money*, 2001, which drew ABCT as three diagrams, [[garrison-macro|Stage 10.1]]); Joseph Salerno (Pace University; academic vice president of the Mises Institute; monetary theory); Hans-Hermann Hoppe (Las Vegas; *Democracy: The God That Failed*, 2001; argumentation ethics); Jörg Guido Hülsmann (University of Angers, France; the 2007 Mises biography); Peter Boettke (George Mason; editor of the *Review of Austrian Economics*); Peter Klein (Baylor University; the theory of entrepreneurial judgment, [[entrepreneur-alertness|Stage 6.1]]). Add the free-banking branch of Lawrence White and George Selgin ([[free-banking|Stage 9.4]]).

Honestly: the school is still small. It has almost no presence in top economics departments, is rare in the top-five journals, and has only a handful of doctoral programs. It thrives on its own institutions, the internet, and a large readership — an achievement, and also the career reality [[career-paths|Stage 14.5]] discusses.

### ⑤ Reaching China: translations, readers and sympathizers

The Austrian School entered the Chinese-speaking world mainly through **translations**. Hayek arrived first: *The Road to Serfdom* had a restricted-circulation Chinese translation as early as the 1960s and a publicly available new translation in 1997; *The Constitution of Liberty*, *Law, Legislation and Liberty* and *The Fatal Conceit* followed from the late 1990s through the 2000s, and in the 1990s Hayek was for a time among the most-discussed Western thinkers in Chinese intellectual circles. Mises came later: the earliest Chinese translation of *Human Action* was made in the 1970s by the Taiwanese economist Hsia Tao-ping (夏道平), who also translated other Mises works; mainland readers reached Mises through these and newer translations from the 2000s onward, and *Socialism*, *The Theory of Money and Credit* and *Liberalism* have since appeared in Chinese. Rothbard's *Man, Economy, and State* and *America's Great Depression*, Kirzner's *Competition and Entrepreneurship* and Huerta de Soto's *Money, Bank Credit, and Economic Cycles* were published in Chinese during the 2000s and 2010s.

**Sympathizers and scholars.** Mainstream economics in China is neoclassical, but the Austrian School has a number of open sympathizers. The best known is Zhang Weiying (张维迎) of Peking University, who has said publicly on several occasions that his thinking moved from neoclassical economics toward the Mises–Hayek line, especially on entrepreneurship, the market process and the knowledge problem; his books such as *The Logic of the Market* and *The Power of Ideas* brought these themes to a general readership. Beyond him there is a body of scholars who have long studied Hayek's thought (interest in Hayek among legal and political theorists has if anything exceeded that among economists), and a community of translators and reading groups that keeps bringing Austrian works into Chinese. In the internet era the school's spread in Chinese has run mainly through translations, public accounts, podcasts and online communities rather than university courses — much as in the United States.

A caveat: this section reports only checkable facts — who translated what, who has publicly expressed sympathy — and does not guess at anyone's complete position. The reception of Austrian economics in China is still unfolding, and there is no settled verdict.

Compress the lesson into one sentence: **Menger lit the fire, Böhm-Bawerk and Wieser fed it, Mises and Hayek carried it out of Vienna, it nearly went out in the 1950s, Rothbard, Kirzner and Lachmann re-lit it in Vermont in 1974, and today it burns in Auburn, Fairfax, Madrid, Angers and on the desks of countless readers.** The next lesson ([[five-pillars|Stage 0.4]]) sorts what these people left behind into five pillars.

## @analogy
Think of a school of thought as **a craft passed from master to apprentice** — say, an endangered way of making a musical instrument.

The first master (Menger) built the first instrument in 1871 and wrote down the plans (the *Principles*). Two apprentices (Böhm-Bawerk and Wieser) turned the plans into a full sequence of steps: one took charge of the “time” step (interest), the other of the “cost” step (opportunity cost and imputation). The third generation (Mises) extended the craft to a new material (money) and ran an evening apprentice class in his own workshop (the private seminar); the fourth (Hayek) came out of that class and carried the craft to London.

Then war came. The workshop was seized, the master fled to New York, and could only teach a few apprentices at night in a borrowed classroom — no salary, no degrees to award. The rest of the trade switched to assembly lines (Keynesianism and mathematical neoclassicism) and the handcraft was written off as obsolete. By the 1960s the people still seriously practicing it would have fit in one classroom.

In 1974 three apprentices (Rothbard, Kirzner, Lachmann) held a week of public classes in a Vermont village and fifty young people came. That same year the old master's fellow apprentice (Hayek) won a great international prize. After that the craft acquired its own guild (the Mises Institute), its own school (George Mason), its own journals — and later reached Madrid, Angers, and readers in China.

That is the point of learning the lineage: **every tool in your hand was made by a particular master in a particular year.** When you use it, you know what problem it was built to solve — and where its weaknesses lie, because the person who made it wrote about them.

## @misconceptions
- **“The Austrian School is the thought of Mises and Hayek.”** — They are the third and fourth generations. Before them came Menger (subjective value, the origin of money), Böhm-Bawerk (time and interest) and Wieser (opportunity cost, imputation); after them Rothbard, Kirzner, Lachmann and today's generation. And Mises and Hayek themselves disagreed on fundamentals (a priori deduction vs the evolution of knowledge, [[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]]).
- **“Hayek's Nobel Prize caused the Austrian revival.”** — Chronologically, the South Royalton conference (June 1974) came before the prize announcement (October 1974), and the organizing work (the Institute for Humane Studies; Rothbard, Kirzner, Lachmann) had begun well before. The prize supplied legitimacy and attention, not the cause. Hayek was honored chiefly for cycle theory and monetary economics, and the committee paired him with the social democrat Myrdal — which pleased neither camp.
- **“Austrians lost to Keynes in the 1930s and have been losers ever since.”** — The 1930s debates did end with Keynes dominating the discipline, but the school's near-disappearance had more causes: exile, no journals or doctoral programs, Mises's methodological absolutism, Hayek's turn to political philosophy. After 1974 it rebuilt institutions; today it has journals, doctoral programs and a global community — small, but complete. “Marginal” and “defeated” are not the same thing.
- **“Mises was a regular professor at NYU.”** — From 1945 to 1969 his status at NYU was visiting professor, with his salary paid by private foundations such as the Volker Fund; the university never gave him a regular chair. In Vienna, too, he held only an unpaid extraordinary professorship; his actual job was economic adviser at the Chamber of Commerce. The detail shows how marginal the school was inside the academic establishment.
- **“Schumpeter, Machlup and Morgenstern were Austrian economists.”** — All were products of Vienna (Schumpeter of Böhm-Bawerk's seminar, the other two of Mises's), but all took mainstream paths: Schumpeter fused creative destruction with an equilibrium framework, Machlup and Haberler became mainstream international economists, Morgenstern founded game theory with von Neumann. “Viennese origin” and “Austrian method” are two different things.

   - [ ] Menger, Böhm-Bawerk and Wieser
   > Mises had died in 1973 and Hayek was in Europe. Rothbard (student at Mises's NYU seminar), Kirzner (doctorate under Mises) and Lachmann (Hayek's student in London) lectured for a week to about 50 young scholars — the event later counted as the start of the revival.

3. Which of the following was NOT a cause of the school's near-extinction in 1934–1974?
   - [ ] Mises's and Hayek's exile ended the school's living presence in Vienna
   - [ ] The Keynesian revolution and mathematical neoclassicism remade the discipline, leaving Austrians as a footnote
   - [ ] The school had no journal or doctoral program, so the young had no career path
   - [x] The school dissolved immediately upon Menger's death in 1921
   > When Menger died, Böhm-Bawerk was already gone, Wieser was still active and Mises was in his prime; the school did not dissolve. The near-extinction came after 1934, caused by exile, the Keynesian revolution, the lack of institutions, Mises's methodological absolutism and Hayek's turn to political philosophy.

4. Which description of Mises's private seminar (1920–1934) is correct?
   - [x] It met every two weeks in Mises's office at the Chamber of Commerce, with no credit or exams, and nearly the whole fourth generation — Hayek, Machlup, Haberler, Morgenstern — came out of it
   - [ ] It was a formal doctoral course at the University of Vienna
   - [ ] It was run by Böhm-Bawerk, with Mises as a student
   - [ ] It was held at NYU, with Rothbard as a student
   > Mises's actual job was economic adviser at the Chamber; the university gave him only an unpaid extraordinary professorship. The private seminar was an evening gathering in his office, continued afterward at a café. The NYU seminar was a different one, from 1945 to 1969.

5. By what main route did Austrian economics reach the Chinese-speaking world?
   - [ ] Through economics courses at Chinese universities
   - [x] Mainly through translations (Hayek first, then Mises, Rothbard, Kirzner and Huerta de Soto), plus open sympathizers, scholars, translators and online communities
   - [ ] Through cultural-exchange programs of the Austrian government
   - [ ] It has never been translated into Chinese
   > Hayek's Road to Serfdom and other works were widely discussed in the 1990s; Human Action was translated in Taiwan by Hsia Tao-ping, and mainland readers later reached Mises through several translations; scholars such as Zhang Weiying have said publicly that they moved from neoclassical economics toward the Mises–Hayek line. The spread ran through translations and communities rather than university courses — much as in the United States.
