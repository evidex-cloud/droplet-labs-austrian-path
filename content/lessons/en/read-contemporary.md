# Contemporary Classics: Kirzner, Lachmann, Garrison, Huerta de Soto, Hoppe

## @hook
Mises died in 1973, Hayek in 1992, Rothbard in 1995. Since then the Austrian School has had no single center; it has three branches, each with its own classics. This lesson gives you a **contemporary reading list**: Kirzner and Lachmann (market process and capital), Garrison (macro), Huerta de Soto and Hülsmann (money and banking), Hoppe and Salerno (method, politics and monetary history), Boettke, Klein and Foss, Lavoie (GMU and the theory of the firm) — what each contributes, how to read it, and where it pushes the school; plus the journals and free resources, and a **fair** account of the three branches (Mises Institute / George Mason / Kirznerian). When you finish, you should be able to build your own path of six to ten books rather than be led by any one branch's list.

## @intuition
First, the “three branches,” because they explain the bias of every Austrian reading list you will find online.

**Branch one: the Mises Institute (Auburn).** Founded in 1982 by Lew Rockwell with Rothbard's backing. Positions: the Mises–Rothbard a priori deductive method, the pure time-preference theory of interest, 100-percent reserves, and reservations about Hayek's “knowledge” argument (Salerno's “dehomogenization”). People: Salerno, Hoppe, Hülsmann, Huerta de Soto, Herbener, Murphy. Journal: the *Quarterly Journal of Austrian Economics* (QJAE). Strengths: a complete system, deep monetary and banking theory, an enormous free library (mises.org). Weaknesses: polemical, politically colored, and so visible that outsiders often equate it with “the Austrian School,” which is unfair to the other branches.

**Branch two: George Mason University (GMU, Fairfax).** Its source is the late-1970s Austrian program at New York University (Kirzner, Lachmann, O'Driscoll, Rizzo), which moved to GMU in the 1980s with Peter Boettke as its standard-bearer. Positions: Hayek's knowledge and spontaneous order, Lachmann's subjective expectations and radical uncertainty, dialogue with new institutional economics (North, Ostrom) and public choice (Buchanan), a more open method (empirical case studies, experiments, analytic narratives). People: Boettke, Coyne, Leeson, Storr, Horwitz, Lavoie (deceased). Journal: the *Review of Austrian Economics* (RAE). Strengths: access to mainstream academia, institutional analysis, empirical work. Weaknesses: critics say it has become too “mainstream,” diluting Mises's apriorism and monetary theory.

**Branch three: the Kirznerians.** Not an institution but the people working from Kirzner's theory of entrepreneurial alertness — with heirs both at GMU and in business schools, and the place where Austrian economics overlaps most with mainstream (management-school) “entrepreneurship theory.” People: Kirzner himself, Sautet, and Klein and Foss (who are in fact critics, arguing for “judgment” rather than “alertness”).

**To be fair:** the three branches agree on about 80 percent of everything (subjective value, praxeology, prices as knowledge, opposition to intervention and central banks); the disagreements cluster on method (a priori vs empirical elements), interest (PTPT vs broader), banking (100 percent vs free banking) and politics (anarcho-capitalism vs classical liberalism). [[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]] handles those head-on. This lesson's list **deliberately crosses branches** — because your goal is to think like an Austrian economist, not to join a faction.

Second: **contemporary books are read differently from the classics.** Menger, Mises, Hayek and Rothbard are “foundation texts,” read to understand the whole building; contemporary books are “problem texts,” each solving one problem — Kirzner solves “why markets tend toward equilibrium,” Lachmann “why they may not,” Garrison “how to draw Austrian macro,” Huerta de Soto “how to analyze bank credit in law and economics at once,” Hoppe “how the method defends itself,” Klein and Foss “what a firm is.” So **choose contemporary books by problem, not by author**.

Third: **the free resources are astonishing.** The mises.org library has free PDFs of nearly every Austrian classic (all the major works of Mises, Rothbard, Menger and Böhm-Bawerk included); Econlib has Hayek, Bastiat and Smith in full; QJAE is free. You can read most of this lesson's list without spending anything.

**In this lesson we break it into five pieces:**

- **① Market process and capital: Kirzner, Lachmann, Lavoie**
- **② Macro, money and banking: Garrison, Huerta de Soto, Hülsmann, Salerno**
- **③ Method, politics and the firm: Hoppe, Boettke, Klein and Foss**
- **④ Journals, biographies and free resources: where to follow current research**
- **⑤ A fair map of the three branches, and how to build your own path**

## @mechanics
### ① Market process and capital: Kirzner, Lachmann, Lavoie

**Kirzner, *Competition and Entrepreneurship* (1973).** About 250 pages, the most influential contemporary Austrian book. The problem: neoclassical price theory assumes the market is already in equilibrium — so how is equilibrium **reached**? Kirzner's answer is **entrepreneurial alertness**: noticing price discrepancies nobody else has seen (the same good selling for 8 here and 10 there), buying low and selling high, and thereby pushing prices toward consistency. The entrepreneur needs no capital and bears no risk; he needs only to “notice.” Competition is this process of discovery, and “monopoly” should be defined by **ownership of resources**, not market share (where he parts from Rothbard, [[monopoly-question|Stage 6.4]]). **How to read:** Chapters 1–3 essential; read Chapter 4 on monopoly alongside Rothbard's *MES* Chapter 10; Chapter 6's defense of advertising and product differentiation still bites. **Controversy:** Kirzner's entrepreneur “bears no uncertainty,” which Misesians (Salerno, Klein) criticize as dropping Mises's “judgment” — the debate of [[entrepreneur-alertness|Stage 6.1]].

**Kirzner, *The Meaning of Market Process* (1992).** A collection containing his most mature answer to the “tendency to equilibrium” question and his replies to Lachmann and the mainstream. The key essays are “The Meaning of Market Process” and “Equilibrium versus Market Process.” **How to read:** Chapters 1–3; it is the methodological complement to the 1973 book. Also read his 1997 survey in the *Journal of Economic Literature*, “Entrepreneurial Discovery and the Competitive Market Process: An Austrian Approach,” 25 pages — one of the best introductions Austrians have written for the mainstream.

**Lachmann, *Capital and Its Structure* (1956).** About 130 pages, underrated for decades and rediscovered in the 1970s. The problem: capital is not an aggregate but a set of **heterogeneous, complementary, specific** capital goods that entrepreneurs must assemble into “capital combinations” (the jigsaw of [[heterogeneous-capital|Stage 3.4]]); every change in expectations breaks part of the combination and forces reassembly. The book makes the insights of Hayek's 1941 *Pure Theory of Capital* readable and turns “malinvestment” from a macro phenomenon into micro-level “capital regrouping.” **How to read:** Chapters 1–3 essential; Chapter 5 on investment and the cycle pairs with Garrison.

**Lachmann, *The Market as an Economic Process* (1986).** Lachmann's late summation. Core claim: the market is a process driven by **divergent subjective expectations**, and there is no reason to assume it must tend toward equilibrium — expectations can reinforce or destroy one another. The strongest statement of “radical subjectivism” inside the school and a fundamental challenge to Kirzner's tendency to equilibrium (Lachmann called the world “kaleidic”). **How to read:** Chapters 1–2 essential; Chapter 6 on markets and institutions connects with GMU's institutional work. **Controversy:** Rothbard and the Misesians thought Lachmann drifted into nihilism — if everything is uncertain, what can economics say? The argument resurfaces in [[memes-reflexivity|Stage 16.5]] on reflexivity and meme stocks.

**Lavoie, *Rivalry and Central Planning* (1985).** A complete restatement of the socialist calculation debate and the founding work of the 1980s GMU branch. Its contribution: moving the debate from “calculation” and “knowledge” to **rivalry** — market competition discovers knowledge because entrepreneurs in **rivalry** with one another are forced to trial and error, while the central planner, having no rival, never learns where he went wrong. The same year he published *National Economic Planning: What Is Left?* **How to read:** Chapters 1–4 essential — the best contemporary background for [[lange-market-socialism|Stages 7.3]], 7.5 and 18.1. Lavoie died young in 2001; his “hermeneutical turn” (restating Austrian method through philosophical hermeneutics) remains contested within GMU.

### ② Macro, money and banking: Garrison, Huerta de Soto, Hülsmann, Salerno

**Garrison, *Time and Money: The Macroeconomics of Capital Structure* (2001).** About 270 pages, the original text behind [[garrison-macro|Stage 10.1]]. The problem: can Austrian macro be drawn with the diagrams of a mainstream textbook? Garrison uses three — the **production possibilities frontier** (consumption vs investment), the **loanable-funds market** (the interest rate) and the **Hayekian triangle** (the structure of production) — to render ABCT as “capital-based macro,” set against Keynes's “labor-based macro” and monetarism. **How to read:** Chapters 3–4 (the three diagrams and saving-driven growth) and Chapter 5 (the credit-driven boom and bust) essential; Chapters 7–9 on Keynes and monetarism are the best supplement to [[vs-keynes|Stages 11.1]] and 11.2. **Controversy:** Misesians find the loanable-funds diagram “too neoclassical,” obscuring pure time preference; as a teaching device, it remains the most successful visualization of Austrian macro.

**Huerta de Soto, *Money, Bank Credit, and Economic Cycles* (Spanish 1998; English 2006, about 900 pages).** The largest contemporary Austrian treatise. Three threads run in parallel: **legal history** (from the Roman-law *depositum irregulare* to medieval banking, arguing that demand deposits are bailments and fractional reserves violate that tradition), **economic theory** (the most complete contemporary statement of ABCT, including replies to Keynesian and monetarist critiques), and **policy** (a three-step transition to 100-percent reserves, gold and the abolition of central banks). **How to read:** Chapters 1–3 on legal history can be skimmed; Chapters 4–6 are the theoretical core and essential; Chapter 7's replies to critics pair with [[abct-critiques|Stage 5.5]]; Chapters 8–9 are the program. It is the flagship of the “100-percent” wing; keep Selgin's and White's free-banking arguments beside it.

**Hülsmann, *The Ethics of Money Production* (2008).** About 250 pages. Joins monetary theory to ethics (in the Catholic social-thought tradition): money production on a free market is a legitimate productive activity, while fiat money and fractional reserves are gains obtained by coercion. It supplies what Rothbard left undone — a systematic **ethical** critique of money. **How to read:** Part One (natural money) and Part Two (inflation) essential. **Hülsmann, *Mises: The Last Knight of Liberalism* (2007).** A biography of over a thousand pages reconstructing Mises's life from the Vienna, Geneva and New York archives. Read after *Human Action* and the tone of that book finally makes sense. **How to read:** as history; skim freely.

**Salerno.** No single “big book,” but several essential papers: “Mises and Hayek Dehomogenized” (1993, RAE) — arguing that Mises's “calculation” argument and Hayek's “knowledge” argument are different arguments, the former more fundamental; “Ludwig von Mises as Social Rationalist” (1990); and the collection *Money: Sound and Unsound* (2010), gathering his essays on deflation, the gold standard and Fed history. **How to read:** the 1993 paper first — it is the key to the Mises Institute/GMU split ([[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]]); then the deflation essays in *Money*, the most systematic Austrian rebuttal of “deflation phobia” (used in [[case-japan|Stage 13.3]] on Japan). **Controversy:** GMU regards “dehomogenization” as exaggerating the Mises–Hayek difference; Boettke wrote a dedicated reply.

### ③ Method, politics and the firm: Hoppe, Boettke, Klein and Foss

**Hoppe, *Economic Science and the Austrian Method* (1995).** An 80-page pamphlet, the most radical contemporary defense of Misesian apriorism. Core: the action axiom is not an “assumption” but a **synthetic a priori** proposition in Kant's sense; denying it is itself an action, hence self-contradictory; economic laws are therefore necessary, and statistics can neither confirm nor refute them. **How to read:** one afternoon, alongside [[apriori-empirical|Stage 2.2]]. **Controversy:** GMU and outside philosophers consider the “synthetic a priori” hard to defend after Kant; Hayekians hold that economics' core propositions contain empirical elements (the “Hayek problem” of [[read-hayek|Stage 12.3]]).

**Hoppe, *Democracy: The God That Failed* (2001).** About 300 pages. Core argument: under monarchy the ruler treats the state as **private property** and has an incentive to protect its long-run capital value (low time preference); under democracy the ruler is a **temporary caretaker** with an incentive to extract during his term (high time preference) — so democracy yields higher taxes, more debt and rising social time preference (“decivilization”). The conclusion is an anarcho-capitalist private-law society. **How to read:** Chapters 1–3 are the economic argument and deserve careful reading; the later chapters are political philosophy and cultural criticism of great controversy (including positions on immigration and “exclusion”) and should be evaluated separately from the economics. **Controversy:** the most contested contemporary Austrian book; even within the Mises Institute branch some keep their distance from its second half. This course recommends only its time-preference argument.

**Boettke, *Living Economics* (2012).** The GMU branch's self-statement: economics is a “way of thinking” (from Mises, Hayek and Buchanan), and contemporary Austrians should collaborate with new institutional economics, public choice and empirical case work to enter the mainstream conversation. It includes intellectual portraits of Mises, Hayek, Kirzner, Buchanan, Ostrom and Rothbard. **How to read:** the portraits in Parts One and Two are the most useful; it is also the best entry into GMU's “mainstreaming” strategy. Also recommended: the *Oxford Handbook of Austrian Economics* (2015), edited by Boettke and Coyne, as a map of current research.

**Klein and Foss, *Organizing Entrepreneurial Judgment* (2012).** About 300 pages, placing Mises's “judgment” (rather than Kirzner's “alertness”) at the center of entrepreneurship theory: the entrepreneur **commits resources** under uncertainty, so the entrepreneur must be a capitalist (or the two functions are inseparable); the firm exists to organize **delegated judgment** (owners delegating part of their judgment to managers). It is the deepest Austrian engagement with modern theory of the firm (Coase, Williamson) and the direct source for [[entrepreneur-alertness|Stages 6.1]] and 18.3 on whether AI can exercise judgment. **How to read:** Chapters 1–4 essential; the critique of Kirzner in Chapter 3 is the clearest text on the “alertness vs judgment” dispute.

### ④ Journals, biographies and free resources: where to follow current research

**Journals.**

- ***Quarterly Journal of Austrian Economics* (QJAE)**: published by the Mises Institute since 1998 (one branch of the split from the 1987 *Review of Austrian Economics*), full text free. Mises–Rothbard orientation; strong on money and cycles.
- ***Review of Austrian Economics* (RAE)**: Springer, founded 1988 (taken over by GMU after the 1998 split), edited by Boettke and Coyne, subscription (author versions often free). Hayek–institutional orientation; empirical and interdisciplinary work.
- ***Advances in Austrian Economics***: an Emerald annual, each volume on one theme (entrepreneurship, capital, institutions, method …) — the best place to follow the latest argument on a topic.
- Also watch for Austrian papers in the *Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization*, the *Journal of Institutional Economics* and *Public Choice* — the GMU branch's route into the mainstream.

**Biographies and intellectual history.**

- Hülsmann, *Mises: The Last Knight of Liberalism* (2007) — above.
- Caldwell, *Hayek's Challenge* (2004) — the best intellectual biography of Hayek and the guide to the “Hayek problem” ([[read-hayek|Stage 12.3]]).
- Rothbard has no biography of comparable weight; Justin Raimondo's *An Enemy of the State* (2000) is partisan — read with a discount.
- Karen Vaughn, *Austrian Economics in America* (1994) — the academic history of the 1970s “revival,” with first-hand testimony on how the three branches formed.

**Free resources.**

- **mises.org/library**: free PDF, EPUB and HTML of nearly every Austrian classic, including most of the works by Mises, Rothbard, Menger, Böhm-Bawerk, Huerta de Soto, Hoppe, Hülsmann and Salerno named in this lesson, plus the full run of QJAE.
- **Econlib (econlib.org)**: Hayek's “The Use of Knowledge in Society,” Bastiat, Smith and Mill in full; the “Austrian School of Economics” entry in the *Concise Encyclopedia*.
- **Mises Academy / Mises University recordings**: each summer's lecture course, from introductory to advanced.
- **Liberty Fund's Online Library of Liberty**: some Hayek, the complete Böhm-Bawerk.
- **[[career-paths|Stage 14.5]]** covers the societies (Society for the Development of Austrian Economics, Property and Freedom Society), summer programs and career paths in full.

### ⑤ A fair map of the three branches, and how to build your own path

**The fair map** (repeated because it matters): the three branches share the foundation and differ in four places — method, interest, banking, politics. **None is “orthodox”**: the Mises Institute can reasonably claim to be more faithful to Mises's text; GMU can reasonably claim to be more faithful to Hayek's method and to Mises's intention of engaging the mainstream; the Kirznerians can reasonably claim to have solved the “tendency to equilibrium” problem the others left open. **The right attitude is to read all three and judge at the points of disagreement** — the assignment of [[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]] and the precondition for [[open-problems|Stage ∞.1]]'s discussion of open problems.

**Building your own path** (the demo generates one from your interests and level):

- **Money and banking**: Rothbard's pamphlet ([[read-rothbard|Stage 12.4]]) → selected Salerno from *Money* → Huerta de Soto Chapters 4–6 → Selgin, *The Theory of Free Banking* (1988) as the opposing view → Hülsmann, *The Ethics of Money Production* → Hayek, *Denationalisation of Money*. About 12–16 weeks. All of [[bitcoin-regression|Stages 17.1–17.5]] on Bitcoin rests on this line.
- **Entrepreneurship and the firm**: Kirzner 1973 → Kirzner's 1997 JEL survey → Klein and Foss 2012 → Lachmann 1956 → the portraits in Boettke's *Living Economics*. About 10–12 weeks. Used in [[creator-economy|Stages 16.3]] and 18.3.
- **Capital and cycles**: Garrison 2001 → Lachmann 1956 → Huerta de Soto Chapters 5–6 → Hayek, *Prices and Production* Lectures 2–3 ([[read-hayek|Stage 12.3]]) → Rothbard, *America's Great Depression* Part I. About 12–14 weeks. Used in [[case-great-depression|Stages 13.2–13.4]] and 18.5.
- **Law and politics**: Hayek, *Law, Legislation and Liberty* Vol. 1 ([[read-hayek|Stage 12.3]]) → Hoppe, *Democracy* Chapters 1–3 → Rothbard, *The Ethics of Liberty* Parts 1–2 → Leoni, *Freedom and the Law* (1961, [[spontaneous-law|Stage 9.3]]) → selected institutional papers by Boettke, Coyne and Leeson. About 10–12 weeks.
- **Method**: Hoppe 1995 → Hayek, *The Counter-Revolution of Science* Part One → Lavoie 1985 Chapters 1–4 → Salerno 1993 → Boettke's reply → Caldwell 2004. About 10 weeks. Direct preparation for [[internal-debates|Stages 14.3]] and ∞.1.

Every path deliberately includes **at least one opposing voice** (Selgin against Huerta de Soto, Klein against Kirzner, Boettke against Salerno). That is not fence-sitting; it is what this course has said since [[what-is-austrian|Stage 0.2]] — the Austrian School is a science rather than a sect precisely because it has real disagreements that can be settled by argument.

## @analogy
Think of the Austrian School from 1871 to 1995 as a **river**: Menger is the source, Mises and Hayek are the confluence of two upstream tributaries, and Rothbard is the stretch where the channel was straightened and embanked.

After 1995 the river enters a **delta**. A delta has no main channel, only distributaries: one (the Mises Institute) runs on between Rothbard's old embankments — deep, fast, clear in direction, but with only its own people on both banks; one (George Mason) flows east into other watersheds — institutional economics, public choice — broad, with many side-channels, though some say it can no longer tell which water is its own; one (the Kirznerians) is neither wide nor deep, but it happens to pass the business-school docks and has become the most common entry for boats from outside.

All three carry the same river's water. Your job is not to pick one and settle there but to **buy a small boat** — this lesson's reading paths — and go a little way down each, stopping where they argue with one another (interest, banking, method, politics) to hear what each bank is saying. When you have crossed the delta it is time to put out to sea ([[empirical-method|Stages 13–18]]): take this river's water and explain the Depression, Japan, 2008 — and network effects, Bitcoin, AI.

## @misconceptions
- **“The Mises Institute is the Austrian School.”** — It is one of three branches, with a reasonable claim to be most faithful to Mises's text; the GMU (Hayek–institutional) and Kirznerian branches are equally Austrian. Equating one branch with the school is unfair to the other two and would cost you Lachmann, Lavoie, and Klein and Foss.
- **“Kirzner's entrepreneur is Mises's entrepreneur.”** — Kirzner's entrepreneur discovers price discrepancies through alertness, needs no capital and bears no uncertainty; Mises's entrepreneur commits resources under uncertainty through judgment. Klein and Foss (2012) criticize Kirzner on exactly this point, and [[entrepreneur-alertness|Stages 6.1]] and 18.3 depend on the distinction.
- **“Read contemporary Austrians author by author, cover to cover.”** — The classics (Menger, Mises, Hayek, Rothbard) should be read whole because they are the building; contemporary books are problem texts, to be chosen by problem, with at least one opposing voice on every path.
- **“All of Hoppe's *Democracy* is economics.”** — Chapters 1–3 on rulers' time preference are economics and deserve careful reading; the later political philosophy and cultural criticism (including positions on immigration) are highly contested, and even within the Mises Institute branch some keep their distance. Evaluate the two separately.
- **“Reading contemporary Austrians means buying a lot of books.”** — The mises.org library offers most of Mises, Rothbard, Menger, Huerta de Soto, Hoppe, Hülsmann and Salerno free, plus all of QJAE; Econlib has Hayek and the classical authors in full. Most of this lesson's list can be read at no cost.
