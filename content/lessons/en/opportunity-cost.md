# Opportunity Cost & the Unseen: Bastiat's Broken Window

## @hook
A boy throws a rock through a bakery window. The crowd consoles the baker: “It's not all bad — the glazier gets work, the money circulates.” In 1850 the French economist Frédéric Bastiat showed exactly where that goes wrong: **you see the glazier's income; you do not see the cobbler's loss — the $250 was about to buy a pair of shoes.** This lesson is about the Austrian definition of cost: **cost is not the money you spend but the best alternative you give up** — subjective, forward-looking, and existing only at the moment of decision. Learning to see the unseen is the base skill for every policy analysis from [[intervention-logic|Stage 8]] to [[five-step-analysis|Stage 14]].

## @intuition
Start with Bastiat's story, updated to today's prices.

A bakery's front window is smashed by a child. The baker sighs and pays a glazier $250 for a new pane. The onlookers begin to console him: “Look on the bright side — the glazier just earned $250. He'll spend it on bread and clothes; the baker and the tailor earn in turn … the money goes round, everybody gets work. If nobody ever broke a window, what would glaziers live on?”

It sounds reasonable, and a more refined version of it is alive and well today: “disasters boost GDP,” “the war ended the Great Depression,” “infrastructure spending creates jobs.” Bastiat's point is that they all make **the same mistake** — they look only at **what is seen** and not at **what is not seen.**

What is seen: the glazier has $250 more.
What is not seen: **the baker was going to spend that $250 on a pair of shoes.** Now he won't. The cobbler has $250 less.

So “the money goes round” is true — it was always going to go round, only now it goes round through the glazier instead of the cobbler. The glazier's gain equals the cobbler's loss exactly; **for society as a whole, that line nets to zero.** But the story is not over. Before the rock, the baker had a window and (was about to have) a pair of shoes. After the rock, he has a window. **Society is one pair of shoes poorer.** The boy did not “create employment” — he moved the cobbler's job to the glazier and destroyed a pair of shoes.

What the story really teaches is not “don't break windows.” It is **what cost actually is.** What did the new window cost the baker? Not the number $250. It cost **the shoes** — the second-ranked use he gave up in order to replace the window. That is **opportunity cost**: the cost of any choice is the best alternative you forgo. It grows straight out of the subjective value theory of [[subjective-value|Stage 1.2]]: on your value scale, first place is what you chose; second place is your cost.

On that definition, cost has three properties that are easy to miss.

**Cost is subjective.** How much the forgone shoes were worth, only the baker knows. The “$250” in the ledger is a price, not a cost. The same $250 costs something different to a person who was going to save it than to one who was going to buy shoes.

**Cost is forward-looking.** It exists at the moment of decision — “if I choose A now, I give up B.” Money already spent and time already gone are not part of the cost of any future choice. That is why **sunk costs do not count**: whether it is worth finishing your PhD after three years has nothing to do with what those three years cost and everything to do with the opportunity cost of the next two.

**Cost is usually invisible.** The glazier's income has an invoice; the cobbler's loss does not — those shoes were never made, and you cannot photograph a pair of shoes that does not exist. **This is the root of every “seen versus unseen” fallacy: what is seen grabs attention by itself; what is unseen has to be deliberately thought about.**

In 1946 Henry Hazlitt compressed Bastiat's method into the “one lesson” of *Economics in One Lesson*: the art of economics is to look not only at the immediate effects of a policy but at its longer-run effects, and not only at its effects on one group but on all groups. One sentence, two dimensions: time (now versus later) and people (those who benefit versus those who pay).

From here we will put that knife to work on jobs “created” by government spending, economies “boosted” by war, reconstruction “stimulus” after disasters, subsidies for favored industries — and on your own decisions: the real cost of an MBA, of starting a company, of staying one more year in a job. You will find that **almost every claim about “stimulus,” “boosting” and “job creation” writes down only one side of the ledger.**

**In this lesson we break it into five pieces:**

- **① Bastiat 1850: what is seen and what is not seen**
- **② Cost = the best alternative forgone: subjective, forward-looking, existing only at the moment of choice**
- **③ Sunk costs don't count: the real price of an MBA**
- **④ Hazlitt's “one lesson”: all groups, the long run**
- **⑤ Applications: government spending, “job creation,” war, disaster “stimulus” and subsidies**

## @mechanics
### ① Bastiat 1850: what is seen and what is not seen

Frédéric Bastiat (1801–1850) was a French economist, legislator and the wittiest free-trade pamphleteer who ever lived. In 1850, months before his death, he published the essay *Ce qu'on voit et ce qu'on ne voit pas* — “That Which Is Seen, and That Which Is Not Seen.” Its first section is **the broken window** (in the original the culprit smashes the shop window of “the good bourgeois James Goodfellow” and the glazier collects six francs; we use $250).

Let us do the accounting carefully. Suppose the baker has $250 in cash and has already decided to buy a pair of shoes with it. Compare two worlds:

One common objection deserves a straight answer: **“What if the baker was not going to buy shoes but was going to save the $250? Then didn't the rock put ‘idle money’ to work?”** This is the core of the Keynesian argument ([[vs-keynes|Stage 11.1]] steelmans it in full). Two replies. First, money in a bank is not idle; it is lent to someone else to invest — the cobbler did not get it, but someone buying a machine did ([[saving-stages|Stage 3.3]]). Second, even if he locked the cash in a drawer, it does not vanish — holding cash slightly raises the purchasing power of everyone else's money (the demand for money, [[money-demand|Stage 4.2]]). **Either way, breaking the window added nothing to society; it merely diverted a pane of glass and a few hours of labor into getting back to where things already were.**

Note also that the broken window **does** raise GDP. The glazier's $250 is a recorded transaction; the cobbler's unmade shoes are not. That is not a bug in GDP but an inherent property of aggregate statistics — they only add up what is seen ([[aggregates-limits|Stage 10.2]]).

### ② Cost = the best alternative forgone: subjective, forward-looking, existing only at the moment of choice

The broken window is about the unseen at the level of society. Zoom in to a single person and the same idea is called **opportunity cost**.

The Austrian concept of cost comes from Friedrich von Wieser in Vienna (*Natural Value*, 1889): **the cost of a thing is the value the resources used to make it would have yielded in their best alternative use.** There is no “quantity of labor” or “money invested” in that definition — cost, like value, is a **valuation**, not an **input**. It is the same reversal of causation as in [[diamonds-water|Stage 1.1]]: value does not come from cost; cost is itself forgone value.

Later the LSE tradition — Robbins, Hayek, Coase, Thirlby — sharpened the concept, and James Buchanan's short 1969 book *Cost and Choice* summarized it in six propositions, the most important of which are:

- **Cost is borne only by the decision-maker.** Nobody can bear your opportunity cost for you, because it is second place on your value scale, which nobody else can see.
- **Cost is subjective.** It exists in the decision-maker's mind.
- **Cost is ex ante.** It rests on expectations about the future, not on what has already happened.
- **Cost vanishes at the moment of choice.** Once you have chosen A, B will never happen, so you can never “see” the cost. All you ever see is the result of A.

That last proposition is Buchanan's deepest insight, and the root of the broken-window fallacy: **cost is structurally invisible.** You chose the window; the shoes will never appear in any ledger. So “seeing the unseen” is not a moral demand (“have more empathy”) but a **technique of thought**: every time you see an expenditure, ask “**what is the second-best use of this money, these resources, these hours?**”

In numbers: you have a Saturday. Options: A, work overtime (earn $300); B, spend it with family; C, sleep. You choose B. The cost of B is not zero (you spent no money), and not $300 (that is A's price, not your valuation) — the cost of B is **whichever of A and C ranks higher for you.** If you rank A above C, then the cost of family time is “the best thing $300 of overtime could have bought.” And if your boss raises overtime pay to $600 next week, **the cost of family time has risen**, even though your family has not changed at all — which is why “cost is subjective and forward-looking” is not philosophical decoration. It changes decisions every day.

### ③ Sunk costs don't count: the real price of an MBA

Opportunity cost is forward-looking, and one direct consequence is that **money already spent and unrecoverable — sunk cost — contributes nothing to the decision in front of you.**

The classic example. You paid $80 for a ticket to a ball game; on the day, it pours. Do you go? Many people say “if I don't go, I've wasted $80.” But the $80 is gone — go or stay, it is not coming back. The choice right now is between two things: “watch the game in the rain” and “stay home.” **The correct comparison is the value of those two options; the $80 is in neither.** Buchanan: once the choice is made, the cost has vanished.

Apply it to a big decision: **what does a two-year MBA cost?** Most people answer “tuition — about $150,000.” That is only the seen half. The unseen half: **two years of salary** — if you earn $100,000 now, that is $200,000 — plus two years of experience, promotions, and perhaps a window to start a company. **Most of the real cost of an MBA is forgone salary, not tuition.** This also explains something that looks odd: why business-school applications fall in booms and rise in recessions. In a recession the forgone salary (the opportunity cost) falls, so the MBA gets “cheaper” — even though tuition has not dropped a dollar.

Same logic: the cost of starting a company is mostly the job you leave; the cost of “staying one more year to see” is the opportunities you did not try that year; the cost of a country assigning ten thousand engineers to a prestige project is what those ten thousand would otherwise have built — not their wages.

Push one step further and you reach capital theory: **the historical cost of a factory already built or a machine already bought does not count either.** A machine bought for $1 million is worth today only what its future earnings or resale price are worth. That is why [[bust-liquidation|Stage 5.3]] says that “liquidating” malinvestments in a bust is not waste but an admission that sunk costs are sunk and a redirection of resources to where people want them. Refusing to liquidate (“we've already put so much in”) is the sunk-cost fallacy at national scale.

### ④ Hazlitt's “one lesson”: all groups, the long run

Henry Hazlitt (1894–1993) was the *New York Times*'s economics columnist and Mises's earliest champion in America. In 1946 he wrote *Economics in One Lesson*, whose opening upgrades Bastiat's method into a general rule: the art of economics consists in tracing the effects of any policy not just for one group but for all groups, and not just in the immediate term but in the longer run.

The rule has two dimensions, and each is a variant of the broken window:

- **The time dimension: now versus later.** Print money today and what is seen is this year's boom; what is unseen is the liquidation of malinvestment three years on ([[abct-one-picture|Stage 5.1]]). Push rates to zero and what is seen is house prices rising; what is unseen is the long projects that were encouraged and that snap when rates come back.
- **The group dimension: those who benefit versus those who pay.** A tariff protects the visible steelworker and harms the invisible factories that use steel and every consumer who buys a car. A subsidy goes to a visible industry; the money comes from invisible taxpayers.

Hazlitt also identified an asymmetry in **political economy**: the beneficiaries are **concentrated, visible and organized** — an industry, a union, a region; the payers are **dispersed, invisible and never going to march over a $3 loss each.** So “looking only at the seen” is not an accidental oversight but a bias systematically reinforced by the structure of interests. [[economics-of-state|Stage 8.4]] on public choice pushes that asymmetry to its conclusion.

Honestly, the knife must also be turned on Austrians themselves. Austrian writers sometimes wield “the unseen” as a universal rebuttal — any policy can be met with “you haven't counted the costs” — without estimating how large those costs are. **Unseen does not mean infinite.** Some interventions may genuinely have opportunity costs smaller than their benefits (certain public-health measures, say). The Austrian contribution is to insist that **the column gets written down**, not to declare that it always dominates. In the five-step method of [[five-step-analysis|Stage 14.1]], “list the unseen costs” is a step, not a conclusion.

### ⑤ Applications: government spending, “job creation,” war, disaster “stimulus” and subsidies

Put the broken-window ledger against the most common claims. Each is stated in its strongest form first.

**“Government infrastructure spending created 50,000 jobs.”** Strong version: the government builds a bridge, contractors hire, workers earn, earnings are spent, and a multiplier amplifies it. Reply: where does the $5 billion come from? Taxes, borrowing or printing. **Taxes**: taxpayers spend $5 billion less — the people they would have hired (the cobblers) are unseen. **Borrowing**: $5 billion is pulled out of the capital market; the firms that would have got those loans are unseen. **Printing**: the Cantillon effect of [[cantillon-inflation|Stage 4.3]] — the contractors who get the new money first benefit, and every later holder of money is diluted. In all three cases the jobs “created” are **transferred** from somewhere else, and transferred from uses that consumers ranked highly to uses that politicians rank highly. **The bridge may well be worth building** — but the argument must be “this bridge is worth more than what taxpayers would have bought themselves,” never “it creates jobs.” Job creation is never an end in itself; if it were, we could dig canals with spoons.

**“World War II ended the Great Depression.”** Strong version: unemployment fell to near zero in the 1940s and GDP soared. Reply: unemployment fell to zero because more than ten million people were drafted, and GDP soared because tanks and shells were counted as output. Ask an American household about its standard of living in 1943 — rationing, no new cars, no new houses. **War is the largest broken window in history.** The economic historian Robert Higgs has dissected this “wartime prosperity” statistical illusion in detail. The real recovery came after 1946, when government spending collapsed and resources returned to civilian production ([[case-great-depression|Stage 13.2]]).

**“Reconstruction after the disaster boosted the economy.”** Strong version: after an earthquake, construction booms and local GDP rebounds. Reply: this is the original broken window, unmodified. Every dollar of rebuilding would otherwise have gone to something **new** and now goes to restoring something **old**. GDP rising is a statistic; wealth falling is a fact.

**“Subsidizing renewables / chips / farming nurtures strategic industries.”** Strong version: some industries have externalities or learning curves and need early support. Reply: every subsidy dollar has an unseen cobbler — and subsidies add one more layer of cost: they tell entrepreneurs that pleasing the government pays better than pleasing consumers, steering the **entrepreneurship** of [[entrepreneur-alertness|Stage 6.1]] into the **rent-seeking** of [[taxes-regulation|Stage 8.3]]. Industrial policy's ledger must always have two columns.

**“If we stop importing and make it ourselves, we keep the jobs.”** Strong version: tariffs protect domestic employment. Reply: the extra money consumers pay for the same goods is the unseen pair of shoes; for every job kept in the protected industry, more disappear downstream and among consumers — only dispersed and invisible. [[exchange-division|Stage 1.5]] puts numbers on this with comparative advantage.

**One step further ahead.** By [[ai-jobs|Stage 18.4]] you will see that “AI took the jobs” has the same structure as “imports took the jobs”: the displaced jobs are seen; the freed resources and the new uses that follow are unseen. The broken-window ledger does not change its rules because the rock is now thrown by an algorithm.

The lesson in one sentence: **cost is the second place you gave up; it is always subjective, always forward-looking, and it disappears from view the moment you choose — so the entire craft of the economist is to write that column back in on society's behalf.**

## @analogy
Think of every expenditure as **a receipt and an empty chair.**

The receipt is what is seen: who got paid, how much, for what. Government annual reports, news headlines, GDP statistics — all receipts. They record only transactions that **happened**.

The empty chair is what is not seen: **the person who would have been sitting here had the money not gone elsewhere.** When the glazier gets his receipt, the cobbler's chair sits empty. At the ribbon-cutting for the infrastructure project, next to every job “created” stands the empty chair of someone a taxpayer would have hired. When the munitions plants run three shifts, the whole civilian economy is a room full of empty chairs.

Empty chairs never make the news — you cannot interview a person who was not hired or photograph shoes that were not made. So the skill Bastiat and Hazlitt teach comes down to one motion: **every time you see a receipt, count the empty chair.**

It applies to you as well. Your calendar is a receipt: you worked overtime this Saturday. The empty chair is the family you did not see and the sleep you did not get. Your résumé is a receipt: two years of MBA. The empty chair is two years of salary and a company you might have started. **Cost always sits in the empty chair, never on the receipt.**

## @misconceptions
- **“Destruction has an upside — it gets money moving and creates jobs.”** — The money was always going to move; it now moves through the glazier instead of the cobbler. The glazier's gain equals the cobbler's loss exactly, and society is net one pair of shoes poorer. Destruction never creates wealth; it only shifts attention from the unseen loser to the seen winner.
- **“Cost is the money spent — whatever the ledger says.”** — The ledger's number is a price, not a cost. Cost is the best alternative you give up; it is subjective, forward-looking, and exists only in the decision-maker's mind. The same $250 costs something different to someone who was going to buy shoes and to someone who was going to save it.
- **“We've already invested so much — it would be a waste to stop now.”** — The sunk-cost fallacy. Money already spent is gone regardless, and belongs to the cost of no present choice. The only relevant comparison is between the future value of ‘continue’ and ‘stop’ — for individuals, and for nations refusing to liquidate malinvestments.
- **“War and disasters raise GDP, so they are good for the economy.”** — GDP adds only seen transactions: tanks, rebuilt houses. It does not subtract what was destroyed or what would otherwise have been produced. War is history's largest broken window; the “prosperity” of the 1940s came with rationing and a falling standard of living.
- **“‘Unseen costs’ is a universal rebuttal that defeats any policy.”** — Unseen does not mean infinite. Some interventions may genuinely have opportunity costs below their benefits. The Austrian contribution is to insist the column be written and estimated item by item, not to claim it always dominates — in [[five-step-analysis|Stage 14.1]]'s five-step method it is one step, not the verdict.

   - [ ] Zero — the glazier earned $250 and the money circulated
   - [ ] $250 in cash
   - [x] The pair of shoes the baker was going to buy with the $250 — the glazier's gain equals the cobbler's loss, and the shoes are the net loss
   - [ ] The glazier's working hours
   > Glazier +$250 and cobbler −$250 is a **transfer**, zero for society; but World A ends with only a window while World B has a window plus shoes. The missing shoes are destroyed wealth.

2. Which of the following matches the Austrian/Buchanan definition of “cost”?
   - [ ] The quantity of labor embodied in production
   - [ ] The historical outlay recorded in the ledger
   - [x] The best alternative the decision-maker forgoes for the chosen option — subjective, ex ante, and vanishing at the moment of choice
   - [ ] Market price times quantity
   > Wieser → LSE → Buchanan, *Cost and Choice* (1969): cost is borne only by the chooser, is subjective, rests on expectations, and “vanishes” at the moment of choice — which is why it is structurally unseen.

3. Why do MBA applications usually rise during recessions?
   - [ ] Because tuition falls in recessions
   - [x] Because forgone salary (the opportunity cost) falls, lowering the real cost of the MBA even though tuition is unchanged
   - [ ] Because people have more time in recessions
   - [ ] Because governments subsidize education in recessions
   > Most of an MBA's real cost is two years of forgone salary, not tuition. In a recession wages and job opportunities shrink, so the opportunity cost falls and the MBA gets “cheaper.”

4. “The government spent $5 billion on a bridge and created 50,000 jobs.” What is the Hazlitt-style follow-up question?
   - [ ] Is the bridge well designed?
   - [x] Where did the $5 billion come from (taxes, borrowing, printing), and whom would the people who paid have hired instead — where are the transferred jobs?
   - [ ] Are the 50,000 jobs well paid?
   - [ ] Did the contractor finish on time?
   > All groups, the long run: whatever the funding source, the jobs are **transferred** from taxpayers, borrowers or money-holders. The bridge may be worth building, but the argument must be “the bridge is worth more than the taxpayers' own uses,” not “it creates jobs.”

5. You paid $80 for a ball-game ticket and it pours on game day. On opportunity-cost logic, what is the correct decision comparison?
   - [ ] Go, or the $80 is wasted
   - [x] Compare the present value of “watch in the rain” and “stay home”; the $80 is sunk and belongs to neither
   - [ ] Don't go, because the $80 is already lost and going would only lose more
   - [ ] See whether the ticket can be resold for $80
   > Sunk costs don't count: the $80 is not coming back whether you go or not. Only two forward-looking options remain, and the cost is whichever one you forgo — not the historical outlay.
