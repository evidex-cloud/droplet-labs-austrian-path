# Free Banking & Currency Competition: Hayek's Denationalisation

## @hook
[[fractional-reserve|Stages 4.4]] and 4.5 showed how fractional reserves and a central bank manufacture credit expansion. The natural next question is: **what if there were no central bank?** Hayek's 1976 answer was to hand money over to competition: let private issuers each put out their own currency, and let the one with the most stable purchasing power win. White and Selgin dug up Scotland between 1716 and 1845 to show that a banking system with no central bank disciplines itself through note clearing. Rothbard, Hoppe and Huerta de Soto reply: competition, yes — but fractional reserves are the problem itself, so go back to 100 percent reserves. **This is the most durable and most honest argument inside the Austrian school**: the consensus is to end the state's monopoly on money; the disagreement is about what the market would choose. Stablecoins and Bitcoin are now turning the argument into an experiment ([[stablecoins-cbdc|Stages 17.3]], 17.5).

## @intuition
Imagine a small town with no central bank and four banks. Each takes gold deposits and issues its own notes — “Bank A notes,” “Bank B notes” — promising gold on demand. Townspeople spend whichever notes they hold; shopkeepers deposit whatever they receive at their own bank.

Now Bank A gets ideas. It holds 100 ounces of gold but prints 500 ounces' worth of notes and lends them out (the other three print 300 each). What happens? A's notes quickly spread across town, and shopkeepers deposit them at B, C and D. Every week those three banks take the A notes they have collected to the **clearinghouse** and demand gold from A. A has collected some of their notes too, and the claims can be netted — but A issued more than anyone else, so far more of its notes flow out than come back: **the net balance has to be settled in gold.** With the numbers in the lesson, A loses about 75 ounces a week and is empty in under a fortnight. Either it contracts fast, or depositors see its reserves draining, queue at the door, and it fails. **With no regulator at all, Bank A is held in check by the reflux of its own notes.** That is the core mechanism of **free banking** as White and Selgin describe it, and it is what Scotland actually ran for over a century, from 1716 to 1845.

Hayek's idea goes one step further: not just banks competing to issue notes on **the same** money (gold), but **different monies themselves** competing. When he wrote *Denationalisation of Money* in 1976 the West was in the grip of the Great Inflation; his diagnosis was that inflation came not from some central banker's mistake but from **the state's monopoly on money** — an issuer who need not fear losing customers will always over-issue. The cure: repeal legal-tender laws, let private institutions issue their own currency units and pledge to hold their purchasing power stable; over-issued currencies depreciate and are abandoned, stable ones win. **Money, too, should have a market.**

Then the split inside the school. Rothbard, Hoppe, Hülsmann and Huerta de Soto agree on “end the monopoly” but object to Bank A's “100 ounces of gold, 300 ounces of notes” from the very start. Their argument has a legal side: you **deposit** gold at the bank, the bank lends it out, and now one lump of gold has two owners — that is fraud, or at least a contract that cannot be valid (the property logic of [[why-property|Stage 9.1]]). And an economic side: whenever a bank issues notes with no gold behind them, that is the credit expansion of [[fractional-reserve|Stage 4.4]], and it starts the cycle of [[abct-one-picture|Stage 5.1]] — the central bank merely amplified the process; it did not invent it. Hence **100 percent reserves**: a bank is either a warehouse (charging storage fees) or a genuine intermediary (lending term funds that depositors have explicitly given up for a period).

White and Selgin answer that deposit contracts are voluntary and depositors know the bank lends; and that fractional free banking does something a 100 percent bank cannot: when people **want to hold more money** (hoarding cash in a panic, say), note reflux slows, and banks can issue a little more to meet that demand, averting the deflation a rise in money demand would otherwise cause. They call this **monetary equilibrium**. The 100 percent camp fires back: that adjustment should come from falling prices, not from banks conjuring notes; and third parties — people who signed no deposit contract at all — still suffer the Cantillon effects of [[cantillon-inflation|Stage 4.3]].

There is no referee for this argument. This lesson lays out the strongest version of each side, then points to something both agree on: **the Federal Reserve is worse than either kind of free banking** — it has neither the hard constraint of 100 percent reserves nor the soft constraint of competitive clearing, and with a lender of last resort and deposit insurance it has dismantled the constraints on commercial banks too. Finally we look at the experiment now under way: stablecoins are privately issued “dollar notes,” and the 2022 collapse of Terra was a textbook bank run; Bitcoin is money **with no issuer**, a form Hayek never imagined. Currency competition has finally begun — and the market's choices so far have surprised both camps.

**In this lesson we break it into six pieces:**

- **① Hayek 1976: why money should have a market too**
- **② Scotland 1716–1845: how a clearinghouse replaces a regulator**
- **③ The 100 percent camp: fractional reserves as fraud, or at least the seed of the cycle**
- **④ Both sides laid flat: strongest points and weakest links**
- **⑤ Why the Fed is worse than either**
- **⑥ Stablecoins and Bitcoin: the first real test of currency competition**

## @mechanics
### ① Hayek 1976: why money should have a market too

Hayek was not always against state money. In *Monetary Nationalism and International Stability* (1937) he was still defending the international gold standard. The turn came from the experience after 1971: gold was abandoned entirely, and governments pushed inflation into double digits through the 1970s. His conclusion is in *Denationalisation of Money* (1976; enlarged 1978): **money issued by a monopolist will be abused; that is not a problem of persons but of incentives.**

His concrete proposal:

- Repeal legal-tender laws and exchange controls; allow any institution to issue a currency unit under its own name (he imagined one called the “ducat”), and let people contract, keep accounts and pay taxes in any currency.
- Each issuer publicly pledges to keep its currency's purchasing power stable against a basket of commodities, and honours the pledge by regulating its issue; an issuer that breaks the pledge is abandoned by the market.
- The outcome of competition is that **the currency with the most stable purchasing power wins** — not because issuers are virtuous, but because nobody wants to hold an unstable one.

The argument descends directly from “competition as a discovery procedure” in [[competition-process|Stage 6.2]]: we do not know what the best money looks like any more than we know what the best car looks like — only competition can find out. Hayek also admitted the difficulty candidly: money has strong network effects ([[network-effects|Stage 15.1]]); people want the money everyone else uses, so competition may be imperfect and switching slow. What he did not foresee is that when currency competition really arrived forty years later, the first entrants were not “private currencies pledged to stable purchasing power” but dollar-pegged stablecoins and issuerless Bitcoin (⑥).

### ② Scotland 1716–1845: how a clearinghouse replaces a regulator

Lawrence White's *Free Banking in Britain: Theory, Experience and Debate, 1800–1845* (1984) and George Selgin's *The Theory of Free Banking* (1988) brought Hayek's thought experiment down to history.

**The facts (roughly):** the Bank of Scotland's monopoly charter of 1695 lapsed in 1716 and was not renewed; the Royal Bank of Scotland (1727), the British Linen Company (1746) and later dozens of banks issued their own notes, all convertible into gold; there was no central bank, no legal-tender monopoly, no reserve requirement; banks branched widely and shareholders mostly bore unlimited liability. The largest failure was the Ayr Bank in 1772, whose shareholders paid creditors out of their personal fortunes. England in the same period restricted banks to six partners and suffered repeated banking crises (1825 above all); Scotland was comparatively stable. The Bank Act of 1845 ended new banks' right of note issue, and the episode closed.

**The mechanism:** the textbook value of this history lies in **note clearing as discipline.** The four-bank example:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{Bank A: gold } 100,\ \text{notes } 500; \\
&\text{Banks B, C, D: gold } 100,\ \text{notes } 300\text{ each} \\[6pt]
&\text{Each week about } 50\%\text{ of notes are spent and deposited,} \\
&\tfrac{3}{4}\text{ of them at other banks} \\[6pt]
&\text{A's outflow: } 500 \times 50\% \times \tfrac{3}{4} \approx 187.5; \\
&\text{A's inflow of others' notes: } 900 \times 50\% \times \tfrac{1}{4} \approx 112.5 \\[6pt]
&\text{Net settlement: A pays gold} \approx 75\text{ per week} \\
&\quad \;\rightarrow\; 100\text{ ounces gone in under two weeks}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Monetary equilibrium** is Selgin's further claim: in such a system the note supply **automatically tracks money demand.** When the public wants to hold more notes (spending them less urgently), reflux slows, net clearings shrink, and banks can issue a little more without losing gold; when the public wants to hold less, reflux speeds up and banks are forced to contract. The result is that nominal spending stays roughly stable and swings in money demand do not become deflationary or inflationary shocks. Selgin argues this beats both a fixed money stock and a stable price level — his “productivity norm” lets prices fall gently as productivity rises.

**An honest addendum:** in 1988 Rothbard published “The Myth of Free Banking in Scotland” in the *Review of Austrian Economics*, noting that Scottish banks **suspended gold payments** from 1797 to 1821 alongside the Bank of England, and held Bank of England notes as reserves — they were not a “free” system independent of London. White replied that the suspension was wartime legal authorization rather than a bank choice, and that the Scottish system's relative stability remains a fact. **Both sides can cite the historical record**, and the reader should know that.

### ③ The 100 percent camp: fractional reserves as fraud, or at least the seed of the cycle

Rothbard (*The Mystery of Banking*, 1983; *The Case for a 100 Percent Gold Dollar*, 1962), Hoppe, Hülsmann and Block (“Against Fiduciary Media,” *Quarterly Journal of Austrian Economics*, 1998) and Huerta de Soto (*Money, Bank Credit, and Economic Cycles*, 1998) form the other camp. The argument has two layers.

**The legal layer.** Huerta de Soto goes back to Roman law: the **irregular deposit** (*depositum irregulare*) — handing fungible goods (grain, coins) to a custodian — obliges the custodian to **keep an equal quantity and quality available at all times**; using the deposit is misappropriation. A demand deposit is by its legal nature a deposit, not a loan: the depositor has not surrendered the use of the funds; he can withdraw at any moment. When the bank lends it out, the same money carries **two simultaneously valid claims of ownership** — the depositor's and the borrower's. In the property logic of [[why-property|Stage 9.1]] that is impossible: a scarce thing cannot be wholly owned by two people at once. So fractional reserves are either fraud (if the depositor is unaware) or a contract that cannot stand (if “awareness” still cannot make two complete titles coexist).

**The economic layer.** A bank issuing notes with no gold behind them (**fiduciary media**) is increasing loans with no real saving behind them — precisely the credit expansion of [[fractional-reserve|Stage 4.4]] and the trigger of the cycle in [[abct-one-picture|Stage 5.1]]. The central bank did not invent the process; it amplified and synchronized it. Credit expansion under free banking is smaller because of clearing, but **it is not zero** — above all when all banks expand **together**: their net settlements are unchanged and nobody loses gold (look at the figure again: if B, C and D also issue 500, A is no longer punished). Huerta de Soto adds that Selgin's “monetary equilibrium” asks banks to judge how much money demand has changed — which is exactly the kind of thing nobody can know, in the sense of [[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]].

Their remedy: split banking into two businesses — the **warehouse** (100 percent reserve demand deposits, for a storage fee) and the **intermediary** (lending term funds that depositors have explicitly given up for a fixed period and cannot withdraw meanwhile). Credit can come only from real saving.

### ④ Both sides laid flat: strongest points and weakest links

**The free bankers' (White, Selgin) strongest points:** (a) it actually ran, and ran more stably than the more regulated England of the same period; (b) the clearing constraint is real, automatic, and needs no regulator; (c) monetary equilibrium answers a genuine problem — under a fixed supply, a sharp rise in money demand becomes a deflationary shock; (d) deposit contracts are voluntary: before 1765, Scottish notes even carried an “option clause” allowing the bank to defer redemption with interest, and note-holders accepted it.

**The free bankers' weakest links:** (a) what constrains expansion *in concert*? Selgin's answer is that joint expansion raises the **variance** of each bank's clearing balances and forces them to hold more reserves; critics find this too weak; (b) how far the Scottish system depended on London is disputed; (c) voluntary contracts do not solve the third-party problem: people who signed no deposit contract still bear the Cantillon effects of new notes ([[cantillon-inflation|Stage 4.3]]).

**The 100 percent camp's (Rothbard, Hoppe, Huerta de Soto) strongest points:** (a) the legal argument is clean — one ounce of gold with two owners makes no sense in property logic; (b) it traces the cycle all the way to the banking business itself rather than stopping at the central bank; (c) it is fully consistent with [[why-property|Stage 9.1]]'s “property is the foundation of the price system”: fiduciary media are “tickets” with no corresponding scarce good, introducing into money exactly the confusion described in ① of that lesson.

**The 100 percent camp's weakest links:** (a) no large-scale historical example (the Bank of Amsterdam after 1609 is often cited, but it too lent secretly in its later years); (b) if depositor and bank both explicitly agree “this is a loan, not a deposit,” the fraud argument lapses and only the cycle argument remains; (c) when money demand jumps, a 100 percent system really does go through the pain of downward price adjustment — the reply “prices will adjust” carries less conviction against sticky wages and debts, as some in the 100 percent camp themselves concede.

What makes the argument genuinely interesting is that **it is fought inside the school with Austrian tools** — subjective value, property logic, credit-expansion theory; both sides use all three. [[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]] places it on the larger Misesian/Hayekian/Rothbardian map. Mises himself stood between the camps: in *Human Action* he endorsed free banking as the **path toward** hard constraint (“free banking is the only method available for the prevention of the dangers inherent in credit expansion”), while also favouring a legal prohibition on any further issue of fiduciary media — and both sides cite him.

### ⑤ Why the Fed is worse than either

List each camp's constraint mechanism and the Fed's position becomes clear:

- **100 percent reserves:** hard constraint — no gold, no notes.
- **Free banking:** soft constraint — the over-issuer loses reserves and faces a run.
- **The Federal Reserve system** (founded 1913): monopoly issue + legal tender (with no redemption obligation at all after 1971) + **lender of last resort** + **deposit insurance** (the FDIC, 1933).

For the Fed itself, no clearing constraint exists: its liabilities redeem into nothing, and nobody can “take dollars back for gold.” For commercial banks the clearing constraint survives (they still settle reserves among themselves), but **the consequences of losing reserves have been removed**: short of reserves, borrow from the Fed (lender of last resort); depositors will not run (deposit insurance). So Bank A from ② is no longer punished — as long as all banks expand together, the Fed supplies whatever reserves they need. This is the “over-issuing together” the 100 percent camp feared in ③, institutionalized.

The dollar has lost roughly 96 percent or more of its purchasing power since 1913 (by a crude reading of official CPI); prices under Scottish free banking were broadly stable. **On this the two Austrian camps agree completely: a central bank is not the solution to banking instability but the socialization of it.** The post-1971 story of [[central-banks-fiat|Stage 4.5]] merely removed the last constraint.

### ⑥ Stablecoins and Bitcoin: the first real test of currency competition

Forty years after Hayek's book, currency competition actually began — in a form nobody expected.

**Stablecoins** ([[stablecoins-cbdc|Stage 17.3]]) are privately issued “dollar notes”: the issuer (Tether, Circle and others) promises 1:1 redemption in dollars, backed by off-chain reserves. These are the bank notes of ②, running on a blockchain. And they **replay the whole argument**: are the reserves 100 percent Treasury bills and cash (close to the 100 percent camp's warehouse model), or do they include commercial paper and loans (fractional)? Are the reserves audited? The 2022 collapse of Terra/UST was a textbook run — its “reserve” was another token it had itself issued, and once confidence wavered reflux became an avalanche, wiping out roughly 40 billion dollars of market value in days. In 2023 USDC briefly lost its peg because about 3.3 billion dollars of its reserves sat at Silicon Valley Bank — **the reserve itself lived in a fractional-reserve bank, and the risk passed straight through.** These episodes are teaching material for both camps: free bankers see clearing and reputation punishing bad issuers; the 100 percent camp sees every old problem of fractional reserves intact.

**Bitcoin** ([[bitcoin-regression|Stages 17.1]], 17.2) is a form Hayek did not imagine: **no issuer.** He pictured issuers pledging to hold purchasing power steady; Bitcoin has nobody pledging anything, only a fixed supply rule. It cannot “regulate its issue to stabilize purchasing power,” so its price swings violently — by Hayek's standard a “bad money,” by the 100 percent camp's standard the ultimate hard money. And inside the Bitcoin ecosystem fractional reserves grew back: exchanges “custodied” customers' coins and used them — the 2022 failure of FTX was a bank run with no central bank, exactly the “one coin, two owners” of ③. [[bitcoin-cycles|Stage 17.5]] asks whether there would still be cycles under a Bitcoin standard; the answer depends on this 100 percent-versus-fractional argument, not on Bitcoin alone.

**The market's choice so far:** people did not flock to “the private currency with the most stable purchasing power”; they flocked to stablecoins **pegged to the dollar** — because the dollar has the largest network ([[network-effects|Stage 15.1]]). That partly refutes Hayek's “the most stable wins,” confirms the network-effect difficulty he himself acknowledged, and confirms the logic of the regression theorem in [[origin-of-money|Stage 4.1]]: a new money must borrow its acceptability from an existing one.

The lesson's conclusion has two layers. **Consensus:** end the state's monopoly on money — every Austrian camp, Hayek and Rothbard alike, stands together on this. **Disagreement:** what the market would choose — competing fractional-reserve notes, 100 percent warehouses, an issuerless fixed-supply money, or all three side by side. That question is no longer a thought experiment; it is being answered in hundreds of billions of dollars of daily transactions, and you now have the tools to read the answer.

## @analogy
Think of the four note-issuing banks as **four shopping malls that print vouchers**, redeemable at any of the four and exchangeable for cash on sight at the issuing mall.

**The world with no central bank:** Mall A prints too many vouchers. Customers spend A vouchers at B, C and D; each week B, C and D bundle up the A vouchers they have collected and go to A for cash — and A's cash drawer empties fast. A has two choices: print less, immediately, or get run on. With no “Mall Regulatory Authority” whatever, A is held in check by its rivals' **redemption demands.** That is the free bankers' clearing discipline.

**The 100 percent camp** looks at the same picture and says: the problem is not that A printed more than the others, but that **every** mall printed more vouchers than the cash in its drawer — customers think a voucher is cash, when it is really the mall's IOU. If the four malls agree to over-print together, no rival runs on anyone, yet customers still hold more vouchers than there is cash. Their rule: vouchers must match the drawer one for one, and a mall that wants to lend opens a separate “loan counter” and tells customers plainly, “this money is locked for a year.”

**The Fed's world:** a “Master Mall” announces that any mall short of cash can borrow from it, and that it guarantees every customer's voucher. Now A no longer fears its rivals, B, C and D discover that over-printing carries no penalty, and all four print together. Vouchers multiply, each one buys less — and the Master Mall's own vouchers redeem into nothing at all.

**Today:** several new companies issue “dollar vouchers” online (stablecoins). Some really hold a dollar in the drawer for every voucher; one held vouchers of its own printing instead (Terra) — and was run to zero within a year. And there is a “voucher” with no issuing mall at all, fixed in quantity, that nobody can over-print (Bitcoin) — but that nobody guarantees the value of tomorrow either. Every old problem of the four malls is replaying in full — only this time you can see into the drawers.

## @misconceptions
- **“Without a central bank, banks would print at will and inflation would be worse.”** — Under free banking an over-issuing bank's notes flow to its rivals, who present them at the clearinghouse for reserves; the over-issuer loses gold and faces a run. Scotland had no central bank from 1716 to 1845, and prices and banks were more stable than in contemporary England. It is the central bank that dismantled this constraint (lender of last resort plus deposit insurance), so that over-issuing together goes unpunished.
- **“All Austrians favour 100 percent reserves.”** — No. The White–Selgin line favours free banking with fractional reserves and argues, via monetary equilibrium, that it handles swings in money demand better; Rothbard, Hoppe and Huerta de Soto favour 100 percent. Mises himself stood between the camps and both cite him. The consensus is only on ending the state monopoly.
- **“Scottish free banking proves fractional-reserve free banking is perfectly stable.”** — Rothbard pointed out that Scottish banks suspended gold payments from 1797 to 1821 with the Bank of England and held its notes as reserves, so they were not fully independent; White has replies, but both sides can cite the record. The honest statement: the Scottish system was relatively stable and clearing discipline genuinely worked, but it was not “pure” free banking.
- **“Hayek predicted private money would win, and Bitcoin and stablecoins prove him right.”** — Half right. Hayek imagined issuers pledging stable purchasing power, with the most stable winning; in reality people flocked to dollar-pegged stablecoins (network effects beat stability), while Bitcoin has no issuer and swings wildly. Currency competition has begun, but the market's choices do not match Hayek's forecast — a difficulty about network effects he himself acknowledged.
- **“100 percent reserves means banks cannot lend.”** — The 100 percent camp separates two businesses: demand deposits are deposits and must be fully backed; term funds are loans the depositor explicitly surrenders for a period, and the bank may on-lend them. Credit still exists; it must simply come from real saving rather than from fiduciary media created out of nothing.

   > The Fed's own liabilities redeem into nothing, so no clearing constraint applies to it; for commercial banks, the consequences of losing reserves are removed by the lender of last resort and deposit insurance — the “over-issuing together” the 100 percent camp feared, institutionalized.
