# Lange's Model & Why 'Market Socialism' Failed

## @hook
In 1936 the Polish economist Oskar Lange gave the socialist camp's cleverest reply to Mises: **you do not need private property to have prices — let a Central Planning Board (CPB) announce a set of prices, watch where surpluses and shortages appear, cut the price where there is a surplus and raise it where there is a shortage, and by trial and error the prices will converge to equilibrium.** This is “market socialism”: a simulated market in place of a real one. Mainstream economics declared Lange the winner. Hayek (1940) and Mises (1949) pointed to the fatal flaw: **trial and error requires someone to make the errors and bear them, and equilibrium is a destination at which an economy never arrives.** Yugoslavia and the reform experiments of the 1980s are the real-world footnotes to a paper debate.

## @intuition
Let us first state Lange's proposal in its strongest form — because it really is clever.

Mises said: without private ownership of the means of production there is no buying and selling of them, hence no prices. Lange replied: **you have defined “price” too narrowly.** What is a price *for* in economics? It is a “term on which alternatives are offered” — it tells you how much of one thing you must give up to get another. That function does not have to be produced by buying and selling.

Imagine a Central Planning Board. For every producer good — steel, electricity, machine-hours, labor-hours — it **announces an arbitrary starting price**, say steel at 600 per ton. It then issues two rules to every state-enterprise manager: (1) **at the given prices, minimize cost**; (2) **expand output until marginal cost equals price.** Managers need not worry whether the prices are “right”; they just follow the rules.

Then the CPB watches. If steel at 600 is in **shortage** (factories want more than the mills produce), the price is too low — raise it to 650. If it is in **surplus**, it is too high — cut to 550. Round after round, prices converge to the level at which supply equals demand — which is exactly the equilibrium price a competitive market would reach. **Walras's “tâtonnement,” executed by a planning board.**

Lange even argued the CPB would do better than a real market: it sees the whole picture, avoids the monopolies and cycles of real markets, and can set the savings rate and the income distribution directly according to social goals. He suggested, half in jest, that a statue of Mises should stand in the CPB's great hall — in gratitude for posing the question that forced socialists to think the answer through.

By the late 1930s most of the profession judged that Lange had won. Samuelson's textbook said so for decades.

So did the Austrians lose? No — but the Austrian reply has to be more careful than “no property, no prices.” Hayek's 1940 essay “Socialist Calculation: The Competitive ‘Solution’” and Chapter 26 of Mises's *Human Action* (1949) each found different holes; together they form this picture:

- **Lange solved a static problem; the economic problem is dynamic.** In a world where preferences, technology, and resources never change, trial and error does converge. But the real world changes daily — the tin mine of [[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]] can collapse at any moment. A CPB price round takes weeks or months; market prices move by the minute. **The CPB is forever chasing a target that has already moved.**
- **“Minimize cost” is not an executable instruction.** Costs are not objective data — which technique is cheaper depends on prices, and the manager does not know which techniques are possible until someone tries them. In a real market this is done by entrepreneurial **trial and error**, and those who guess wrong lose money and exit. In the CPB's world, who tries? And who bears the failed trials?
- **No capital market.** In a market, “should this factory be built?” is decided by investors voting with their own money; in Lange's model, investment is decided by the CPB, which has no price with which to evaluate a factory that does not yet exist.
- **Nobody bears losses.** Mises's phrase was “**playing market**” — like children playing house: the managers imitate the entrepreneur's motions without the entrepreneur's situation. A man who does not lose his own property when his decision is wrong is not an entrepreneur; he is a bureaucrat.

History supplied footnotes. From 1950 Yugoslavia ran “workers' self-management” — factories run by workers' councils, competing in markets — the closest large-scale experiment to Lange's model; the results were chronic high unemployment, high inflation, widening regional gaps, and a debt crisis in the 1980s. China's 1980s “dual-track” price system offers evidence from the other direction: the plan-track prices never came out right, and what brought the economy to life was **the ever-widening market track**. We will treat both cases carefully, because both are more complicated than textbooks make them.

Finally, Lange himself said in 1967 that if he were rewriting his essay he would simply say: feed the equations into an electronic computer and solve them in a second. That sentence carries the debate from the 1930s to today. [[bigdata-planning|Stage 7.5]] starts there.

**In this lesson we break it into five pieces:**

- **① Lange's proposal: simulating a market by trial and error (strongest version)**
- **② Hayek's 1940 reply: speed, cost, and the entrepreneur**
- **③ Mises's reply: “playing market” and the missing capital market**
- **④ Static vs dynamic: the real dividing line**
- **⑤ Real-world footnotes: Yugoslavia, the dual track, and Lange 1967**

## @mechanics
### ① Lange's proposal: simulating a market by trial and error (strongest version)

Some genealogy first. In 1908 the Italian economist Enrico Barone showed that, mathematically, the allocation problem facing a socialist “Ministry of Production” could be written as a system of simultaneous equations identical to those of a competitive market. In 1929 the American economist Fred M. Taylor, in his presidential address to the American Economic Association, proposed that since the equations were too many to solve, one should use **trial and error** — set prices, watch inventories change, adjust. In 1936–37 Lange systematized Taylor's idea in “On the Economic Theory of Socialism,” published in two parts in the *Review of Economic Studies*; Abba Lerner, in a series of contemporaneous articles and in *The Economics of Control* (1944), supplied the “price = marginal cost” rule. Together this is the “**Lange–Lerner model**,” or the “competitive solution.”

The model's core mechanism:

- **Consumer-goods and labor markets are real**: consumers spend wages, workers choose occupations freely. These markets form prices on their own.
- **There is no market for producer goods**, but there are **accounting prices**, set by the CPB.
- State managers obey two **parametric rules**: taking prices as given, (a) choose the factor combination that minimizes average cost; (b) expand output until marginal cost equals product price.
- The CPB observes **surpluses and shortages** of each producer good and adjusts by rule: shortage → raise, surplus → lower.
- Investment is set by the CPB according to a socially chosen savings rate; the interest rate is an accounting rate that equates investment demand to that rate.

Lange's direct rebuttals of Mises were two. First, Mises confused “price” with “market exchange ratio,” whereas a price in the economic sense is merely a term on which alternatives are offered, and accounting prices serve that purpose. Second, Mises said socialism was “impossible,” but if trial and error converges, it is possible — the rest is a question of efficiency.

The elegance of the proposal is that it **accepts Mises's whole problem framing** (prices are needed, calculation is needed) and then claims to satisfy it without private ownership. This is far more sophisticated than labor-hours or calculation in kind. It also correctly notes that some passages of Mises's 1920 essay do read as if “no exchange, no numbers,” and Lange offered another way of producing numbers.

### ② Hayek's 1940 reply: speed, cost, and the entrepreneur

Hayek edited *Collectivist Economic Planning* in 1935, which included the English translation of Mises's 1920 essay; in 1940 he published “Socialist Calculation: The Competitive ‘Solution’” in *Economica*, addressed to Lange and to H. D. Dickinson (*Economics of Socialism*, 1939). His critique has five layers.

**Layer one: the number and granularity of prices.** Lange speaks of “the price of steel” as if steel were one thing. In a real market there are hundreds of grades of steel, each with a different price at each location and delivery date; an old machine and a new one, a morning delivery and an afternoon one, are different goods. The CPB would have to set not thousands of prices but millions — and adjust each continuously.

**Layer two: the speed of adjustment.** Each round of trial and error requires: shortage observed → reported and aggregated → CPB meets → new prices published → factories adjust. Hayek reckoned this took weeks at least. In a real market, every transaction adjusts a price. By the time the CPB publishes, the cause of the shortage may have changed. **Lange's “convergence” assumes the world holds still while the CPB adjusts.**

**Layer three: “minimize cost” has no executable content.** Lange tells managers to “choose the least-cost factor combination.” Hayek asks: **how does the manager know which combinations are possible?** Cost is not looked up in a table — it depends on whether the manager knows of a new process, a supplier, a substitute material. In a market, this knowledge is “tried out” by rivals ([[competition-process|Stage 6.2]]); those who try wrong lose money, those who try right earn and are imitated. The CPB's rule assumes the set of possible techniques is known — a replay of the “given” problem of [[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]].

**Layer four: who is the entrepreneur?** Market adjustment is not accomplished by “price changes → rule-following response” but by **entrepreneurs anticipating how prices will change** ([[entrepreneur-alertness|Stage 6.1]]). A manager who acts only on current accounting prices is always one step behind; a manager who acts on his own judgment about the future is gambling — and if he loses, who pays? Lange's model has no place for entrepreneurs, only rule-following managers.

**Layer five: capital and risk.** Lange lets the CPB set total investment, but **which projects** get capital? Evaluating a factory that does not yet exist requires someone willing to stake his own property on his judgment of it. Without that, investment decisions become political decisions.

### ③ Mises's reply: “playing market” and the missing capital market

Mises answered Lange in *Human Action* (1949), Part 5, Chapter 26, “The Impossibility of Economic Calculation under Socialism.” His language was blunter than Hayek's, but the arguments are complementary.

**“Playing market.”** Lange's managers, Mises wrote, are like children playing war, playing railroad, playing school — they imitate the entrepreneur's motions without the entrepreneur's situation. A real entrepreneur stakes **his own** property on **his own judgment** about the future; if he is right his property grows, if he is wrong it shrinks or vanishes. That act of staking is the entire mechanism by which a market steers resources to their right uses (the profit and loss of [[profit-loss|Stage 6.3]]). A salaried manager's judgment does not make him poorer. “One cannot play speculation and investment. The speculators and investors expose their own wealth, their own destiny.” You cannot manufacture owners by ordering people to behave like owners.

**The capital market is the heart, and Lange cut it out.** Mises stressed that the crux of a market economy is not “how existing factories are run” but **“where capital should go”** — build a new plant or expand an old one, this industry or that, long projects or short ones (the re-shaping of production stages in [[saving-stages|Stage 3.3]]). In a market those decisions are made by stock markets, bond markets, banks, venture investors, and millions of savers using their own money. Lange has the CPB set the savings rate and allocate investment — but with what does the CPB judge “is this factory worth building”? No price can tell it, because the factory does not yet exist; its “price” could only come from the bids of people willing to bear its risk.

**Speculators are not parasites; they are the forward-looking part of the price.** Lange's model has no speculators. In a real market, the anticipatory quality of prices is precisely what speculators supply: they buy ahead of scarcity and sell ahead of glut, so prices begin moving before events arrive. Remove them and prices reflect only the past — which is why the CPB's “observe the shortage, then reprice” is always a step behind.

Mises had one more, deeper point, continuous with the calculation argument of [[mises-1920|Stage 7.1]]: Lange's accounting prices are **announced** by the CPB, not formed by **exchange** among owners. An announced number, however many rounds it is adjusted, is not a measure of “what others would give up for this ton of steel” — it is the CPB's **administrative reaction** to a shortage. Salerno-school Austrians regard this as the key difference between the Misesian and Hayekian replies ([[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]] ④): Hayek says the CPB is too slow; Mises says the CPB's numbers are not prices at all.

### ④ Static vs dynamic: the real dividing line

In hindsight, the most interesting feature of the debate is that the two sides **never agreed on what would count as solving the problem**.

Lange's framework is **Walrasian general equilibrium**: the economic problem is to find a price vector that clears all markets simultaneously. In that framework the market is merely a “solution algorithm” — Walras himself described its adjustment as “tâtonnement,” with an imaginary auctioneer calling prices, correcting, and calling again. Lange's contribution was to say: if the market is an auctioneer, replace it with a real one (the CPB). **Within the static framework, that argument holds.** The mainstream awarded Lange the victory because the mainstream lives inside that framework ([[vs-math-models|Stages 2.4]] and 11.3).

The Austrian framework is the **market process**: the economic problem is not “finding equilibrium” but “continuously re-coordinating plans in a world where preferences, technology, and resources never stop changing” ([[competition-process|Stage 6.2]]). In that framework equilibrium is never reached and the “solving” is never finished; what matters is not the destination but **the speed, direction, and error-correction of adjustment**. The market is not an auctioneer but a continuing contest started by entrepreneurs and refereed by profit and loss.

Once the line is drawn, much becomes clear:

- Lange says “trial and error converges” — Austrians ask, converges to what? To a target that moved while you were converging.
- Lange says “accounting prices are prices too” — Austrians say a price's worth lies not in being a number but in **who staked what, and why, to produce it**.
- Lange says “the CPB sees the whole and avoids cycles” — Austrians reply that the “whole” the CPB sees is reported statistics, which omit exactly the time-and-place and tacit knowledge of [[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]].

The Austrian side owes a confession here: **the Austrians of the 1930s did not draw this line clearly.** Part of Hayek's 1935 critique was “too many equations to solve” — a computational-complexity point that handed Lange the opening “then use trial and error,” and later the 1967 opening “then use a computer.” Only in the 1940s (Hayek 1945, 1948) and in Lavoie's reinterpretation (1985) did Austrians put the weight squarely on “process and discovery.” Looking back, Austrians won on substance but lost a round on debating technique in the 1930s — a case [[writing-argument|Stage 14.2]] uses when discussing how to argue in writing.

### ⑤ Real-world footnotes: Yugoslavia, the dual track, and Lange 1967

Beyond the paper debate, history offers a few approximate experiments. None is clean, so we tread carefully.

**Yugoslav self-management (from 1950).** After Tito broke with Stalin, Yugoslavia built a system of “workers' self-management”: enterprises nominally socially owned, run by workers' councils, competing in markets, profits shared among workers. It is the closest large-scale experiment to market socialism. The results: enterprises tended to distribute profits rather than invest and were reluctant to hire (new workers dilute the per-head payout), producing chronic unemployment and underinvestment; banks under political pressure kept lending to loss-making firms — what the Hungarian economist János Kornai called the “**soft budget constraint**” (1979–80) — fueling persistent inflation; a foreign-debt crisis and hyperinflation in the 1980s; and the system dissolved along with the country. It confirmed two Misesian points: without real owners, nobody is responsible for long-lived capital; without the hard constraint of losses, a “market” is form without substance.

**China's dual-track prices in the 1980s.** This case needs more care, because both sides cite it. In the 1980s China allowed enterprises to sell above-plan output at market prices alongside plan prices, creating a “plan track” and a “market track.” It **was not** the Lange model — the CPB did not adjust plan prices by trial and error; it was closer to a **transition mechanism**: the plan track preserved the old system's stock, while the market track allocated the increment at real prices. The market track's share then grew steadily, and by the mid-1990s most prices were market-determined. The Austrian reading: **the dynamism came from the expanding market track and de facto property rights (the household responsibility system, township-and-village enterprises), not from plan-track price-setting**; and the arbitrage and rent-seeking of the dual-track period (buy at plan price, sell at market price) were exactly the distortions that “two prices for one good” must produce ([[price-controls|Stage 8.2]]). Critics can reply that the stability of gradual transition was itself the dual track's achievement — and Austrians should concede that it involved far less chaos than “shock therapy.” But what it shows is that the market track worked, not that the plan track could calculate. Hungary's “New Economic Mechanism” of 1968 was another semi-marketizing attempt with a similar outcome: improvement without resolution.

**Lange 1967.** In a festschrift for Maurice Dobb, Lange published “The Computer and the Market.” Were he to rewrite his 1936 essay, he said, the task would be much simpler: “My answer to Hayek and Robbins would be: so what's the trouble? Let us put the simultaneous equations on an electronic computer and we shall obtain the solution in less than a second.” He even described the market's trial-and-error as a “computing device of the pre-electronic age” — a slow one, prone to cycles.

That sentence is both the turning point of the debate and its continuation: it shifts the question from “are there prices?” to “are there data and computing power?” And that is exactly the question [[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]] already answered and [[bigdata-planning|Stage 7.5]] answers again — **the equations can be solved, but where are the coefficients?** They are tacit, or generated only in the market process, or reported by people with an incentive to lie. Computers changed the speed of solving; they did not change where the coefficients come from. From Lange 1967 to *The People's Republic of Walmart* (2019) to “AI central planning” ([[ai-central-planning|Stage 18.1]]) is one argument reincarnated three times — each incarnation deserves to be taken seriously, and each gets stuck in the same place.

## @analogy
Imagine a city-building game with an odd rule: you are not the mayor. You are **the Price Office**.

The game has five goods: flour, electricity, steel, trucks, labor-hours. Each turn you may do one thing: look at which good is piling up in warehouses (surplus) and which has factories queuing for it (shortage), then nudge the former's price down and the latter's up. Factory managers act on your prices — use less of what is dear, more of what is cheap.

Game one: the world stands still. Population constant, no new products, always sunny. After a dozen turns all five prices settle; no surplus, no shortage. **You win — Lange was right.**

Game two: “realistic mode.” Each turn something random happens — residents suddenly fall in love with baking, the truck plant invents a model that uses half the steel, a power station fails, a new product called “electric car” appears and needs a “battery” that is not on your five-line price sheet at all. You can still adjust only once per turn. You discover: every time you finish adjusting, things have changed again; you raised the electricity price because of last turn's shortage, but this turn the station is repaired and electricity is in surplus. Worse: what price for “battery”? You have no row for it. **You are forever chasing last turn's world.**

Game three: you finally ask — may the factory managers set their own prices, decide what to make, keep the gains and eat the losses? The game answers: yes, but that is no longer Price Office mode. That mode is called “market.”

Those three games are the Lange–Austrian debate. Lange wins game one; Austrians say the real world only ever runs game two; and Mises adds that game three is not “game two with tweaks” — it is a different game, because someone **has put their own property on the line.**

## @misconceptions
- **“Lange proved socialism can have prices, so Mises was wrong.”** — Lange proved that in a static world of unchanging preferences, technology, and resources, administrative repricing converges. Austrians never denied that; they deny it has anything to do with a real economy, where prices move with every trade while the CPB takes weeks per round, forever chasing a target that has already moved.
- **“The Austrian objection to Lange was that there were too many equations to solve.”** — That was part of Hayek's 1930s critique, and Austrians later admitted it was a debating error, because it invited “then use trial and error / a computer.” The core objection is that nobody bears losses, there are no entrepreneurs, no capital market, and accounting prices are not formed by owners staking property — none of which concerns computing power.
- **“Yugoslavia failed because of ethnic conflict and external shocks, not market socialism.”** — Those factors were real, but the internal defects of self-management were predictable from theory: workers distributing profits rather than investing, refusing to hire because new workers dilute payouts, loss-makers kept alive by political credit (Kornai's soft budget constraint). These are direct consequences of having no real owners.
- **“China's dual track shows plan prices and market prices can coexist effectively.”** — The dual track was not the Lange model; the CPB did not reprice by trial and error. It was a transition mechanism: the market track allocated the increment, and its share grew until it replaced the plan track. The arbitrage and rent-seeking of the coexistence period were exactly the distortions “two prices” must produce.
- **“Lange said in 1967 that computers could solve the equations, so technology has settled the debate.”** — The speed of solving equations was never the issue; the coefficients (preferences, possible techniques, switching costs) are tacit, or generated only in the market process, or reported by people with an incentive to lie. Lange swapped “are there prices?” for “is there computing power?” — [[bigdata-planning|Stages 7.5]] and 18.1 deal with that swap directly.
