# ABCT in One Picture: How an Artificially Low Rate Lies

## @hook
The interest rate is not a price tag; it is a letter. Savers are telling entrepreneurs “this is how long we are willing to wait.” When the banking system expands credit with no new saving behind it and pushes the loan rate below the natural rate, the letter is forged: entrepreneurs read “people are willing to wait longer,” and they start longer, more roundabout projects — but consumers have not cut their spending by a cent. **Two groups are now fighting over the same pile of resources.** Costs and rates eventually snap back, the long projects are exposed as unprofitable, and half-finished structures litter the landscape. Mises (1912, 1928, 1949) and Hayek (1931) traced this chain link by link: **the boom is not health, the boom is the disease; the bust is not the disease, the bust is the diagnosis.**

## @intuition
Start with two things you already know.

**First ([[natural-rate|Stage 3.5]]):** the interest rate is the price of time preference. If someone lends 100 today to get 105 back next year, they are willing to wait a year for a 5% reward. A rate of 5% is the combined verdict of millions of savers and borrowers on “how long are we willing to wait.” Think of it as a letter that says: *this is how much present consumption society is willing to set aside for the future.*

**Second ([[fractional-reserve|Stage 4.4]]):** fractional-reserve banks can make new loans without anyone depositing a single extra dollar — the loan itself creates the deposit. When a central bank wants to “stimulate,” this is exactly what it does: it injects reserves, lowers the policy rate, and makes borrowing cheaper.

Now put the two together. The banking system pushes the loan rate from 5% down to 2% — **but nobody is one bit more willing to wait than they were yesterday.** Savers' time preference has not moved; their letter is unchanged. What changed is that the postman altered the “5” to a “2” on the way.

The entrepreneur cannot see the alteration. All he has is the number. What does 2% mean? It means “funding is cheap, society is patient.” So a project that pays back over 8 years — a new mine, a new chip fab, an entire office district, a supply chain built from scratch — which did not make sense at 5%, suddenly makes sense at 2%. He is not a fool. He has **done exactly the right thing given what the letter says.**

The trouble is that consumers have not cut back. Nobody drank less coffee, skipped a phone upgrade or cancelled a holiday to “free up” resources for a fab that will not produce anything for 8 years. **The long projects need workers, steel, power and engineers; consumer-goods production needs workers, steel, power and engineers.** Both sides are pulling the same rope. The rope tightens: wages rise, materials rise, and then rates rise — because banks cannot expand forever, and once prices are climbing the central bank no longer dares to hold the rate down.

When the rate returns to 5% — or overshoots to 7% because of inflation — the projects that only penciled out at 2% are exposed halfway through construction. No one will fund their completion; they are abandoned, sold for scrap, or repurposed. Half-dug mines, half-empty towers, startups nobody will refinance — that is the **bust**. And its true cause is not the day of the crash; it is the day, four years earlier, when the rate was forged.

There is one question this story cannot dodge, and Rothbard put it on the first pages of *America's Great Depression* (1963): **entrepreneurs make their living by forecasting, and the market weeds out bad forecasters every day — so why would they all err at once?** One firm choosing the wrong project is routine. Thousands choosing wrong simultaneously and collapsing together demands a common cause. The Austrian answer: they were all reading **the same forged letter** — the interest rate.

This lesson does one thing: it draws that chain as a single picture. [[boom-malinvestment|Stage 5.2]] opens up what happens inside the boom, [[bust-liquidation|Stage 5.3]] explains why the liquidation is the cure, [[abct-history|Stage 5.4]] reads 1929, 2000, 2008 and 2020–22 through this lens, and [[abct-critiques|Stage 5.5]] lets the critics take the stand. [[garrison-macro|Stage 10.1]] turns it into a formal macro model with Garrison's three diagrams, and [[ai-bubble-abct|Stage 18.5]] asks whether today's AI capital-spending frenzy looks like this picture.

**In this lesson we break it into five pieces:**

- **① The rate is a letter: what savers are actually saying**
- **② How credit expansion forges the letter: 5% becomes 2%**
- **③ Entrepreneurs act on the letter: why long projects are the most rate-sensitive**
- **④ Tug-of-war: consumers didn't cut back, and there aren't enough resources for both ends**
- **⑤ The snap and the “cluster of errors”: Rothbard's question and the Austrian answer**

## @mechanics
### ① The rate is a letter: what savers are actually saying

Knut Wicksell (*Interest and Prices*, 1898) distinguished two rates: the **natural rate** — the rate at which saving and investment would meet if lending happened in kind, with no banks in between — and the **market rate**, the loan rate banks actually charge. Mises took the pair over in *The Theory of Money and Credit* (1912) and wired it to the Austrian theory of time preference ([[time-preference|Stage 3.1]]): the natural rate is not the “productivity of capital” but **the expression of society's overall time preference** — how much present people will forgo for the future.

Why call it a letter? Because the rate coordinates two groups who never meet:

- **Savers**, by consuming less and lending, are in effect saying: “We don't need these resources for consumer goods right now — use them for things that take time.”
- **Entrepreneurs**, by borrowing, are saying: “I'll take these resources and build something that won't produce output for years.”

A rate of 5% means the two sets of plans line up at 5%: the resources savers release are just enough for the long projects entrepreneurs want to launch at that rate. Hayek drew this as a triangle in *Prices and Production* (1931) ([[roundabout-production|Stage 3.2]]): the base is production time, the height is the value of final consumer goods. **When real saving rises, the rate falls and the triangle gets longer and flatter** — people are willing to wait more, so society can afford more roundabout, more productive processes ([[saving-stages|Stage 3.3]]).

Everything hangs on the word *real*. When the rate falls because of real saving, **consumption really has fallen** — resources really have been released from consumer-goods production and can flow to the long projects. The letter is genuine.

### ② How credit expansion forges the letter: 5% becomes 2%

Now let the banking system do what [[fractional-reserve|Stage 4.4]] described: lend without new saving. The central bank buys bonds, injects reserves, and banks push new loans into the market at lower rates. The loan rate falls from 5% to 2%.

In *Monetary Stabilization and Cyclical Policy* (1928) Mises called this **circulation credit** — not “commodity credit” that passes one person's saving to another, but purchasing power created out of nothing. In the market it looks identical to real saving: a sum available to borrow, a lower rate. **There is nothing in the number 2% that lets an entrepreneur tell whether it means “people are willing to wait more” or “this was printed.”**

In numbers: an economy earns 1,000 a year; people save 100 and consume 900. The natural rate of 5% is the rate at which investment demand equals that 100. Now banks conjure another 100 of loans — investment funding becomes 200, but consumption is still 900. **Income of 1,000; spending plans of 1,100.** The extra 100 corresponds to no resources anyone has actually released; it can only get resources by bidding them away from someone else. This is what Mises called **forced saving**: not savers voluntarily setting consumption aside, but holders of the new money elbowing consumers out with higher prices (the Cantillon effect of [[cantillon-inflation|Stage 4.3]] returns here — the new money reaches borrowers first, and they buy at the old prices).

### ③ Entrepreneurs act on the letter: why long projects are the most rate-sensitive

This is the step people most often get wrong, so let us do the arithmetic.

A **long project**: invest 100 today, collect 14.3 a year for 8 years starting next year (about 114 in total). Whether it is worth doing depends on whether those 8 payments, discounted back to today at the going rate, exceed 100:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{at }5\%\text{:} &\quad 14.3 \times 6.46 \approx 92.4 \;\rightarrow\; \NPV \approx -7.6\text{, don't build} \\
\text{at }3\%\text{:} &\quad 14.3 \times 7.02 \approx 100.4 \;\rightarrow\; \NPV \approx 0\text{, marginal} \\
\text{at }2\%\text{:} &\quad 14.3 \times 7.33 \approx 104.7 \;\rightarrow\; \NPV \approx +4.7\text{, build!}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now a **short project**: invest 100, collect 54 in each of the next two years (108 in total):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{at }5\%\text{:} &\quad 54 \times 1.86 \approx 100.4 \;\rightarrow\; \NPV \approx +0.4 \\
\text{at }2\%\text{:} &\quad 54 \times 1.94 \approx 104.9 \;\rightarrow\; \NPV \approx +4.9
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice two things. First, the cut from 5% to 2% improves the long project's NPV by about 12.3 and the short project's by only about 4.5 — **the longer the payback, the more sensitive to the rate** (finance calls this “duration”). Second, the long project should not exist at 5% at all, yet at 2% it becomes a “good business.” So credit expansion does not make all investment “a little bigger”; it **systematically tilts capital toward the projects with the longest payback, furthest from the consumer**: mines, infrastructure, chip fabs, real estate, platform startups, mergers — [[boom-malinvestment|Stage 5.2]] goes through them one by one.

This is the shape of the Hayekian triangle in a boom: **the base is stretched** (more upstream, more roundabout stages break ground) while **the height is not shortened to match** (consumption has not fallen) — both ends inflate at once. Garrison's *Time and Money* (2001) draws this as a tug-of-war, which [[garrison-macro|Stage 10.1]] develops formally.

### ④ Tug-of-war: consumers didn't cut back, and there aren't enough resources for both ends

The boom looks wonderful from inside. Upstream firms break ground, hire, raise wages; workers take the higher wages and buy more consumer goods; consumer-goods firms expand and hire too. **GDP up, employment up, stocks up.** But notice: the economy's real resources — labor, materials, machines, time — have not multiplied. Only the money has.

Three things then happen, in order:

- **Factor prices rise.** Long projects and consumer-goods producers bid for the same engineers and the same steel. Wages and input prices climb, and the long projects blow through their budgets. In year 3 of an 8-year project, the remaining 5 years turn out to cost 30% more than planned.
- **Consumer prices rise.** Workers spend their higher wages on consumer goods whose capacity has not expanded (part of the resources went upstream). Prices rise and profits flow back to the stages nearest consumption — Hayek's “Ricardo effect”: **consumer goods become relatively dearer, which makes short, labor-intensive production profitable again** and pulls capital back down from upstream.
- **Rates rebound.** Bank reserves are not infinite, and once inflation shows up the central bank stops holding the rate down — and may have to hike ([[central-banks-fiat|Stage 4.5]]). The loan rate goes back to 5%; inflation expectations push it to 7%. Projects that only worked at 2% lose their funding overnight.

Mises stressed in Chapter XX of *Human Action* (1949) that **a boom cannot be sustained forever by more credit expansion.** To keep it going, each round of expansion must be bigger than the last — and the end is either the banks stopping (the bust) or the public losing faith in the money altogether (the crack-up boom of [[cantillon-inflation|Stage 4.3]]). Neither road bypasses the liquidation.

### ⑤ The snap and the “cluster of errors”: Rothbard's question and the Austrian answer

Back to Rothbard's question. Any business-cycle theory must explain two facts. First, **why errors come in clusters** — not one firm misjudging, but whole industries and whole economies misjudging together. Second, **why the capital-goods industries (upstream) swing far more than consumer-goods industries** — in every bust, mining, construction, machinery and raw materials fall far harder than food and household staples.

The mainstream answers — “deficient demand,” “animal spirits,” “a collapse of confidence” — cannot handle the first: why did confidence collapse for everyone on the same day? Nor the second: if it were simply aggregate demand shrinking, why does the damage land so precisely upstream?

ABCT answers both at once:

- **Errors cluster** because every entrepreneur is reading the same price — the interest rate. It is the one number in the economy that prices *time*, and every feasibility calculation for a long project uses it. Once it is forged, errors are not randomly scattered but **systematically biased in one direction**: too long, too far, too roundabout. Entrepreneurs have not lost their judgment ([[entrepreneur-alertness|Stage 6.1]]); their judgment has been fed false data.
- **Upstream swings more** because long projects are the most rate-sensitive (the duration arithmetic of ③): they inflate first when the rate is pushed down and collapse first when it rebounds. The boom–bust amplitude of the capital-goods industries *is* the amplitude of the triangle's base being stretched and pulled back.

Rothbard added an honest qualification: this is a theory of the **upper turning point** — why the boom must end and why the errors must surface. How long the bust lasts, and how policy can turn a liquidation into a decade of stagnation, needs a separate analysis ([[bust-liquidation|Stage 5.3]], [[case-great-depression|Stage 13.2]]). Nor does it claim every recession is a credit cycle — recessions caused by war, plague or lockdown are a different animal ([[abct-history|Stage 5.4]]). And it is not a forecasting machine: it tells you **what is buried in the boom**, not **which day it blows** ([[abct-critiques|Stage 5.5]], [[common-mistakes|Stage 14.4]]).

The whole lesson in one sentence: **credit expansion makes the interest rate lie; entrepreneurs honestly act on the lie; consumers do not cooperate; there are not enough resources; the lie is exposed in the projects furthest from the consumer.** This picture is the foundation for the next four lessons — and the one you will keep pulling out when you read Fed statements in [[reading-the-fed|Stage 13.5]], ask whether a Bitcoin standard would still have cycles in [[bitcoin-cycles|Stage 17.5]], and weigh AI capital spending in [[ai-bubble-abct|Stage 18.5]].

## @analogy
Mises gave the best analogy himself in *Human Action*, so we will borrow it: **the master builder and the bricks.**

A master builder is going to put up a house. He counts the bricks in the warehouse, draws the plans, orders materials, and starts. Now suppose the warehouse clerk lied — the ledger says 120,000 bricks, but there are really only 100,000. The builder doesn't know. He designs a bigger house for 120,000 bricks: wider foundations, more floors, longer corridors.

For months everything goes well. The foundations are beautiful, the crew is energized, the neighbors say “what a magnificent building.” **That is the boom** — not because there are more bricks, but because the builder believes there are.

At brick number 100,000 the truth comes out: the house is two-thirds done and the bricks are gone. The builder now has three options. One: tear down part of what is built and use those bricks to finish a smaller house — that is **liquidation**, painful but it yields a house you can live in. Two: stop work and let the shell weather in the rain — that is **prolonged stagnation**. Three: the clerk forges another ledger so the builder keeps building — that is **another round of credit expansion**; the house gets bigger, the shortfall gets worse, and the eventual tear-down is harsher.

Mises's point: **the earlier the error is discovered, the smaller the loss.** Discover the shortfall while laying foundations and you just redraw the plans; discover it on the eighth floor and there is a great deal to demolish. That is why Austrians oppose using more credit to “extend the boom” — it does not save the house, it postpones the discovery to the moment when the loss is greatest.

The clerk who forged the ledger is the banking system that pushed the rate from 5% to 2%; the phantom 120,000 bricks are the false signal that “society is willing to wait longer”; the builder did nothing wrong, he just followed the numbers — **a cluster of errors doesn't need a crowd of fools, only one forged ledger.**

## @misconceptions
- **“ABCT says there is ‘too much investment,’ so Austrians are against investment.”** — No. The key word is malinvestment, not overinvestment: capital is steered into projects too long and too far from the consumer, projects consumers are not actually willing to wait for. Investment financed by real saving, however large, causes no cycle — consumption really fell, resources really were released. The problem is never “too much,” it is “in the wrong place.”
- **“Low interest rates are bad in themselves; Austrians want high rates.”** — Austrians object only to rates pushed artificially below the natural rate. A low rate produced by more saving is good news: it means society is willing to wait longer and can afford more roundabout production. The test is not how high the rate is, but whether it reflects genuine time preference.
- **“The bust happens because confidence suddenly collapses or demand suddenly falls short.”** — That is the symptom, not the cause. Why did confidence collapse on that particular day? ABCT's answer: because that is when the half-built projects were discovered not to pencil out. Demand-shortfall stories cannot explain why every bust lands hardest on upstream capital-goods industries rather than evenly across the economy.
- **“As long as the central bank never hikes, the boom can last forever.”** — Mises addressed this directly in Human Action: sustaining the boom requires each round of credit expansion to be larger than the last; the real resource constraint does not vanish because there is more money, so factor prices and consumer prices keep climbing until either the central bank stops or the currency itself breaks (the crack-up boom). Neither road avoids the liquidation.
- **“ABCT predicts every recession, so Austrians are the best market timers.”** — It is a theory of what is buried inside a boom and why the upper turning point must come, not of which day it comes. Recessions caused by war, pandemic or lockdown are outside its scope. Treating it as a forecasting machine is exactly the “permabear” habit [[common-mistakes|Stage 14.4]] criticizes.
