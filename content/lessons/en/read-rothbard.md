# Rothbard: Man, Economy, and State & America's Great Depression

## @hook
If Mises's *Human Action* is a city, Rothbard's *Man, Economy, and State* (1962) is its **zoning map**: starting from the action axiom and proceeding in textbook order, chapter by chapter, through money, monopoly and intervention, with numerical examples and diagrams at every step. A year later, *America's Great Depression* (1963) took that theory to a real stretch of history — the credit expansion of the 1920s and Hoover's interventions. This lesson shows you how to read both books: their structure, their central claims, where they are **faithful** to Mises and where they **openly depart** from him (monopoly price, interest, 100-percent reserves), and — stated plainly — Rothbard's strengths as a system-builder and the controversies he generated as a polemicist.

## @intuition
Start with Rothbard's place in the school, because it determines how you should read him.

When Mises finished *Human Action* in 1949 he was 68; the book distilled decades of thought but was not written for students — no exercises, no diagrams, and many arguments left for the reader to complete. In the early 1950s the William Volker Fund commissioned the young Rothbard to write a “textbook version of *Human Action*.” As he wrote, Rothbard found that too many of Mises's arguments needed filling in, and he started deriving from scratch. What he delivered ten years later was not a textbook but an independent treatise of nearly a thousand pages — *Man, Economy, and State* (*MES*). Its final section, *Power and Market*, a complete taxonomy of intervention, was cut by the original publisher as too radical and appeared separately only in 1970; the 2004 Scholar's Edition from the Mises Institute rejoined the two, and that is today's standard text.

So *MES* has two faces. **One is a faithful systematization of Mises** — the action axiom, time preference, the regression theorem, ABCT, all present. **The other is Rothbard's revisions** — three places where he says plainly that Mises was wrong or not thorough enough: there is no “monopoly price” on a free market; interest is determined entirely by time preference, with no role for productivity; fractional-reserve banking is fraud and reserves should be 100 percent. These three are the clearest forks inside the school ([[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]]).

The second book, *America's Great Depression* (1963), does something different. It has a definite thesis: **the crash of 1929 was the inevitable result of the Federal Reserve's credit expansion of 1921–29, and the depression dragged on for a decade because Hoover — not Roosevelt — was the first president to block liquidation with large-scale intervention.** That is the reverse of the mainstream story then and now, which says the 1920s had no inflation because prices were stable and that Hoover was a hands-off “liquidationist.” Rothbard's method is what [[empirical-method|Stage 13.1]] calls “theory plus history”: first establish the logic of the cycle with ABCT, then use statistics and documents to show that this episode fits the pattern. The book shaped the main non-Friedmanite account of the Depression and drew hard criticism — over its measure of the money supply and over whether it exaggerated Hoover's role.

Third, you need to know three more Rothbard books, each of which retells part of *MES* for a general reader: *What Has Government Done to Our Money?* (1963, a pamphlet introduction to monetary theory), *The Mystery of Banking* (1983, the mechanics of fractional reserves and central banks), and *The Ethics of Liberty* (1982, which supplies the natural-rights theory *MES* deliberately left out).

Finally, said plainly: **Rothbard is the Austrian School's strongest system-builder and its most controversial figure.** His strength is clarity, thoroughness, and pushing every argument to its logical end; his controversies come from his polemical style (merciless toward Hayek, the Chicago School and fellow Austrians alike), from his late political alliances (from the New Left of the 1960s to the “paleo” right of the 1990s), and from binding his economics so tightly to anarcho-capitalist political philosophy that many readers cannot evaluate the two separately. This course's stance: **read his economics with the political philosophy in brackets; when you have finished, open the brackets and judge for yourself.**

**In this lesson we break it into five pieces:**

- **① The structure of *MES*: from the action axiom to *Power and Market*, twelve chapters plus one book**
- **② Where it follows Mises and where it leaves him: monopoly price, interest, 100-percent reserves**
- **③ *America's Great Depression*: thesis, method, evidence and critics**
- **④ Three “small books”: the money pamphlet, *The Mystery of Banking*, *The Ethics of Liberty***
- **⑤ How to read Rothbard: strengths, controversies, and an honest procedure**

## @mechanics
### ① The structure of *MES*: from the action axiom to *Power and Market*, twelve chapters plus one book

The Scholar's Edition of *MES* (2004, Mises Institute, about 1,400 pages including *Power and Market*) is a strict deductive chain. Unlike *Human Action* it **contains no epistemology** — Rothbard put his methodology into separate essays such as “In Defense of ‘Extreme Apriorism’” (1957) and “Praxeology: The Methodology of Austrian Economics” (1976) — and begins deducing from the axiom on page one:

- **Chapter 1, Fundamentals of Human Action.** The action axiom and its implications: ends and means, time, uncertainty, scales of value, marginal utility (with Rothbard's famous horses-and-grain numerical example), the law of returns, the disutility of labor. A **compression and formalization** of Part One of *Human Action* — if you read Chapters 1, 4 and 7 in [[read-human-action|Stage 12.2]], this reads like worked solutions.
- **Chapter 2, Direct Exchange.** From isolated Crusoe to two-person exchange to a many-person market; demand and supply schedules derive price formation between marginal pairs ([[price-formation|Stage 1.3]]); the market demand curve as a summation of individual valuations. The most diagram-heavy chapter in the book.
- **Chapter 3, The Pattern of Indirect Exchange.** The emergence of money (Menger's evolution, [[origin-of-money|Stage 4.1]]), money prices and economic calculation, the demand for money.
- **Chapter 4, Prices and Consumption.** Consumer-goods pricing, purchasing power, the role of speculation, shifts in demand.
- **Chapter 5, Production: The Structure.** The structure of production, capital goods, time. Here Rothbard introduces his “production-stage table” — a horizontal numerical layout (100 units of consumer goods → 80 units of first-order capital goods → 60 → 40 …) that turns the Hayekian triangle into an accounting table.
- **Chapter 6, Production: The Rate of Interest and Its Determination.** The **pure time-preference theory of interest**: interest is set entirely by time preference; capital's “productivity” plays no role. A direct rejection of Böhm-Bawerk's “third ground” and Fisher's two-factor “impatience plus opportunity” — one of *MES*'s most important theoretical contributions (② below).
- **Chapter 7, Production: General Pricing of the Factors.** Factor pricing by marginal-productivity imputation; Rothbard adopts the neoclassical language of discounted marginal value product (DMVP) but grounds it in subjective imputation.
- **Chapter 8, Production: Entrepreneurship and Change.** Profit and loss, the entrepreneur, uncertainty; the ERE used exactly as in *Human Action*.
- **Chapter 9, Production: Particular Factor Prices and Productive Incomes.** Firm size and the limits of vertical integration — the **“one big firm cannot calculate” argument**: if a firm absorbs the entire market for some capital good it loses the ability to price that good, so firm size has an intrinsic ceiling on the free market (used in [[monopoly-question|Stages 6.4]] and 15.2).
- **Chapter 10, Monopoly and Competition.** The most contested chapter: Rothbard argues that “monopoly price” is an **undefinable** concept on a free market and that only government-granted privilege creates monopoly (② below).
- **Chapter 11, Money and Its Purchasing Power.** The demand for and supply of money, the definition of inflation, non-neutrality, and the analysis of fractional-reserve banking that leads to his 100-percent reserve position.
- **Chapter 12, The Economics of Violent Intervention in the Market.** The threefold classification (below); in the Scholar's Edition this leads straight into *Power and Market*.
- ***Power and Market* (1970).** The full taxonomy: **autistic intervention** (direct coercion of one person, e.g. a prohibition), **binary intervention** (a coerced exchange between the intervener and an individual, e.g. taxation, conscription), **triangular intervention** (the intervener interfering in an exchange between two others, e.g. price controls, licensing). Each category is then analyzed in turn: taxation (including the rejection of a “neutral tax”), price controls, antitrust, licensing, tariffs, labor legislation, welfare — ending with a chapter on ethical critiques of the market. It is the complete map behind [[intervention-logic|Stages 8.1–8.4]], and the economic side of Rothbard's later anarcho-capitalism.

### ② Where it follows Mises and where it leaves him: monopoly price, interest, 100-percent reserves

**The faithful part** (where you can safely treat *MES* as the textbook version of *Human Action*): the action axiom and its implications; the ordinal nature of marginal utility; the regression theorem; the non-neutrality of money and Cantillon effects; the use of the ERE; the theory of the entrepreneur and profit; the impossibility of socialist calculation; the basic chain of ABCT; the critique of interventionism's “middle of the road.” In all of these Rothbard **adds examples, diagrams and rigor** — for instance the production-stage table and the full numerical derivation of marginal pairs that *Human Action* lacks.

**The three departures**, each of which you must judge for yourself:

**Fork one: monopoly price (Chapter 10).** Mises (*Human Action*, Chapter 16) holds that if the whole supply of a resource is controlled by one seller and demand is inelastic above the “competitive price,” the seller can restrict sales and obtain a “monopoly price” higher than the competitive one — and that this can happen on a free market. Rothbard's reply: **what is the “competitive price”?** In a real market you cannot observe the price “that would have prevailed without monopoly”; every seller sets output according to expected demand and holds part of the supply off the market, so “restricting sales to raise price” is indistinguishable from ordinary pricing. Therefore “monopoly price” has no operational definition on a free market; the only meaningful definition of monopoly is **a government-granted exclusive privilege** (licenses, patents, tariffs). The argument was widely adopted by later Austrians ([[monopoly-question|Stages 6.4]] and 15.2 both use it), but some — Kirzner among them — think Rothbard threw away a useful tool for analyzing “resource monopoly.”

**Fork two: interest (Chapter 6).** Böhm-Bawerk gave “three grounds” for interest: differences between present and future provision, systematic underestimation of the future, and **the technical superiority of roundabout production** (the third ground — capital's “productivity”). Fisher simplified this to “impatience plus opportunity.” Mises elevated time preference to a category of action but did not entirely exclude productivity. Rothbard says: **productivity cannot explain interest**, because if a capital good yields higher output, competition bids its price up until that extra output is fully capitalized — whatever interest remains can only come from “present money is worth more than future money,” i.e. time preference. This is the **pure time-preference theory** (PTPT). It is now the standard position of the Mises Institute wing; the GMU wing and outside critics hold that it neglects the effect of the capital stock on the rate. The stance taken in [[time-preference|Stages 3.1]] and 3.5 is PTPT — now you know its source and its critics.

**Fork three: 100-percent reserves (Chapter 11, and *The Mystery of Banking*).** Mises, in 1912 and 1949 alike, favored a gold standard with free banking, arguing that competing banks would restrain one another ([[fractional-reserve|Stages 4.4]] and 9.4); his critique of fractional reserves was mainly **economic** (they cause cycles). Rothbard goes further: lending out demand deposits issues two titles to the same money, which is **fraud** in law (like a warehouse issuing more receipts than it holds), and should be prohibited regardless of consequences. That is a **legal-ethical** argument stacked on the economic one. The school split accordingly into a “100-percent” wing (Rothbard, Huerta de Soto, Hülsmann, Salerno) and a “free-banking” wing (White, Selgin, closer to Hayek's and Mises's original view). When [[bitcoin-cycles|Stage 17.5]] asks whether a Bitcoin standard would have cycles, the two wings give different answers.

### ③ *America's Great Depression*: thesis, method, evidence and critics

**Structure.** Three parts. Part I, “Business Cycle Theory” (three chapters: ABCT restated, then replies to the critiques current in 1963 — Keynesian, “over-investment,” “under-consumption”). Part II, “The Inflationary Boom: 1921–1929” (four chapters tracking money supply, Fed policy and international factors year by year — especially the 1927 easing to help Britain hold the gold standard). Part III, “The Great Depression: 1929–1933” (four chapters recording what Hoover did, year by year).

**Thesis one: the 1920s were an inflationary decade despite stable prices.** The mainstream (including Friedman and Schwartz later) held there was no inflation in the 1920s because consumer prices barely moved. By Rothbard's own measure, the money supply rose from about $45.3 billion in June 1921 to about $73.0 billion in June 1929, an increase of about 61 percent, roughly 7.7 percent a year. Prices did not rise because productivity gains should have made them **fall** — an effect offset by credit expansion. This is exactly the “fallacy of price stabilization” Hayek's 1929 book had warned of ([[read-hayek|Stage 12.3]]). **Critics:** Rothbard's monetary aggregate is extremely broad (it includes life-insurance reserves, savings-and-loan shares and more), which critics say overstates the expansion; narrower measures grow far more slowly. This is a genuine methodological dispute — read his appendix and judge which measure is more reasonable.

**Thesis two: Hoover was an interventionist, not a liquidationist.** The standard story is that Hoover believed in letting markets clear, stood aside as the slump worsened, and intervention began only with Roosevelt. Rothbard uses Hoover's own speeches and policies against it: in November 1929 Hoover summoned business leaders and demanded that **wages be maintained** (so real wages rose as prices fell and unemployment deepened); the Smoot–Hawley tariff of 1930; the Federal Farm Board's price-support purchases of 1931; the Reconstruction Finance Corporation's bailouts of banks and railroads in 1932; large tax increases. Rothbard's conclusion: **Hoover did what Roosevelt later did, only on a smaller scale** — and those interventions blocked liquidation and stretched a recession that should have ended in two years into a decade. The first half of the thesis has since been partly absorbed by mainstream historians (“Hoover was no laissez-faire president” is now common knowledge); the causal claim that intervention prolonged the depression remains contested.

**Method.** This is the model for [[empirical-method|Stage 13.1]]'s “explain, don't test”: the theory (ABCT) is not being **tested** by the episode — it was derived a priori; the historical material shows that the **particular facts** of the period fit the pattern the theory describes. You may reject the method (Friedman's methodology demands falsifiable theory), but you must see what Rothbard is doing before you can criticize it fairly. [[case-great-depression|Stage 13.2]] sets the book against Friedman and Schwartz's *Monetary History* head to head.

**Influence and critics.** The book is the standard Austrian account of the Depression and, after 2008, the door through which many readers re-entered the school. Criticism concentrates on three points: the monetary aggregate; too little attention to international factors (the breakdown of the gold standard, the European banking crisis of 1931); and its treatment of the **monetary contraction** of 1929–33 — Rothbard sees it as a normal part of liquidation, Friedman as the Fed's dereliction, and the late Hayek conceded that “secondary deflation” was genuinely harmful ([[read-hayek|Stage 12.3]]). **This is a point of disagreement both inside and outside the school; be honest with yourself about it.**

### ④ Three “small books”: the money pamphlet, *The Mystery of Banking*, *The Ethics of Liberty*

***What Has Government Done to Our Money?* (1963).** A pamphlet of about 100 pages and many readers' first Austrian book. Three parts: how money works in a free society (Menger's evolution, the monetary unit, why more money does not mean more wealth); how government interferes (seigniorage, legal tender, fractional reserves, central banks, leaving gold); and the breakdown of the Western monetary order (from the classical gold standard through Bretton Woods to 1971). **How to read:** one afternoon; it is the popular version of *MES* Chapters 3 and 11 and the skeleton of [[central-banks-fiat|Stage 4.5]]. Later editions append *The Case for a 100 Percent Gold Dollar*.

***The Mystery of Banking* (1983).** About 300 pages on how fractional reserves and central banks actually operate: the deposit multiplier, reserve requirements, open-market operations, how a central bank coordinates simultaneous expansion across banks. Far more concrete than *MES* Chapter 11, and more polemical — its purpose is to show the central bank is an “inflation machine.” **How to read:** Chapters 1–8 for the mechanics; the American banking history from Chapter 9 on pairs well with Part II of *America's Great Depression*. [[fractional-reserve|Stage 4.4]]'s credit-expansion simulator uses its multiplier framework.

***The Ethics of Liberty* (1982).** The thing *MES* deliberately **did not** do: *MES* is value-free in method (*Power and Market* analyzes the **consequences** of intervention without saying intervention is “wrong”), and Rothbard saved the ethics for this book — from self-ownership and homesteading to a system of property rights and thence to the illegitimacy of the state (anarcho-capitalism). **How to read:** it is not economics; read it to understand why Rothbard ended where he did, and to compare with Hayek's evolutionary argument ([[read-hayek|Stage 12.3]]) and Hoppe's argumentation ethics ([[read-contemporary|Stage 12.5]]). **This course's position:** the economics does not depend on this book; you can accept all of *MES* and reject *The Ethics of Liberty*, as many Austrian economists do.

### ⑤ How to read Rothbard: strengths, controversies, and an honest procedure

**Strengths.** Three. First, **clarity**: Rothbard is the most readable author in the school — short sentences, many examples, no detours; you always know what he is claiming. Second, **thoroughness**: he pushes every argument to its logical end with no “on the whole” or “perhaps” — the exact opposite of Hayek, which is why [[writing-argument|Stage 14.2]] uses the two as a contrast in writing. Third, **system**: *MES* is the only complete Austrian system after *Human Action* and the only one written in teaching order; *Power and Market*'s threefold classification remains the most usable framework for analyzing intervention.

**Controversies**, stated plainly. First, **polemical style**: he was cutting toward Hayek (“not thorough enough”), toward Chicago (“social engineers”), and toward fellow Austrians Kirzner and Lachmann; as a result his economics is often judged as “Rothbardian” rather than Austrian. Second, **political alliances**: he worked with the New Left against the war in the 1960s, helped found the Libertarian Party in the 1970s, and turned to the “paleo” coalition in the 1990s — each alliance stamped his economics with that era's political label. Third, **bundling economics with political philosophy**: readers struggle to separate *MES* from the anarchism of *The Ethics of Liberty*, though the two are logically **independent**. Fourth, **empirical method**: the monetary aggregate and causal inference of *America's Great Depression* drew substantive criticism to which Rothbard never fully replied.

**An honest procedure:**

1. **Read *MES* Chapters 1–4 first**, treating them as the solutions manual to Part Four of *Human Action*; at the end of each chapter, redo Rothbard's numerical examples yourself (the horses-and-grain table and the production-stage table both redraw well in a notebook).
2. **In Chapters 6, 10 and 11, write Mises's version in the margin** (② above gives you the three forks), then add one sentence: whom I agree with, and why. This is your preparation for [[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]].
3. **Read *America's Great Depression* with two critiques in hand**: is the monetary aggregate too broad? Is the treatment of monetary contraction sound? After Part II, turn to the appendix and inspect his tables.
4. **Leave *The Ethics of Liberty* for last**, and tell yourself explicitly: the economic conclusions do not depend on it.
5. **Handle the polemical tone** as you did with Mises in [[read-human-action|Stage 12.2]]: treat every barb as a signpost — “there is an argument here.”

When you have read Rothbard you will hold the clearest and most thorough version of the school. The next lesson ([[read-contemporary|Stage 12.5]]) shows how contemporary authors divide between him and Hayek; [[case-great-depression|Stage 13.2]] pits *America's Great Depression* against Friedman; and [[common-mistakes|Stage 14.4]] uses the Rothbardian wing's “permabear” tendency and failed hyperinflation calls to show the cost of the virtue called thoroughness.

## @analogy
Think of Mises as an **old architect** who drew the master plan of a city (*Human Action*) — precise lines, but with many spots annotated only “handle in the usual way.”

Rothbard is the **young chief engineer** who took over. He turned the master plan into construction drawings (*MES*): the load on every beam, the route of every pipe, a floor plan for every level, a number on every room. The construction crews (students) could finally build from the drawings. That is his strength — without him, many people never get into the city at all.

But the young engineer changed the drawings in three places. The old architect had left a room called “monopoly price”; the engineer said it could not be built and removed it. On the “interest” floor the architect had left two columns (time preference and productivity); the engineer said the second bore no load and removed it. The architect had allowed the bank building to stand on half a foundation (fractional reserves); the engineer called it an illegal structure and demanded a full one. All three changes had engineering reasons; all three had critics who said he went too far.

Then he went out to a field of ruins beyond the walls (1929–33) and said, drawings in hand: **this was not an earthquake; it was shoddy construction in the 1920s (credit expansion) plus someone after 1929 who refused to demolish the unsafe buildings (Hoover).** The survey report is very persuasive, though some say the tape measure he used (the monetary aggregate) reads long.

Finally he wrote a book called *Who Should Run the City* (*The Ethics of Liberty*), arguing there should be no city government at all. That book has nothing to do with the construction drawings — but many people who disagreed with it never looked at the drawings. **Don't make that mistake.**

## @misconceptions
- **“*MES* is just a popular simplification of *Human Action*.”** — It is a re-systematization that departs from Mises on monopoly price, interest and 100-percent reserves and drops Mises's entire epistemology. Read the two side by side; the differences are a map of the school's internal debates.
- **“Rothbard says there is no monopoly on a free market.”** — He says “monopoly price” cannot be defined on a free market because there is no observable “competitive price” to compare with; he grants that one person may own the whole supply of a resource. Meaningful monopoly comes only from state-granted exclusive privilege. Kirzner and others think this discards a useful analytical tool.
- **“*America's Great Depression* proves ABCT.”** — Rothbard's method is explicitly “explain, don't test”: the theory holds a priori, and history shows the particular facts fit its pattern. Accept or reject the method, but do not say the book “tests” anything — it does not claim to. [[empirical-method|Stage 13.1]] goes into detail.
- **“Rothbard's money-supply measure is the standard one.”** — It is extremely broad (life-insurance reserves, savings-and-loan shares and more), far wider than the later M2, and critics say it overstates the 1920s expansion. Inspect the appendix tables and judge for yourself.
- **“You cannot accept Rothbard's economics without accepting anarcho-capitalism.”** — *MES* is value-free in method; the ethics were deliberately placed in a separate book, *The Ethics of Liberty*. The two are logically independent, and many Austrian economists accept *MES* while rejecting the political philosophy. Read the economics with the politics in brackets.
