# Why Property Rights Exist: Scarcity, Conflict & First Use

## @hook
If everything in the world were inexhaustible, nobody would ever need the sentence “this is mine.” Two people cannot come to blows over a melody; they can very easily come to blows over a field. **Property is not a moralist's invention but an answer to the fact of scarcity: when two plans for the same thing collide, there has to be a rule — knowable in advance — that says whose plan yields.** This lesson derives property from scarcity, derives prices and economic calculation from property (back to [[mises-1920|Stage 7.1]]), and then uses the result to look at intellectual property, the question Austrians still argue about most fiercely among themselves.

## @intuition
Start with a question that sounds childish: why has nobody ever sued over the Pythagorean theorem? You use it, I use it, several billion people use it at the same moment, and there is not one bit less of it. Now ask: why do people sue — sometimes bleed — over a 200-square-metre building plot? Because if you put a house on it, I cannot grow vegetables on it. **Same plot, two plans, and the plans exclude each other.**

That is where the whole story begins. This course has been talking about “scarcity” since [[what-economics-studies|Stage 0.1]], but scarcity is usually described too abstractly. Its most concrete meaning is this: **a thing is scarce when A's use of it prevents, or degrades, B's use of it.** Economists have a word for that property: the good is **rivalrous**. A field, a chair, a fish, a domain name, a Bitcoin UTXO — all rivalrous. A song, a formula, a recipe, a block of code — none of them: if you sing it, I can still sing it, and the song is not diminished.

Whenever a thing is rivalrous and more than one person wants it, **the possibility of conflict exists.** Note the word possibility. Nobody is claiming people must fight; the claim is that when two plans collide, something has to decide who gives way. There are only three candidates: a fight (the stronger uses it), a fresh negotiation every single time (exhausting), or **a rule fixed in advance**, so that everyone knows the answer without either fighting or bargaining. **Property is that rule.**

Seen this way, property is first of all not a moral concept but an **engineering** one: it is the “traffic code” a society evolves to avoid collisions over rivalrous resources. There is nothing sacred about “stop on red, go on green”; its value is that everyone knows it beforehand, so no intersection needs a negotiation. Property is the same. The rule “first user owns it” is not obviously holier than “draw lots,” but it has one enormous practical advantage: **the parties themselves can apply it, with no referee handing things out.**

Only then does the story reach the part Austrians care about most. [[mises-1920|Stage 7.1]] presented Mises's 1920 argument: without private ownership of the means of production there is no market in them, hence no prices, hence no economic calculation. Turn it around: **property is not merely a rule against fighting; it is the foundation of the price system.** Only when a field or a machine can genuinely be sold by some particular person does a number like “what this field is worth” appear in the market; and only with that number can an entrepreneur work out whether turning the field into a factory site creates or destroys value. So when Austrians talk about property, they are never repeating the political slogan that “the rich have a right to keep what they have.” They are saying something starker: **without exchangeable property, a society is blind.**

The lesson also takes on a question on which Austrians have no consensus: **intellectual property.** If a song is not scarce, does it need “property” at all? Stephan Kinsella says no — property was designed for rivalrous resources, and stretching it over non-rivalrous “ideas” manufactures scarcity that was not there. Rothbard thought copyright could be achieved by contract. You will see that the two sides differ less about the conclusion than about the answer to “why does property exist in the first place?” — which is exactly what this lesson is about.

Finally, property is not carved once into stone. Harold Demsetz's famous 1967 paper tells a true story: the indigenous hunters of Labrador in Canada had no private hunting grounds before the fur trade; once beaver pelts became valuable, private territories appeared. **When a thing goes from “not worth fighting over” to “worth fighting over,” property gets invented.** The same logic runs to the present day: domain names, spectrum, Bitcoin, platform accounts — each is a story of new scarcity breeding new property, and [[zero-marginal-cost|Stages 15.3]], 16.4 and 17.4 each pick one up.

**In this lesson we break it into six pieces:**

- **① From scarcity to conflict: rivalry, excludability and the precondition for property**
- **② First use: Locke's “mixing labor” and Rothbard's “first user”**
- **③ Mises's utilitarian case: property as the infrastructure of social cooperation**
- **④ Hoppe's argumentation ethics: a contested “irrefutability”**
- **⑤ Property and economic calculation: nothing sellable, no prices**
- **⑥ The IP debate and how property evolves: Kinsella, Rothbard and Demsetz's furs**

## @mechanics
### ① From scarcity to conflict: rivalry, excludability and the precondition for property

The first step in understanding property is to sort resources along two axes:

- **Rivalry**: does one person's use reduce another's? A field, yes; a song, no.
- **Excludability**: can others be kept out at reasonable cost? A fenced field, yes; the air, hardly.

The key conclusion: **conflict can only arise over rivalrous resources.** Two people cannot obstruct each other by “both using the Pythagorean theorem.” So the argument “property exists to avoid conflict” naturally covers rivalrous goods only — a point that sounds bland and is in fact the entire powder keg of the intellectual-property fight.

Note too that rivalry is a property **of uses**, not of physics. A river is rivalrous as drinking water (the more I drink, the less you get) and non-rivalrous as scenery (my looking leaves you the whole view). The same object can sit in different cells of the grid for different uses. That is why the boundaries of property keep moving: **when some use goes from not worth contesting to worth contesting, property in that use gets drawn** (see ⑥).

### ② First use: Locke's “mixing labor” and Rothbard's “first user”

Once you accept “a rule is needed,” the next question is “which rule?” Logically there are many candidates: first user owns it; whoever needs it most owns it; draw lots; equal shares per head; assignment by an authority. The Rothbard–Hoppe wing of the Austrian school picks the first, and the reasons are worth examining closely.

John Locke gave the classic statement in Chapter V of the *Second Treatise of Government* (1689): the world begins unowned; a person owns his body and his labor; **by “mixing” his labor with an unowned thing he makes it his own** — clear a patch of wilderness and it is yours. Locke added his famous proviso: provided “enough, and as good” is left for others.

Rothbard, in *The Ethics of Liberty* (1982), distilled this into the **homesteading principle**: **an unowned scarce resource belongs to the first person who puts it to use (“transforms” it).** He dropped Locke's proviso (arguing it would make every title permanently uncertain) and stressed that the criterion is *first use*, not *amount of labor* — ten years of sweat on land that already has an owner does not make it yours.

Why first use rather than something else? Hoppe gives the clearest answer: **of all the candidate rules, only first use can be applied by the parties themselves without a referee, and without an infinite regress.** Look at the alternatives:

- “Whoever needs it most” — who judges need? A referee is required, and the referee's power over the resource needs its own justification.
- “Latecomers get a share too” — then the first user's incentive to develop anything vanishes, and every latecomer has a still-later comer behind him, so title never settles.
- “Divide the world's resources per head” — every birth forces a global recomputation of every title.

The virtue of first use is not that it is “fair.” It is that it is **clear, self-enforcing, and puts resources into use at the earliest moment.** In numbers: A clears 3 hectares of wilderness in 2020; B arrives in 2023. Under first use, B can clear the remaining wilderness or buy from A; nobody has to assess who is more “deserving.” This is also the baseline [[externalities-coase|Stage 9.2]] uses to decide who is imposing an externality on whom: **the prior user's use is the baseline, and it is the newcomer who changes the baseline who “invades.”**

### ③ Mises's utilitarian case: property as the infrastructure of social cooperation

Mises took an entirely different road. He does not invoke natural rights, and he denies that economics can prove any ethical proposition. His argument, repeated in *Socialism* (1922), *Liberalism* (1927) and *Human Action* (1949), has three steps:

1. People live well through **the division of labor and exchange** (the law of association from [[exchange-division|Stage 1.5]]): a hundred people each fending for themselves produce far less than a hundred who specialize and trade.
2. The division of labor requires that each person can **expect** to keep the fruits of his effort — otherwise nobody sows a crop that ripens next year, and nobody builds a factory that pays off in ten.
3. Therefore **private property is the precondition of social cooperation**; not a privilege of one class, but the institutional arrangement that makes everyone — including the poorest — better off than self-sufficiency would.

Mises's argument is utilitarian, but not Bentham's “greatest happiness of the greatest number” — he explicitly rejected interpersonal comparisons of utility ([[subjective-value|Stage 1.2]]). His “utility” means: **the ends almost everyone pursues (more material welfare, longer life) require private property as their means**; opponents of property usually do not have different ends, they have made a mistake about cause and effect. The job of economics, on his view, is to make that cause-and-effect chain clear.

The strength of this road is that it needs no metaphysical premise. Its weakness is just as plain: **if someone genuinely does not care about material welfare, or rates “equality” above “prosperity,” the utilitarian argument has no grip on him.** Rothbard's criticism of Mises on precisely this point is where the intra-Austrian split between natural law and utilitarianism begins; [[internal-debates|Stage 14.3]] takes it up in full.

### ④ Hoppe's argumentation ethics: a contested “irrefutability”

Hans-Hermann Hoppe, in *A Theory of Socialism and Capitalism* (1989), proposed a third road, an attempt to prove the private-property ethic “irrefutable” **in the same way Mises established the action axiom.** In outline:

- Every *justification* happens inside an *argument* — to convince me, you have to reason with me.
- Argument presupposes that each party has exclusive control over his own body (otherwise neither could speak), and presupposes prior appropriation of the resources argument needs (a place to stand, air to breathe).
- Therefore **anyone who denies first-use property while arguing has already presupposed it in the act of arguing** — a “performative contradiction,” just as denying the action axiom is itself an action.

Rothbard was delighted and called it a dazzling breakthrough for libertarian ethics. But **the argument is deeply contested among Austrians and libertarians themselves.** Several Austrian and libertarian scholars — including Robert Murphy and Gene Callahan in a 2006 critique in the *Journal of Libertarian Studies*, and David Friedman among others — raised objections of several kinds:

- Arguing presupposes only that you can use your voice and your spot **right now**; it does not yield **permanent, exclusive** ownership of bodies or land.
- Moving from “arguing presupposes X” to “X is ethically correct” is a leap of categories — a slave-owner can concede in argument that the slave can currently speak without conceding the slave's freedom.
- At most it shows the denier is *inconsistent*; it does not show the affirmer is *right*.

In this course we present it as **an ambitious argument that is still in dispute.** You do not need to accept it to accept property — ② and ③ each stand on their own. But it is worth knowing, because it shows the Rothbardian ambition to build ethics too as a deductive system, and the limits of that ambition.

### ⑤ Property and economic calculation: nothing sellable, no prices

Now connect property back to the spine of this course. [[mises-1920|Stage 7.1]] covered Mises's 1920 argument that socialism cannot allocate the means of production rationally. Most people remember the slogan “no prices, no calculation,” but Mises's own sentence has a step before that: **without private ownership of the means of production there is no market in them; without a market, no prices.** Property is the first premise of the syllogism.

Why must it be *private* property? Because a price is the outcome of an **exchange**, and exchange requires that each party truly owns something it can hand over. Picture a society in which the state owns everything: the steel plant's director “sells” steel to the car plant's director. That is not an exchange; it is one owner's left hand passing something to its right hand. However many ledger entries it produces, the numbers are not prices, because **nobody in the transaction can say “no.”** Prices carry knowledge ([[hayek-knowledge|Stage 7.2]]) precisely because they are the sediment of countless people who could each have said no.

A numerical example. A used lathe in a private-property society: A bids 80,000, B bids 90,000, C bids 120,000; the price settles somewhere between 90,000 and 120,000 (the marginal pairs of [[price-formation|Stage 1.3]]). Looking at “120,000,” an entrepreneur can calculate: buy it for parts manufacturing, revenue 150,000 a year, labor 50,000 — worth it or not? In a society without property the lathe is never *bid for*; it is *allocated* — and the allocator does not know to whom it is worth 120,000, because **nobody is staking his own money.**

So property has a double identity in the Austrian system: the **conflict-avoiding rule** of ①–②, and the **physical layer of the price system** of ⑤. Put the two together and you have the sense of Mises's remark that private ownership is not merely an economic matter but a matter of civilization. [[zero-marginal-cost|Stage 15.3]] uses this frame to price digital goods, and [[ai-central-planning|Stage 18.1]] asks whether an AI could “compute” prices without property — the answer is already buried here.

### ⑥ The IP debate and how property evolves: Kinsella, Rothbard and Demsetz's furs

With ①–⑤ in hand we can watch the most heated applied dispute inside the school.

**The anti-IP side**: Stephan Kinsella, *Against Intellectual Property* (2008; first published in the *Journal of Libertarian Studies* in 2001). The argument starts straight from ①: property exists because of conflict over rivalrous resources; **ideas are not rivalrous** — my learning a recipe leaves you knowing it no less; hence there is no conflict over ideas for property to resolve. What patents and copyrights actually do is **grant the inventor a partial ownership stake in genuinely scarce resources — your paper, your hard drive, your factory**: I may not use my own materials to build the machine in your patent. To Kinsella this is **artificial scarcity replacing natural scarcity**, an invasion of first-use property rather than an extension of it. He also cites empirical work (Boldrin and Levine's *Against Intellectual Monopoly*, 2008) finding thin evidence that patents promote innovation.

**The side that defends some IP**: Rothbard, in Chapter 10 of *Man, Economy, and State* (1962), distinguished patents from copyrights. He rejected patents (they forbid an independent inventor from using what he independently discovered — a state-granted monopoly) but held that **copyright can be achieved by contract**: I sell you a book with the condition “not to be copied”; copying is breach; and he argued the restriction could travel with the object to later buyers. Ayn Rand (not an Austrian) held that creation is the source of value and creators should own what they create. Mises in *Human Action* was cautious: he called patents a “privilege,” but noted that without them some research might go unfunded — he gave no verdict.

**The core of the dispute** is a different answer to “why does property exist?” If property is only a conflict-avoidance rule (①–②), Kinsella's conclusion follows almost automatically; if property is a reward for *creation* (another reading of Locke's labor-mixing), IP has a place. This course does not adjudicate, but it makes two observations. First, Kinsella's reply to Rothbard's “copyright by contract” has force — a contract binds only its signatories, not a third party who heard the song from someone else. Second, [[zero-marginal-cost|Stage 15.3]] will show that **digital goods get priced perfectly well without IP** (first-mover advantage, brand, service, convenience), so the premise “no IP, no incentive to create” deserves testing rather than assumption.

Finally, **how property evolves.** Harold Demsetz — Chicago school, not Austrian — in “Toward a Theory of Property Rights” (1967) told a case study: the Montagnais of the Labrador peninsula originally hunted in common, and nobody “owned” beaver; when the fur trade took off in the eighteenth century, beaver pelts went from nearly worthless to a major commodity, and the conflicts of over-hunting became **worth** resolving — so private hunting territories (family lands) emerged. Demsetz's proposition: **property rights get defined when the gains from defining them exceed the cost of doing so.** It is a mainstream contribution, but Austrians use it gladly, because it confirms the logic of ① and is a textbook spontaneous-order story ([[spontaneous-order|Stage 7.4]]) — nobody *designed* the Montagnais territory system; it grew.

The same story plays out every day now: **domain names** (nobody cared before the 1990s; now “first to register” plus an arbitration mechanism), **spectrum** ([[externalities-coase|Stage 9.2]]), **platform accounts and content** ([[platform-governance|Stage 16.4]] asks whether an account is the user's property, the platform's, or a contractual relationship), and **Bitcoin UTXOs** ([[defi-code-order|Stage 17.4]]: a rivalrous digital resource that excludes by cryptography rather than by courts — humanity's first top-left-cell good built inside a purely digital world). Once you have this lesson, all of those become variants of one question: **Is it scarce? Is the conflict worth resolving? Which rule can enforce itself?**

## @analogy
Think of property as **the painted lines in a car park.**

An empty lot with 3 cars in it needs no lines — park anywhere, no collisions. **Not scarce, no rule needed.** Push it to 300 cars and the trouble starts: your car parked at an angle takes a space and a half, and now I cannot get in. There are three ways out: the loudest voice parks (a fight); every arriving driver negotiates with the neighbors (bargaining, exhausting); or **paint lines** — and once the lines are down, nobody negotiates; one glance tells you whether a space is yours to take. **The lines are property.**

Where do the lines go? The simplest rule is **first come, first parked**: an empty bay is yours; an occupied one is not. No attendant is needed — every driver can apply the rule alone, which is the merit of homesteading. You can imagine other rules — “allocate by how expensive the car is,” “allocate by who is in more of a hurry” — but each one needs an attendant at the gate making a judgment, and what entitles the attendant to judge?

Then comes the most important step in this lesson: **once the lines are painted, bays can be rented.** The sentence “third floor, next to the lift, 800 a month” is possible only because that bay has a clear boundary and a clear owner. And only with the number 800 can a developer calculate whether digging one more underground level pays. That is the chain from property to prices to economic calculation. A lot with no lines has no rents, and so nobody knows whether to dig.

As for the IP quarrel, it is as if someone asked: “I park with a particularly elegant *technique* — if others copy my technique, are they taking my bay?” Kinsella would say: a technique takes up no space; however many people copy it, you can still park. There is no collision to draw a line around. Rothbard would say: I can sign a contract with my driving instructor forbidding him to teach it. The argument is not about whether the technique is elegant, but about **what the lines were painted for in the first place.**

## @misconceptions
- **“Property rights are an ideology for protecting the wealth of the rich.”** — The Austrian argument starts from scarcity and is indifferent to wealth: whenever a resource is rivalrous and more than one person wants it, a rule knowable in advance is needed, or the strong take everything — and in a world without rules, it is the weak who lose most. Mises's utilitarian case adds that the division of labor makes even the poorest far richer than self-sufficiency would, and the division of labor rests on property.
- **“First use means whoever grabs it keeps it.”** — Grabbing is the invasion of something already owned; homesteading is the first productive use of an *unowned* scarce resource. Rothbard's criterion is the first person to transform or put the thing to use, not the first to announce a claim — drawing a line on a map is not homesteading; clearing, building and continuous use are.
- **“Intellectual property is a natural extension of property; opposing IP is opposing property.”** — That is precisely what Austrians disagree about. Kinsella holds that ideas are non-rivalrous and need no property, and that IP actually restricts others' use of their own scarce physical goods; Rothbard defended copyright by contract and rejected patents. Both sides see themselves as defenders of property; the split is over the premise of why property exists.
- **“Hoppe's argumentation ethics has settled the case for private property once and for all.”** — It is an ambitious argument, but several Austrian and libertarian scholars have criticized it (momentary control during an argument does not yield permanent ownership; inconsistency is not the same as error). This course treats it as a contested contribution, not a verdict; the first-use and utilitarian arguments each stand independently of it.
- **“Property is created by the state; without a state there is no property.”** — Demsetz's fur case, the medieval law merchant ([[spontaneous-law|Stage 9.3]]), first-to-register domain names, and Bitcoin's cryptographic exclusion all show property growing without a legislator. A state may recognize, register and enforce property, or wreck it; but the logic of property comes from scarcity and conflict and precedes any statute.
